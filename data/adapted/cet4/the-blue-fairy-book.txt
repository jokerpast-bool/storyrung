CHAPTER 1. Part 1

My father had a small estate in Nottinghamshire. I was the third of four sons. He sent me to Cambridge when I was fourteen years old. After I studied there for three years, I became an apprentice to Mr. Bates. He was a famous surgeon in London. My father sometimes sent me small sums of money. I spent the money on learning navigation and other skills. These skills are useful to people who travel. I always believed that one day I would travel too.

Three years after I left him, my good master Mr. Bates recommended me as a ship's surgeon on the “Swallow.” I sailed on it for three years. When I came back, I settled in London. I rented part of a small house. I married Miss Mary Burton. She was the daughter of Mr. Edmund Burton, a hosier.

But my good master Bates died two years later. I had few friends, and my business began to fail. I decided to go to sea again. After several trips, I accepted an offer from Captain W. Pritchard. He was the master of the “Antelope,” and he was making a trip to the South Sea. We set sail from Bristol on May 4, 1699. At first, our trip was very successful.

But on our journey to the East Indies a violent storm drove us to the north-west of Van Diemen’s Land. Twelve of our crew died from hard work and bad food. The rest were very weak. On the 5th of November the weather was very misty. The sailors saw a rock within 120 yards of the ship. But the wind was so strong that we were driven straight onto it, and the ship broke apart at once. Six of the crew, and I was one of them, lowered the boat and got away from the ship. We rowed about three leagues until we could row no more. So we trusted ourselves to the waves. After about half an hour a sudden storm turned the boat over. What happened to my companions in the boat, or to those who escaped onto the rock or stayed in the ship, I cannot say. But I believe they were all lost. For my part, I swam as luck led me, and the wind and tide pushed me forward. When I could struggle no longer, I found I could touch the bottom. By this time the storm had died down a lot. At last I reached the shore at about eight in the evening. I walked nearly half a mile inland but could not find any sign of people. I was very tired, and because the weather was hot I felt very sleepy. I lay down on the grass, which was very short and soft, and slept more deeply than ever before in my life for about nine hours. When I woke, it was just daylight. I tried to get up but could not. I happened to be lying on my back, and I found my arms and legs were tied to the ground on each side. My hair, which was long and thick, was tied down in the same way. I could only look up. The sun began to grow hot, and the light hurt my eyes. I heard a confused noise around me but could see nothing except the sky. After a little time I felt something alive and moving on my left leg. It moved gently over my chest and came almost up to my chin. When I looked down, I saw it was a human creature, not six inches high, with a bow and arrow in his hands and a quiver on his back. At the same time I felt at least forty more following the first. I was very surprised, and I roared so loud that they all ran back in fear. Some of them were hurt when they fell as they jumped from my sides onto the ground. However, they soon returned. One of them was brave enough to get a full look at my face, and he lifted up his hands in wonder. I lay all this time in great worry. At last I struggled to get free, and I managed to break the strings that tied my left arm to the ground. At the same time, with a violent pull that gave me great pain, I loosened the strings that tied down my hair a little. Now I was just able to turn my head about two inches. But the creatures ran off a second time before I could seize them. Then there was a great shout, and in an instant I felt more than a hundred arrows shot into my left hand. They stung me like many needles. They also shot another round into the air, and some fell on my face. I covered my face at once with my left hand. When this shower of arrows was over, I groaned with grief and pain. Then I tried again to get free, and they shot another round of arrows, larger than the first. Some of them tried to stab me with their spears. But by good luck I was wearing a leather jacket, which they could not pierce. By this time I thought it was most wise to lie still until night. My left hand was already loose, so I could easily free myself then. As for the people, I thought I could fight the greatest army they could bring against me, if they were all the same size as the one I saw. When the people saw that I was quiet, they shot no more arrows. But by the noise I heard I knew their number had increased. About four yards from me, for more than an hour, there was a knocking, like people at work. Then I turned my head that way as well as the pegs and strings would let me. I saw a stage set up about a foot and a half from the ground, with two or three ladders to climb it. From this stage one of them, who seemed to be a person of quality, made me a long speech. I could not understand a word, though I could tell from his manner that he sometimes threatened me and sometimes spoke with pity and kindness. I answered in few words, but in the most humble manner. I was almost dying of hunger, so I could not help showing my impatience by putting my finger often to my mouth to show that I wanted food. He understood me very well. He came down from the stage and ordered several ladders to be set against my sides. More than a hundred of the people climbed up and walked toward my mouth with baskets full of food. The King had sent it by his orders when he first heard news of me. There were legs and shoulders like mutton but smaller than the wings of a lark. I ate them two or three at a mouthful and took three loaves at a time. They supplied me as fast as they could, with a thousand signs of wonder at my appetite. I then made a sign that I wanted something to drink. They guessed that a small quantity would not be enough for me. They were a very clever people, so they hung up one of their largest barrels, rolled it toward my hand, and beat out the top. I drank it all in one long drink, which I could well do, for it did not hold half a pint. They brought me a second barrel, which I drank, and made signs for more. But they had none to give me. However, I could not wonder enough at the daring of these tiny people. They dared to climb and walk on my body while one of my hands was free. They did not shake at the very sight of so huge a creature as I must have seemed to them. After some time a person of high rank from his Imperial Majesty appeared before me. His Excellency climbed onto my right leg and came to my face with about a dozen of his followers. He spoke for about ten minutes, often pointing forward. I later found that he was pointing toward the capital city, about half a mile away. His Majesty had ordered that I should be carried there. I made a sign with my free hand, putting it to the other hand, but over his Excellency’s head for fear of hurting him or his followers. I wanted to show that I wished for my freedom. He seemed to understand me well enough, for he shook his head. But he made other signs to let me know that I should have enough meat and drink and very good treatment. Then I once more thought of trying to escape. But I felt the sting of their arrows on my face and hands, which were all covered in blisters. I also saw that the number of my enemies had increased. So I gave signs to let them know that they might do with me what they pleased. Then they rubbed my face and hands with a sweet-smelling ointment, which in a few minutes took away all the sting of the arrows. The relief from pain and hunger made me sleepy, and soon I fell asleep. I slept about eight hours, as I was told afterward. This was no wonder, for the doctors, by the Emperor’s orders, had mixed a sleeping drink into the barrels of wine.

It seems that when I was found sleeping on the ground after I landed, the Emperor heard of it early. He decided that I should be tied in the way I have described. This was done at night while I slept. He also decided that plenty of meat and drink should be sent to me, and that a machine should be prepared to carry me to the capital city. Five hundred carpenters and engineers were set to work at once to prepare the engine. It was a frame of wood, raised three inches from the ground. It was about seven feet long and four wide, and it moved on twenty-two wheels. But the difficulty was to place me on it. Eighty poles were put up for this purpose. Very strong cords were tied to cloths that the workmen had tied round my neck, hands, body, and legs. Nine hundred of the strongest men were used to pull up these cords with pulleys fixed on the poles. In less than three hours I was raised and put into the engine, and tied down there. Then fifteen hundred of the Emperor's largest horses, each about four and a half inches high, were used to pull me toward the capital. But while all this was done I still lay in a deep sleep. I did not wake until four hours after we began our journey.

The Emperor and all his Court came out to meet us when we reached the capital. But his great officials would not let his Majesty risk his person by climbing on my body. Where the carriage stopped there stood an ancient temple. It was said to be the largest in the whole kingdom, and it was decided that I should stay there. Near the great gate, through which I could easily go, they fixed ninety-one chains, like those that hang from a lady's watch. These were locked to my left leg with thirty-six padlocks. When the workmen found it was impossible for me to break loose, they cut all the strings that tied me. Then I rose up, feeling as sad as ever I did in my life. But the noise and surprise of the people when they saw me rise and walk were too great for words. The chains that held my left leg were about two yards long. They gave me freedom not only to walk back and forth in a half circle, but also to go in and lie at full length inside the temple. The Emperor came toward me from among his courtiers, all dressed most splendidly. He looked at me with great wonder, but kept beyond the length of my chain. He was taller by about the width of my nail than any of his Court. That alone was enough to fill the viewers with awe, and he was graceful and majestic. To see him better, I lay down on my side, so that my face was level with his, and he stood three yards off. However, I have since had him many times in my hand, and so I cannot be fooled. His dress was very simple. But he wore a light helmet of gold, decorated with jewels and a plume. He held his sword drawn in his hand, to defend himself if I should break loose. It was almost three inches long, and the hilt was of gold, rich with diamonds. His voice was high, but very clear. His Imperial Majesty spoke often to me, and I answered. But neither of us could understand a word.

CHAPTER 2. Part 2

After about two hours the Court left. I was left with a strong guard to keep the crowd away. Some of the crowd had been rude enough to shoot their arrows at me as I sat by the door of my house. But the colonel ordered six of them to be caught and tied up and given to me. I put five of them into my coat pocket. As for the sixth, I made a face as if I would eat him alive. The poor man screamed very loudly. The colonel and his officers were very upset, especially when they saw me take out my small knife. But I soon made them feel easy. I cut the strings that tied him and put him gently on the ground, and he ran away. I treated the rest in the same way, taking them one by one out of my pocket. I saw that both the soldiers and the people were very happy at this sign of my kindness.

Toward night I got into my house with some difficulty. I lay on the ground there, as I had to do for two weeks, until a bed was made for me out of six hundred beds of the usual size.

Six hundred servants were given to me, and three hundred tailors made me a suit of clothes. Also, six of his Majesty's greatest scholars were told to teach me their language. Soon I was able to talk in a simple way with the Emperor, who often visited me. The first words I learned were to ask him to please give me my freedom. I repeated this every day on my knees. But he answered that this must take time. He said that first I must swear peace with him and his kingdom. He also told me that by the laws of the nation I must be searched by two of his officers. Since this could not be done without my help, he trusted them in my hands. Whatever they took from me should be returned when I left the country. I picked up the two officers and put them into my coat pockets. These gentlemen had pen, ink, and paper with them. They made an exact list of everything they saw. I later translated it into English, and it ran as follows:

"In the right coat pocket of the great Man-Mountain we found only one great piece of rough cloth, large enough to cover the carpet of your Majesty's chief room of state. In the left pocket we saw a huge silver chest, with a silver cover, which we could not lift. We asked that it should be opened. One of us stepped into it and found himself up to the middle of his leg in a sort of dust. Some of it flew into our faces and sent us both into a fit of sneezing. In his right waistcoat pocket we found a number of white thin substances, folded one over another, about the size of three men. They were tied with a strong rope and marked with black figures, which we humbly believe to be writings. In the left there was a sort of machine, from the back of which extended twenty long poles. With these, we believe, the Man-Mountain combs his head. In the smaller pocket on the right side were several round flat pieces of white and red metal, of different sizes. Some of the white pieces, which appeared to be silver, were so large and heavy that my comrade and I could hardly lift them. From another pocket hung a huge silver chain, with a wonderful kind of machine fastened to it. It had a globe half silver and half of some clear metal. For on the clear side we saw certain strange figures, and thought we could touch them until we found our fingers stopped by the shining substance. This machine made a constant noise, like a water-mill. We believe it is either some unknown animal, or the god he worships. But probably the latter, for he told us that he seldom did anything without consulting it.

"This is a list of what we found about the body of the Man-Mountain, who treated us with great civility."

I had one private pocket which escaped their search. It contained a pair of spectacles and a small spy-glass. Since these were of no importance to the Emperor, I did not think myself bound in honor to discover them.

CHAPTER 3. Part 3

My good behavior and gentle ways had won over the Emperor and his Court, and in fact the people in general. So I began to hope I might get my freedom soon. The people slowly became less afraid of danger from me. Sometimes I would lie down and let five or six of them dance on my hand. At last the boys and girls dared to come and play hide-and-seek in my hair.

The horses of the army and of the royal stables were no longer shy. They had been led before me every day. One of the Emperor's huntsmen, on a large horse, took my foot, shoe and all. It was indeed a huge jump. One day I amused the Emperor in a very unusual way. I took nine sticks and fixed them firmly in the ground in a square. Then I took four other sticks and tied them side by side at each corner, about two feet from the ground. I tied my cloth to the nine sticks that stood upright. I stretched it on all sides until it was as tight as the top of a drum. Then I asked the Emperor to let a troop of his best horse, twenty-four in number, come and exercise on this flat ground. His Majesty approved of the plan. I took them up one by one, with the proper officers to exercise them. As soon as they got into order they divided into two parties. They shot dull arrows, drew their swords, fled and pursued, and in short showed the best military discipline I ever saw. The sticks side by side kept them and their horses from falling off the stage. The Emperor was so pleased that he ordered this entertainment to be repeated several days. He persuaded the Empress herself to let me hold her in her chair within two yards of the stage. From there she could view the whole performance. Luckily no accident happened, only once a fiery horse, striking with his foot, made a hole in my cloth and threw down his rider and himself. But I immediately helped them both. I covered the hole with one hand and set down the troop with the other, as I had taken them up. The horse that fell was strained in the shoulder, but the rider was not hurt. I repaired my cloth as well as I could. However, I would not trust its strength any more in such dangerous attempts.

I had sent so many requests for my freedom that his Majesty at last mentioned the matter in a full council. No one opposed it except Skyresh Bolgolam, admiral of the realm. Without any cause he was pleased to be my deadly enemy. However, he agreed at last, though he managed to draw up the conditions himself on which I should be set free. After they were read I was asked to swear to perform them in the way their laws required. This was to hold my right foot in my left hand, and to place the middle finger of my right hand on the top of my head, and my thumb on the top of my right ear. But I have made a translation of the conditions, which I here offer to the public:

"Golbaste Mamarem Evlame Gurdile Shefin Mully Ully Gue, Most Mighty Emperor of Lilliput, joy and terror of the universe, whose lands extend to the ends of the world, king of all kings, taller than the sons of men, whose feet press down to the center, and whose head strikes against the sun, at whose nod the princes of the earth shake their knees, pleasant as the spring, comfortable as the summer, fruitful as autumn, dreadful as winter: His Most High Majesty proposes to the Man-Mountain, lately arrived at our heavenly lands, the following articles, which by a serious oath he shall be required to perform:

"First. The Man-Mountain shall not leave our lands without our permission under the great seal.

"Second. He shall not dare to come into our capital without our express order. At that time the inhabitants shall have two hours' warning to keep within doors.

"Third. The said Man-Mountain shall keep his walks to our main high roads, and not try to walk or lie down in a meadow or field of corn.

"Fourth. As he walks the said roads he shall take the greatest care not to step on the bodies of any of our loving subjects, their horses or carriages, nor take any of our subjects into his hands without their own consent.

"Fifth. If a special rider requires extraordinary speed the Man-Mountain shall be required to carry in his pocket the rider and horse a six days' journey, and return the said rider (if so required) safe to our imperial presence.

"Sixth. He shall be our ally against our enemies on the island of Blefuscu, and do his best to destroy their fleet, which is now preparing to invade us.

"Lastly. Upon his serious oath to observe all the above articles, the said Man-Mountain shall have a daily allowance of meat and drink sufficient for the support of 1,724 of our subjects, with free access to our royal person, and other marks of our favor. Given at our palace at Belfaburac, the twelfth day of the ninety-first moon of our reign."

I swore to these articles with great cheerfulness. Then my chains were immediately unlocked, and I was at full liberty.

One morning, about two weeks after I had obtained my freedom, Reldresal, the Emperor’s secretary for private affairs, came to my house. He was attended by only one servant. He ordered his coach to wait at a distance. He asked me to give him an hour of my time. I offered to lie down so that he could more easily reach my ear. But he chose to let me hold him in my hand during our conversation. He began with polite words about my liberty. But he added that, except for the present state of things at Court, perhaps I might not have obtained it so soon. “For,” he said, “however successful we may seem to foreigners, we are in danger of an invasion from the island of Blefuscu. That is the other great empire of the universe. It is almost as large and as powerful as this one of his Majesty. As for what we have heard you say, that there are other kingdoms in the world, filled with human creatures as large as yourself, our philosophers are very uncertain. They rather guess that you dropped from the moon, or one of the stars. A hundred people of your size would soon destroy all the fruit and cattle of his Majesty’s lands. Besides, our histories of six thousand moons mention no other regions than the two great empires of Lilliput and Blefuscu. As I was going to tell you, they are engaged in a very stubborn war. It began in the following manner. Everyone agrees that the original way of breaking eggs was upon the larger end. But his present Majesty’s grandfather, while he was a boy, was going to eat an egg. He broke it according to the ancient practice and happened to cut one of his fingers. Upon this, the Emperor, his father, made a law commanding all his subjects to break the smaller end of their eggs. The people so strongly disliked this law that there have been six rebellions raised on that account. In one, an emperor lost his life, and in another, an emperor lost his crown. It is calculated that eleven hundred persons have at different times suffered rather than break their eggs at the smaller end. But these rebels, the Bigendians, have found so much support at the Emperor of Blefuscu’s Court, where they always fled for safety, that a bloody war, as I said, has been carried on between the two empires for thirty-six moons. And now the Blefuscudians have equipped a large fleet and are preparing to come down upon us. Therefore his Imperial Majesty, placing great trust in your courage and strength, has commanded me to set the case before you.”

I asked the secretary to present my humble duty to the Emperor. I asked him to let the Emperor know that I was ready, at the risk of my life, to defend him against all invaders.

CHAPTER 4. Part 4

It was not long before I told his Majesty the plan I had made for taking the enemy's whole fleet. The Empire of Blefuscu is an island separated from Lilliput only by a channel eight hundred yards wide. I asked the most experienced seamen about the depth of the channel. They told me that in the middle, at high water, it was seventy glumguffs (about six feet of European measure). I walked toward the coast. There I lay down behind a small hill and took out my small telescope. I looked at the enemy's fleet at rest in the harbor--about fifty warships and other vessels. I then came back to my house and gave orders for a great quantity of the strongest ropes and iron bars. The rope was about as thick as thin string, and the bars were the length and size of a knitting needle. I twisted the rope three times to make it stronger. For the same reason I twisted three of the iron bars together and bent the ends into a hook. I fixed fifty hooks to fifty ropes. Then I went back to the coast. I took off my coat, shoes, and stockings and walked into the sea in my leather jacket about half an hour before high water. I moved through the water as quickly as I could, swimming in the middle about thirty yards, until I felt the ground. In this way I reached the fleet in less than half an hour. The enemy was so afraid when they saw me that they jumped out of their ships and swam to the shore. There could not have been fewer than thirty thousand of them. Then I fixed a hook to the hole at the front of each ship and tied all the ropes together at the end. Meanwhile the enemy shot several thousand arrows, and many of them stuck in my hands and face. My greatest fear was for my eyes. I would have lost them if I had not suddenly thought of the pair of glasses that had escaped the Emperor's searchers. I took them out and put them on my nose. Protected in this way, I went on with my work despite the arrows. Many of them struck the glass of my glasses, but the only effect was to move them slightly. Then I took the tied end in my hand and began to pull. But not a ship would move, because they were held too firmly by their anchors. So the boldest part of my plan remained. I let go of the rope and cut with my knife the ropes that held the anchors. I received more than two hundred shots in my face and hands. Then I took up again the tied end of the ropes to which my hooks were tied, and with great ease I pulled fifty of the enemy's largest warships after me.

When the Blefuscudians saw the fleet moving in order, and me pulling at the end, they gave a scream of grief and despair that it is impossible to describe. When I had got out of danger I stopped for a short time to pick out the arrows that stuck in my hands and face. I rubbed on some of the same ointment that was given to me when I arrived. I then took off my glasses, and after waiting about an hour, until the tide had fallen a little, I walked on to the royal port of Lilliput.

The Emperor and his whole Court stood on the shore waiting for me. They saw the ships move forward in a large half-moon, but could not see me. In the middle of the channel I was under water up to my neck. The Emperor decided that I had drowned, and that the enemy's fleet was coming in a hostile manner. But he was soon made easy, because the channel grew shallower with every step I made. In a short time I came close enough to hear, and holding up the end of the rope by which the fleet was tied, I cried in a loud voice: "Long live the most powerful Emperor of Lilliput!" The Prince received me at my landing with all possible joy, and made me a Nardal on the spot, which is the highest title of honor among them.

His Majesty wished that I would take some opportunity to bring all the rest of his enemy's ships into his ports. He seemed to think of nothing less than defeating the whole Empire of Blefuscu and becoming the only ruler of the world. But I plainly said that I would never be the means of bringing a free and brave people into slavery. The wisest of the Ministers were of my opinion, but my open refusal was so opposed to his Majesty's ambition that he could never forgive me. And from this time a plot began between himself and those of his Ministers who were my enemies, which nearly ended in my complete destruction.

About three weeks after this great act there arrived a group of ambassadors from Blefuscu, with humble offers of peace. Peace was soon made, on terms very advantageous to our Emperor. There were six ambassadors, with a train of about five hundred persons, all very grand. They had been told in private that I had helped them, so they made me a visit. They paid me many compliments on my courage and generosity and invited me to their kingdom in the name of the Emperor their master. I asked them to present my most humble respects to the Emperor their master. I decided to visit his royal person before I returned to my own country. So the next time I had the honor to see our Emperor, I asked his general permission to visit the Blefuscudian monarch. He granted it to me, but in a very cold manner, and I later learned the reason.

When I was just preparing to pay my respects to the Emperor of Blefuscu, a distinguished person at Court, to whom I had once done a great service, came to my house very secretly at night. Without sending his name, he asked to be admitted. I put his lordship into my coat pocket and gave orders to a trusted servant to admit no one. I locked the door, placed my visitor on the table, and sat down by it. His lordship's face was full of trouble. He asked me to hear him with patience in a matter that highly concerned my honor and my life.

"You are aware," he said, "that Skyresh Bolgolam has been your mortal enemy ever since your arrival. His hatred has increased since your great success against Blefuscu, by which his glory as admiral is obscured. This lord and others have accused you of treason, and several councils have been called in the most private manner on your account. Out of gratitude for your favors I obtained information of the whole proceedings, risking my head for your service, and this was the charge against you:

“First, you brought the imperial fleet of Blefuscu into the royal port. Then his Majesty commanded you to seize all the other ships. He also ordered you to put to death all the Bigendian exiles, and all the people of the empire who would not immediately agree to break their eggs at the smaller end. And you, like a false traitor to his Most Serene Majesty, excused yourself from this service. You said you were unwilling to force people's consciences and destroy the liberties and lives of an innocent people.

“Again, when ambassadors arrived from the Court of Blefuscu, you helped them and entertained them, like a false traitor. You knew they were servants of a prince who had lately been in open war against his Imperial Majesty.

“Moreover, you are now preparing to voyage to the Court of Blefuscu. This is contrary to the duty of a faithful subject.

“In the debate on this charge,” my friend continued, “his Majesty often urged the services you had done him. But the admiral and treasurer insisted that you should be put to a shameful death. But Reldresal, secretary for private affairs, who has always proved himself your friend, made a suggestion. He said that if his Majesty would please to spare your life and only give orders to put out both your eyes, justice might in some measure be satisfied. At this Bolgolam rose up in fury. He wondered how the secretary dared desire to preserve the life of a traitor. And the treasurer, pointing out the expense of keeping you, also urged your death. But his Majesty was graciously pleased to say that since the council thought the loss of your eyes too easy a punishment, some other might afterward be inflicted. And the secretary, humbly desiring to be heard again, said this about expense: your allowance might be gradually lessened. Then, for want of sufficient food, you should grow weak and faint, and die in a few months. When that happened, his Majesty’s subjects might cut your flesh from your bones and bury it. They would leave the skeleton for the admiration of posterity.

“Thus, through the great friendship of the secretary the affair was arranged. It was commanded that the plan of starving you by degrees should be kept a secret. But the sentence of putting out your eyes was entered on the books. In three days your friend the secretary will come to your house and read the accusation before you. He will point out the great mercy of his Majesty, that only condemns you to the loss of your eyes. He does not doubt that you will submit to this humbly and gratefully. Twenty of his Majesty’s surgeons will attend, to see the operation well performed. They will do this by discharging very sharp-pointed arrows into the balls of your eyes as you lie on the ground.

“I leave you,” said my friend, “to consider what measures you will take. And, to escape suspicion, I must immediately return, as secretly as I came.”

His lordship did so. And I remained alone, in great perplexity. At first I was bent on resistance. For while I had liberty I could easily with stones pelt the metropolis to pieces. But I soon rejected that idea with horror. I remembered the oath I had made to the Emperor, and the favors I had received from him. At last, I had his Majesty’s leave to pay my respects to the Emperor of Blefuscu. So I resolved to take this opportunity. Before the three days had passed I wrote a letter to my friend the secretary telling him of my resolution. And, without waiting for an answer, I went to the coast. I entered the channel, and between wading and swimming reached the port of Blefuscu. The people there had long expected me, and they led me to the capital.

His Majesty, with the royal family and great officers of the Court, came out to receive me. And they entertained me in a manner suited to the generosity of so great a prince. I did not, however, mention my disgrace with the Emperor of Lilliput. I did not suppose that prince would disclose the secret while I was out of his power. But in this, it soon appeared, I was deceived.

CHAPTER 5. Part 5

Three days after I arrived, I walked out to the northeast coast of the island to see what I could find. I saw something far out in the sea that looked like a boat turned over. I took off my shoes and stockings. I walked two or three hundred yards through the water. I clearly saw it was a real boat. I thought a storm might have driven it from a ship. I went back to the city at once for help. After a huge amount of work, I got my boat to the royal port of Blefuscu. A great crowd of people came and were full of wonder at such a huge vessel. I told the Emperor that good fortune had brought this boat to me. It could carry me to some place where I might return to my native country. I begged him for orders for materials to fit it up, and for leave to depart. After many kind speeches, he was pleased to grant this.

Meanwhile the Emperor of Lilliput was uneasy at my long absence. But he never imagined that I had the least notice of his plans. He sent a person of rank to inform the Emperor of Blefuscu of my disgrace. This messenger had orders to show the great mercy of his master. His master was content to punish me with the loss of my eyes. He expected that his brother of Blefuscu would send me back to Lilliput, bound hand and foot, to be punished as a traitor. The Emperor of Blefuscu answered with many civil excuses. He said that as for sending me bound, his brother knew it was impossible. Also, though I had taken away his fleet, he was grateful to me for many good offices I had done him in making the peace. But both their Majesties would soon be made easy. I had found a huge vessel on the shore, able to carry me on the sea. He had given orders to fit it up. He hoped in a few weeks both empires would be free from me.

With this answer the messenger returned to Lilliput. The monarch of Blefuscu secretly offered me his gracious protection if I would continue in his service. But I hastened my departure. I resolved never more to put confidence in princes.

In about a month I was ready to take leave. The Emperor of Blefuscu, with the Empress and the royal family, came out of the palace. I lay down on my face to kiss their hands. They graciously gave me their hands. His Majesty presented me with fifty purses of sprugs (their greatest gold coin) and his picture at full length. I put it at once into one of my gloves to keep it from being hurt. Many other ceremonies took place at my departure.

I stored the boat with meat and drink. I took six cows and two bulls alive, with as many ewes and rams. I intended to carry them into my own country. To feed them on board, I had a good bundle of hay and a bag of corn. I would gladly have taken a dozen of the natives. But the Emperor would by no means permit this. Besides a diligent search into my pockets, his Majesty pledged my honor not to carry away any of his subjects, though with their own consent and desire.

Having this way prepared all things as well as I was able, I set sail. When I had made twenty-four leagues, by my reckoning, from the island of Blefuscu, I saw a sail steering to the northeast. I hailed her, but could get no answer. Yet I found I gained upon her, for the wind slackened. In half an hour she spied me, and discharged a gun. I came up with her between five and six in the evening, Sept. 26, 1701. But my heart leaped within me to see her English colors. I put my cows and sheep into my coat pockets, and got on board with all my little cargo. The captain received me with kindness. He asked me to tell him what place I came from last. But at my answer he thought I was raving. However, I took my black cattle and sheep out of my pocket. After great astonishment, this clearly convinced him.

We arrived in England on the 13th of April, 1702. I stayed two months with my wife and family. But my eager desire to see foreign countries would suffer me to remain no longer. However, while in England I made great profit by showing my cattle to persons of quality and others. Before I began my second voyage I sold them for 600_l_. I left 1500_l_. with my wife, and fixed her in a good house. Then I took leave of her and my boy and girl, with tears on both sides. I sailed on board the “Adventure.” (1)

THE PRINCESS ON THE GLASS HILL

Once upon a time there was a man who had a meadow which lay on the side of a mountain. In the meadow there was a barn in which he stored hay. But there had not been much hay in the barn for the last two years. Every St. John’s eve, when the grass was in the height of its vigor, it was all eaten clean up. It was just as if a whole flock of sheep had gnawed it down to the ground during the night. This happened once, and it happened twice. But then the man got tired of losing his crop. He said to his sons--he had three of them, and the third was called Cinderlad--that one of them must go and sleep in the barn on St. John’s night. It was absurd to let the grass be eaten up again, blade and stalk, as it had been the last two years. The one who went to watch must keep a sharp look-out, the man said.

The eldest was quite willing to go to the meadow. He would watch the grass, he said. He would do it so well that neither man, nor beast, nor even the devil himself should have any of it. So when evening came he went to the barn, and lay down to sleep. But when night was drawing near there was such a rumbling and such an earthquake that the walls and roof shook again. The lad jumped up and took to his heels as fast as he could. He never even looked back. The barn remained empty that year just as it had been for the last two.

On the next St. John’s eve the man again said that he could not go on in this way. He was losing all the grass in the far field year after year. One of his sons must just go there and watch it, and watch well too. So the next oldest son was willing to show what he could do. He went to the barn and lay down to sleep, as his brother had done. But when night was coming near there was a great deep sound, and then an earthquake. It was even worse than that on the former St. John’s night. When the youth heard it he was very afraid, and went off, running as if for a bet.

The year after, it was Cinderlad’s turn. But when he made ready to go the others laughed at him, and made fun of him. “Well, you are just the right one to watch the hay,” they said. “You have never learned anything but how to sit among the ashes and bake yourself!” Cinderlad, however, did not trouble himself about what they said. When evening came near he walked away to the far field. When he got there he went into the barn and lay down. But in about an hour the deep sound and the sharp noise began, and it was terrible to hear it. “Well, if it gets no worse than that, I can manage to stand it,” thought Cinderlad. In a little time the sharp noise began again, and the earth shook so that all the hay flew about the boy. “Oh! if it gets no worse than that I can manage to stand it,” thought Cinderlad. But then came a third deep sound, and a third earthquake. It was so violent that the boy thought the walls and roof had fallen down. But when that was over everything suddenly grew as still as death around him. “I am pretty sure that it will come again,” thought Cinderlad. But no, it did not. Everything was quiet, and everything stayed quiet. When he had lain still a short time he heard something that sounded as if a horse were standing eating just outside the barn door. He went softly to the door, which was slightly open, to see what was there. A horse was standing eating. It was so big, and fat, and fine a horse that Cinderlad had never seen one like it before. A seat for riding and a set of straps lay upon it, and a complete suit of metal clothing for a fighting man. Everything was of bright red metal, and so bright that it shone again. “Ha, ha! it is you who eat up our hay then,” thought the boy. “But I will stop that.” So he made haste, and took out his steel for striking fire, and threw it over the horse. Then it had no power to move from the spot, and became so gentle that the boy could do what he liked with it. So he mounted it and rode away to a place which no one knew of but himself, and there he tied it up. When he went home again his brothers laughed and asked how he had got on.

“You did not lie long in the barn, if even you have been so far as the field!” said they.

“I lay in the barn until the sun rose, but I saw nothing and heard nothing, not I,” said the boy. “God knows what there was to make you two so afraid.”

“Well, we shall soon see whether you have watched the field or not,” answered the brothers. But when they got there the grass was all standing just as long and as thick as it had been the night before.

The next St. John’s eve it was the same thing, once again. Neither of the two brothers dared to go to the far field to watch the crop, but Cinderlad went. Everything happened exactly the same as on the previous St. John’s eve. First there was a deep sound and an earthquake, and then there was another, and then a third. But all three earthquakes were much, very much more violent than they had been the year before. Then everything became still as death again. The boy heard something eating outside the barn door. So he went as softly as he could to the door, which was slightly open. Again there was a horse standing close by the wall of the house, eating and chewing. It was far larger and fatter than the first horse. It had a seat for riding on its back, and a set of straps was on it too, and a full suit of metal clothing for a fighting man, all of bright silver, and as beautiful as anyone could wish to see. “Ho, ho!” thought the boy. “Is it you who eat up our hay in the night? But I will put a stop to that.” So he took out his steel for striking fire, and threw it over the horse’s long hair. The beast stood there as quiet as a young sheep. Then the boy rode this horse, too, away to the place where he kept the other, and then went home again.

“I suppose you will tell us that you have watched well again this time,” said the brothers.

“Well, so I have,” said Cinderlad. So they went there again, and there the grass was, standing as high and as thick as it had been before. But that did not make them any kinder to Cinderlad.

When the third St. John’s night came, neither of the two older brothers dared to lie in the far barn to watch the grass. They had been so badly scared the night they slept there that they could not forget it. But Cinderlad dared to go, and everything happened just the same as on the two nights before. There were three earthquakes, each worse than the one before. The last one threw the boy from one wall of the barn to the other. Then everything suddenly became as still as death. After he had lain quietly for a short time, he heard something eating outside the barn door. Then he once more went quietly to the door, which was slightly open. There was a horse standing just outside it. It was much larger and fatter than the two others he had caught. “Ho, ho! So it is you who are eating up our hay this time,” thought the boy. “But I will put a stop to that.” So he pulled out his steel for striking fire and threw it over the horse. It stood as still as if it had been nailed to the field, and the boy could do just what he liked with it. Then he got on it and rode away to the place where he had the two others. Then he went home again. Then the two brothers laughed at him just as they had done before. They told him that they could see he must have watched the grass very carefully that night, for he looked just as if he were walking in his sleep. But Cinderlad did not worry about that. He just told them to go to the field and see. They did go, and this time too the grass was standing, looking as fine and as thick as ever.

The King of the country in which Cinderlad’s father lived had a daughter. He would give her to no one who could not ride up to the top of the glass hill. There was a high, high hill of glass, as smooth as ice, and it was close to the King’s house. Upon the very top of this hill the King’s daughter was to sit with three gold apples in her lap. The man who could ride up and take the three golden apples would marry her and have half the kingdom. The King had this announced in every church in the whole kingdom, and in many other kingdoms too. The Princess was very beautiful, and all who saw her fell deeply in love with her, even against their own will. So there is no need to say that all the princes and knights were eager to win her, and half the kingdom as well. For this reason they came riding there from the very end of the world. They were dressed so finely that their clothes shone in the sunlight. They rode on horses that seemed to dance as they went. There was not one of these princes who did not think that he was sure to win the Princess.

When the day set by the King had come, there was such a crowd of knights and princes under the glass hill that they seemed to move in great numbers. Everyone who could walk or even move slowly was there too, to see who won the King’s daughter. Cinderlad’s two brothers were there too. But they would not agree to let him go with them. He was so dirty and black from sleeping and digging among the ashes that they said everyone would laugh at them if they were seen with such a fool.

“Well, then, I will go all alone by myself,” said Cinderlad.

When the two brothers got to the glass hill, all the princes and knights were trying to ride up it, and their horses were covered in white foam. But it was all useless. As soon as the horses set foot on the hill, down they slipped. There was not one that could get even a couple of yards up. That was not strange, for the hill was as smooth as a glass window and as steep as the side of a house. But they were all eager to win the King’s daughter and half the kingdom. So they rode and they slipped, and so it went on. At last all the horses were so tired that they could do no more. They were so hot that foam dropped from them, and the riders were forced to give up the attempt. The King was just thinking that he would have it announced that the riding should begin again on the following day, when perhaps it might go better. Then suddenly a knight came riding up on so fine a horse that no one had ever seen the like of it before. The knight had armor of copper, and his bridle was of copper too. All his equipment was so bright that it shone. The other knights all called out to him that he might just as well save himself the trouble of trying to ride up the glass hill, for it was no use to try. But he did not listen to them. He rode straight to it and went up as if it were nothing at all. So he rode for a long way—it may have been a third part of the way up. But when he had got so far, he turned his horse around and rode down again. But the Princess thought that she had never yet seen so handsome a knight. While he was riding up, she was sitting and thinking, “Oh! How I hope he may be able to come up to the top!” And when she saw that he was turning his horse back, she threw one of the golden apples down after him, and it rolled into his shoe. But when he had come down from the hill, he rode away, and so fast that no one knew what had become of him.

So all the princes and knights were told to come before the King that night. Then the one who had ridden so far up the glass hill might show the golden apple which the King’s daughter had thrown down. But no one had anything to show. One knight came forward after the other, and none could show the apple.

At night, Cinderlad’s brothers came home again. They had a long story to tell about riding up the glass hill. At first, they said, no one could get even one step up. But then came a knight. He had armor of copper and a bridle of copper. His armor and coverings were so bright that they shone from far away. It was quite a sight to see him ride. He rode one-third of the way up the glass hill. He could easily have ridden the whole way if he had wanted. But he turned back. He had decided that was enough for one time. “Oh! I would have liked to see him too, I really would,” said Cinderlad. He was sitting by the chimney among the ashes, as usual. “You, indeed!” said the brothers. “You look as if you were fit to be among such great men! You are a dirty animal to sit there!”

The next day the brothers wanted to set out again. This time too Cinderlad begged them to let him go with them and see who rode. But no, they said he was not fit to do that. He was much too ugly and dirty. “Well, well, then I will go all alone by myself,” said Cinderlad. So the brothers went to the glass hill. All the princes and knights began to ride again. This time they had made the horses’ shoes rough on purpose. But that did not help them. They rode and they slipped as they had done the day before. Not one of them could get even a yard up the hill. When they had tired out their horses so that they could do no more, they had to stop altogether again. The King was thinking it would be good to announce that the riding would take place next day for the last time. That way they might have one more chance. But suddenly he remembered something. It would be good to wait a little longer to see if the knight in copper armor would come on this day too. But nothing was to be seen of him. Just as they were still looking for him, a knight came riding on a horse that was much, much finer than the one the knight in copper armor had ridden. This knight had silver armor and a silver saddle and bridle. All were so bright that they shone and sparkled when he was a long way off. Again the other knights called to him. They said he might just as well give up trying to ride up the glass hill. It was no use to try. But the knight paid no attention to that. He rode straight away to the glass hill. He went still farther up than the knight in copper armor had gone. But when he had ridden two-thirds of the way up, he turned his horse around and rode down again. The Princess liked this knight still better than she had liked the other. She sat wishing that he might be able to get up above. When she saw him turning back, she threw the second apple after him. It rolled into his shoe. As soon as he had got down the glass hill, he rode away so fast that no one could see what had become of him.

THE STORY OF PRINCE AHMED AND THE FAIRY PARIBANOU

There was a sultan, who had three sons and a niece. The eldest of the Princes was called Houssain, the second Ali, the youngest Ahmed, and the Princess, his niece, Nouronnihar.

The Princess Nouronnihar was the daughter of the younger brother of the Sultan. He died, and left the Princess very young. The Sultan took upon himself the care of his daughter’s education. He brought her up in his palace with the three Princes. He proposed to marry her when she arrived at a proper age. He also proposed to make an alliance with some neighboring prince by that means. But he perceived that the three Princes, his sons, loved her passionately. He thought more seriously on that affair. He was very much concerned. The difficulty he foresaw was to make them agree. The two youngest should consent to yield her up to their elder brother. He found them positively obstinate. He sent for them all together, and said to them: “Children, since for your good and quiet I have not been able to persuade you no longer to aspire to the Princess, your cousin, I think it would not be amiss if every one traveled separately into different countries. That way you might not meet each other. And, as you know I am very curious, and delight in everything that’s singular, I promise my niece in marriage to him that shall bring me the most extraordinary rarity. For the purchase of the rarity you shall go in search after, and the expense of traveling, I will give you every one a sum of money.”

As the three Princes always did what the Sultan wanted, and each told himself that luck might be good to him, they all agreed to it. The Sultan paid them the money he had promised. That very day they gave orders to prepare for their travels. They said goodbye to the Sultan, so they could be ready to leave the next morning. They all left by the same gate of the city. Each was dressed like a merchant. Each had a trusted officer dressed like a slave with him. They were all well mounted and equipped. They traveled the first day together. They all stayed at a small hotel, where the road split into three different ways. At night, when they were eating supper together, they all agreed to travel for a year. They agreed to meet at that hotel. The first one to come would wait for the others. They had all said goodbye to the Sultan together, so they could all return together. The next morning at sunrise, they hugged and wished each other good luck. Then they got on their horses and took a different road each.

Prince Houssain, the oldest brother, arrived at Bisnagar, the capital of the kingdom of that name, and the home of its king. He went and stayed at a small hotel for foreign merchants. He learned that there were four main areas where merchants of all kinds sold their goods and kept shops. In the middle of them stood the castle, or rather the King’s palace. He went to one of these areas the next day.

Prince Houssain could not look at this area without wonder. It was large and divided into several streets. They all had arched roofs and were shaded from the sun, and yet very light too. The shops were all the same size. All who sold the same kind of goods lived in one street. The craftsmen also kept their shops in the smaller streets.

There were so many shops, full of all kinds of goods. There were the finest linens from several parts of India, some painted in the brightest colors, showing animals, trees, and flowers. There were silks and fine cloths from Persia, China, and other places. There was porcelain from both Japan and China, and there were wall hangings. He was so surprised that he could not believe his own eyes. But when he came to the goldsmiths and jewelers, he was in a kind of great joy to see such huge amounts of worked gold and silver. He was amazed by the shine of the pearls, diamonds, rubies, emeralds, and other precious stones for sale.

Another thing Prince Houssain especially admired was the great number of flower sellers who filled the streets. The Indians love that flower so much that no one will move without a small bunch in his hand or a ring of flowers on his head. The merchants keep them in pots in their shops, so the air is full of sweet smell.

Prince Houssain went through that area, street by street. His thoughts were full of the riches he had seen. He was very tired. A merchant saw this and politely invited him to sit down in his shop. He accepted. But he had not sat down long before he saw a man who called out public notices pass by. The man had a piece of wall hanging on his arm, about six feet square. He called out a price of thirty purses. The Prince called to the man and asked to see the wall hanging. It seemed to him to be valued at a very high price, not only for its size, but for the poor quality of the material. When he had looked at it well, he told the man that he could not understand how so small a piece of wall hanging, and of such ordinary appearance, could be set at so high a price.

The man, who took him for a merchant, replied: “If this price seems so wild to you, your surprise will be greater when I tell you I have orders to raise it to forty purses, and not to sell it for less.” “Certainly,” answered Prince Houssain, “it must have something very unusual in it, which I know nothing of.” “You have guessed it, sir,” replied the man, “and you will agree when you come to know that whoever sits on this piece of wall hanging may be carried in an instant wherever he wishes to be, without being stopped by any obstacle.”

At these words of the man, the Prince of the Indies thought that the main reason for his travel was to carry home to the Sultan, his father, some rare and special thing. He thought that he could not meet with any thing that could give him more satisfaction. “If the wall hanging,” said he to the man, “has the power you say it has, I shall not think forty purses too much, but shall make you a present besides.” “Sir,” replied the man, “I have told you the truth. And it is an easy matter to prove it to you, as soon as you have made the deal for forty purses, on condition I show you the test. But I suppose you have not so much with you. To receive the money I must go with you to your small hotel, where you stay. With the leave of the master of the shop, we will go into the back shop, and I will spread out the wall hanging. When we have both sat down, and you have formed the wish to be carried into your room at the hotel, if we are not carried there it shall be no deal, and you shall be free. As to your present, though I am paid for my trouble by the seller, I shall receive it as a kindness, and be very much obliged to you, and thankful.”

Trusting the man, the Prince accepted the conditions and concluded the deal. He got the master’s leave, and they went into his back shop. They both sat down on it. As soon as the Prince formed his wish to be carried into his room at the hotel, he at once found himself and the man there. He did not need a better proof of the power of the wall hanging. He counted out forty pieces of gold to the man, and gave him twenty pieces for himself.

In this way Prince Houssain became the owner of the tapestry. He was very happy that he had found such a rare piece when he arrived at Bisnagar. He never doubted it would win him the hand of Nouronnihar. In short, he thought it was impossible for his younger brothers to find anything that could compare with it. He could sit on his tapestry and be at the meeting place that very day. But he had to stay there for his brothers, as they had agreed. He also wanted to see the King of Bisnagar and his Court. He wanted to learn about the strength, laws, customs, and religion of the kingdom. So he chose to stay there longer and spend some months satisfying his interest.

Prince Houssain could have stayed longer in the kingdom and Court of Bisnagar. But he was so eager to be nearer the Princess that he spread the tapestry. He and the officer he had brought with him sat down. As soon as he had formed his wish, they were carried to the inn where he and his brothers were to meet. There he passed for a merchant until they came.

Prince Ali, Prince Houssain's second brother, planned to travel into Persia. He took the road. Three days after he parted with his brothers, he joined a group of traders. After four days' travel he arrived at Schiraz, which was the capital of the kingdom of Persia. Here he passed for a jeweler.

The next morning Prince Ali, who traveled only for his pleasure and had brought nothing but just necessaries with him, dressed himself. Then he took a walk into that part of the town which they at Schiraz called the bezestein.

Many criers passed backward and forward with several sorts of goods, offering to sell them. Among them he was not a little surprised to see one who held an ivory telescope in his hand. It was about a foot in length and the thickness of a man's thumb. The crier cried it at thirty purses. At first he thought the crier was mad. To inform himself, he went to a shop and said to the merchant, who stood at the door: "Pray, sir, is not that man" (pointing to the crier who cried the ivory perspective glass at thirty purses) "mad? If he is not, I am very much deceived."

"Indeed, sir," answered the merchant, "he was in his right senses yesterday. I can assure you he is one of the ablest criers we have. He is the most employed of any when anything valuable is to be sold. And if he cries the ivory perspective glass at thirty purses, it must be worth as much or more, on some account or other. He will come by presently, and we will call him, and you shall be satisfied. In the meantime sit down on my sofa, and rest yourself."

Prince Ali accepted the merchant's kind offer. Presently afterward the crier passed by. The merchant called him by his name. Pointing to the Prince, he said to him: "Tell that gentleman, who asked me if you were in your right senses, what you mean by crying that ivory perspective glass at thirty purses. It seems not to be worth much. I should be very much amazed myself if I did not know you." The crier spoke to Prince Ali and said: "Sir, you are not the only person that takes me for a madman on account of this perspective glass. You shall judge yourself whether I am or no, when I have told you its property. I hope you will value it at as high a price as those I have showed it to already. They had as bad an opinion of me as you.

"First, sir," continued the crier, presenting the ivory pipe to the Prince, "observe that this pipe is furnished with a glass at both ends. Consider that by looking through one of them you see whatever object you wish to behold." "I am," said the Prince, "ready to make you all imaginable reparation for the scandal I have thrown on you if you will make the truth of what you say appear." He had the ivory pipe in his hand. After he had looked at the two glasses he said: "Show me at which of these ends I must look that I may be satisfied." The crier presently showed him. He looked through, wishing at the same time to see the Sultan his father. He immediately beheld him in perfect health, set on his throne, in the midst of his council. Afterward, as there was nothing in the world so dear to him, after the Sultan, as the Princess Nouronnihar, he wished to see her. He saw her at her toilet laughing, and in a pleasant humor, with her women about her.

Prince Ali wanted no other proof to be persuaded that this perspective glass was the most valuable thing in the world. He believed that if he should neglect to purchase it he should never meet again with such another rarity. He therefore took the crier with him to the khan where he lodged. He counted him out the money and received the perspective glass.

Prince Ali was overjoyed at his bargain. He persuaded himself that his brothers would not be able to meet with anything so rare and admirable. He believed the Princess Nouronnihar would be the recompense of his fatigue and trouble. He thought of nothing but visiting the Court of Persia incognito and seeing whatever was curious in Schiraz and thereabouts. He waited till the caravan with which he came returned back to the Indies. As soon as the caravan was ready to set out, the Prince joined them. He arrived happily without any accident or trouble, otherwise than the length of the journey and fatigue of traveling, at the place of rendezvous. There he found Prince Houssain, and both waited for Prince Ahmed.

Prince Ahmed took the road to Samarcand. The next day after he arrived there, he went into the bezestein, as his brothers had done. He had not walked long when he heard a crier. The crier had an artificial apple in his hand. He cried it at five and thirty purses. Prince Ahmed stopped the crier and said to him: "Let me see that apple. Tell me what power and special qualities it has, to be valued at so high a price." "Sir," said the crier, giving it into his hand, "if you look at the outside of this apple, it is very worthless. But if you consider its qualities, its powers, and the great use and benefit it is to mankind, you will say it is no price for it. You will say that he who possesses it is master of a great treasure. In short, it cures all sick persons of the most deadly diseases. And if the patient is dying, it will recover him immediately and restore him to perfect health. And this is done in the easiest manner in the world, which is by the patient's smelling the apple."

"If I may believe you," replied Prince Ahmed, "the powers of this apple are wonderful, and it is invaluable. But what ground have I, for all you tell me, to be persuaded of the truth of this matter?" "Sir," replied the crier, "the thing is known and averred by the whole city of Samarcand. But, without going any further, ask all these merchants you see here, and hear what they say. You will find several of them will tell you they had not been alive this day if they had not made use of this excellent remedy. And, that you may better comprehend what it is, I must tell you it is the fruit of the study and experiments of a celebrated philosopher of this city. He applied himself all his lifetime to the study and knowledge of the powers of plants and minerals. And at last he attained to this composition, by which he performed such surprising cures in this town as will never be forgot. But he died suddenly himself, before he could apply his sovereign remedy. And he left his wife and a great many young children behind him, in very indifferent circumstances. She, to support her family and provide for her children, is resolved to sell it."

While the crier told Prince Ahmed about the good powers of the artificial apple, many people came around them and said the same thing. One of them said he had a friend who was very sick and near death. He said this was a good chance to show Prince Ahmed the test. Then Prince Ahmed told the crier he would give him forty purses if he cured the sick person.

The crier, who had orders to sell it at that price, said to Prince Ahmed: "Come, sir, let us go and do the test, and the apple will be yours. I can tell you it will always have the result you want." In short, the test worked. The Prince counted out forty purses to the crier, and the crier gave him the apple. Then he waited without complaint for the first caravan that would return to the Indies. He arrived in perfect health at the inn where Princes Houssain and Ali waited for him.

When the Princes met, they showed each other their treasures. Right away they saw through the glass that the Princess was dying. They sat down on the carpet, wished themselves with her, and were there in a moment.

Prince Ahmed saw himself in Nouronnihar's room. He rose off the tapestry, and the other two Princes did the same. They went to the bed and put the apple under her nose. Some moments later the Princess opened her eyes. She turned her head from one side to another and looked at the people who stood around her. Then she sat up in bed and asked to be dressed, just as if she had woken from a deep sleep. Her women quickly told her, in a way that showed their joy, that she owed the three Princes thanks for the sudden return of her health, and especially Prince Ahmed. She right away showed her joy at seeing them. She thanked them all together, and afterward Prince Ahmed in particular.

While the Princess was dressing, the Princes went to throw themselves at the feet of the Sultan their father and show him respect. But when they came before him, they found he had been told of their arrival by the chief of the Princess's eunuchs. He had also been told how the Princess had been fully cured. The Sultan received and embraced them with the greatest joy, both for their return and for the recovery of the Princess his niece. He loved her as much as if she had been his own daughter, and the physicians had given up on her. After the usual ceremonies and polite words, the Princes each presented his rare gift. Prince Houssain presented his tapestry, which he had taken care not to leave behind in the Princess's room. Prince Ali presented his ivory seeing glass, and Prince Ahmed presented his artificial apple. After each had praised his present, when they put it into the Sultan's hands, they begged him to say their fate. They asked him to declare which of them he would give the Princess Nouronnihar to as a wife, according to his promise.

The Sultan of the Indies heard all that the Princes could say further about their rare gifts, without interrupting them. He was also well informed about what had happened with the cure of the Princess Nouronnihar. He stayed silent for some time, as if he were thinking about what answer he should make. At last he broke the silence and said to them: "I would declare for one of you children with a great deal of pleasure if I could do it with justice. But consider whether I can do it or no. It is true, Prince Ahmed, the Princess my niece owes her cure to your artificial apple. But I must ask you whether you could have helped her so well if you had not known by Prince Ali's seeing glass the danger she was in, and if Prince Houssain's tapestry had not brought you so soon. Your seeing glass, Prince Ali, told you and your brothers that you were likely to lose the Princess your cousin, and there you must admit a great debt.

"You must also agree that that knowledge would have been of no use without the artificial apple and the tapestry. And lastly, Prince Houssain, the Princess would be very ungrateful if she should not show her thanks for the service of your tapestry, which was so necessary a means toward her cure. But consider, it would have been of little use if you had not known of the Princess's illness by Prince Ali's glass, and Prince Ahmed had not used his artificial apple. Therefore, as neither tapestry, ivory seeing glass, nor artificial apple has the least preference one before the other, but, on the contrary, there is a perfect equality, I cannot grant the Princess to any one of you. The only fruit you have gathered from your travels is the glory of having equally helped to restore her health.

"If all this be true," added the Sultan, "you see that I must use other means to decide for certain in the choice I ought to make among you. And as there is time enough between this and night, I will do it today. Go and get each of you a bow and arrow, and go to the great plain, where they exercise horses. I will soon come to you, and declare I will give the Princess Nouronnihar to him that shoots the farthest."

The three Princes had nothing to say against the decision of the Sultan. When they were out of his presence, they each got a bow and arrow. They gave them to one of their officers and went to the plain appointed, followed by a great crowd of people.

The Sultan did not make them wait long for him. As soon as he arrived, Prince Houssain, as the eldest, took his bow and arrow and shot first. Prince Ali shot next, and much beyond him. And Prince Ahmed last of all, but it so happened that nobody could see where his arrow fell. And despite all the effort that was used by himself and everybody else, it was not to be found far or near. And though it was believed that he shot the farthest, and that he therefore deserved the Princess Nouronnihar, it was, however, necessary that his arrow should be found to make the matter more evident and certain. And despite his complaint, the Sultan judged in favor of Prince Ali. He gave orders for preparations to be made for the wedding, which was celebrated a few days after with great magnificence.

Prince Houssain would not honor the feast with his presence. In short, his grief was so violent and unbearable that he left the Court. He gave up all right of succession to the crown, to become a hermit.

Prince Ahmed also did not come to Prince Ali’s and the Princess Nouronnihar’s wedding, any more than his brother Houssain did. But he did not give up the world as his brother had done. He could not imagine what had happened to his arrow. So he slipped away from his servants and decided to look for it. He did not want to blame himself later for not trying. With this plan, he went to the place where Prince Houssain’s and Prince Ali’s arrows were found. From there he went straight forward, looking carefully on both sides of him. He went so far that at last he began to think his work was all for nothing. But he could not help going on until he came to some steep, rough rocks. These rocks were the end of his journey. They were in a empty country, about four leagues from where he started.

II

When Prince Ahmed came quite near these rocks, he saw an arrow. He picked it up and looked at it closely. He was very surprised to find it was the same one he had shot. “Surely,” he said to himself, “neither I nor any man alive could shoot an arrow so far.” He found it lying flat, not stuck in the ground. So he decided it had hit the rock and bounced back. “There must be some secret in this,” he said to himself again. “And it may be good for me. Perhaps fortune, to make up for taking away what I thought was the greatest happiness, has saved a greater blessing for my comfort.”

These rocks were full of caves, and some of those caves were deep. The Prince entered one. Looking around, he saw an iron door. It seemed to have no lock, but he feared it was locked. However, when he pushed against it, it opened. He saw an easy way down, but no steps. He walked down with his arrow in his hand. At first he thought he was going into a dark, hidden place. But soon a very different light came, different from the one he had left. He entered a large, wide place. At about fifty or sixty steps away, he saw a wonderful palace. He did not have time to look at it then. At the same time, a lady with a grand and noble manner came forward as far as the porch. A large group of ladies followed her. They were so beautifully dressed and so beautiful that it was hard to tell which one was the mistress.

As soon as Prince Ahmed saw the lady, he hurried as fast as he could to go and show his respect. The lady, for her part, saw him coming and spoke first before he could speak to her. She said to him: “Come nearer, Prince Ahmed, you are welcome.”

The Prince was very surprised to hear his own name in a place he had never heard of, though it was so near his father’s capital. He could not understand how a lady who was a stranger to him could know him. At last he returned the lady’s polite greeting by throwing himself at her feet. Then he stood up again and said to her:

“Madam, I give you a thousand thanks for promising me a welcome in a place where I believed my foolish curiosity had made me go too far. But, madam, may I, without being rude, dare to ask you how you know me? And how you, who live so near me, should be such a great stranger to me?”

“Prince,” said the lady, “let us go into the hall. There I will answer your request.”

After these words the lady led Prince Ahmed into the hall. Then she sat down on a sofa. When the Prince, at her request, had done the same, she said: “You say you are surprised that I should know you and not be known by you. But you will no longer be surprised when I tell you who I am. You surely know that your religion teaches you to believe that the world is lived in by genies as well as men. I am the daughter of one of the most powerful and famous genies. My name is Paribanou. The only thing I have to add is that you seemed to me worthy of a happier fate than that of having the Princess Nouronnihar. And so that you might reach it, I was present when you shot your arrow. I knew it would not go beyond Prince Houssain’s. I took it in the air and gave it the motion it needed to hit the rocks near which you found it. And I tell you that it lies in your power to use the good chance that presents itself to make you happy.”

As the Fairy Paribanou said these last words in a different tone, she looked tenderly at Prince Ahmed at the same time. A modest blush was on her cheeks. It was not hard for the Prince to understand what happiness she meant. He then considered that the Princess Nouronnihar could never be his. And the Fairy Paribanou was far better than her in beauty, pleasantness, and wit. And, as much as he could guess from the greatness of the palace, in huge riches. He blessed the moment that he thought of looking for his arrow a second time. Giving in to his love, he replied: “Madam, if I should all my life have the happiness of being your slave, and the admirer of the many charms which delight my soul, I should think myself the most blessed of men. Pardon in me the boldness which makes me ask this favor. Do not refuse to admit me into your Court, a prince who is entirely devoted to you.”

“Prince,” answered the Fairy, “will you not give me your promise, as I give mine to you?” “Yes, madam,” replied the Prince, in a burst of joy. “What can I do better, and with greater pleasure? Yes, my sultaness, my queen, I will give you my heart without the least reserve.” “Then,” answered the Fairy, “you are my husband, and I am your wife. But, as I suppose,” she went on, “that you have eaten nothing today, a small meal shall be served for you while preparations are made for our wedding feast tonight. And then I will show you the rooms of my palace, and you shall judge if this hall is not the poorest part of it.”

Some of the Fairy’s women, who came into the hall with them, guessed her intentions. They went out at once and soon returned with some excellent meats and wines.

When Prince Ahmed had eaten and drunk as much as he wanted, the Fairy Paribanou took him through all the rooms. There he saw diamonds, rubies, emeralds and all kinds of fine jewels, mixed with pearls, agate, jasper, porphyry, and all kinds of the most precious marbles. But not to speak of the richness of the furniture, which was beyond price, there was such plenty everywhere that the Prince had never seen anything like it. He said he could not have imagined that anything in the world could equal it. "Prince," said the Fairy, "if you admire my palace so much, which is indeed very beautiful, what would you say to the palaces of the chief of our genies, which are much more beautiful, spacious, and magnificent? I could also charm you with my gardens, but we will leave that for another time. Night is coming near, and it will be time to go to supper."

The next hall which the Fairy led the Prince into, and where the table was laid for the feast, was the last room the Prince had not seen, and not at all less fine than the others. When he entered it he admired the great number of candle holders with wax candles perfumed with amber. There were so many of them, but instead of being confused, they were placed with such exact symmetry that they formed a pleasing and pleasant sight. A large side table was set out with all kinds of gold plates, so finely made that the workmanship was much more valuable than the weight of the gold. Several groups of beautiful women, richly dressed, and whose voices were delightful, began a concert, accompanied with all kinds of the most harmonious instruments. When they sat down at table the Fairy Paribanou took care to help Prince Ahmed to the most delicate meats. She named each one as she invited him to eat of them. The Prince found them to be so exquisitely nice that he praised them with exaggeration, and said that the entertainment far surpassed those of man. He found also the same excellence in the wines, which neither he nor the Fairy tasted until the dessert was served, which consisted of the choicest sweetmeats and fruits.

The wedding feast was continued the next day, or rather, the days following the celebration were a continual feast.

At the end of six months Prince Ahmed, who always loved and honored the Sultan his father, conceived a great desire to know how he was. That desire could not be satisfied without his going to see. He told the Fairy of it, and desired she would give him leave.

"Prince," said she, "go when you please. But first, do not take it amiss that I give you some advice how you shall behave yourself where you are going. First, I do not think it proper for you to tell the Sultan your father of our marriage, nor of my quality, nor the place where you have been. Beg of him to be satisfied in knowing you are happy, and desire no more. And let him know that the sole end of your visit is to make him easy, and inform him of your fate."

She appointed twenty gentlemen, well mounted and equipped, to attend him. When all was ready Prince Ahmed took his leave of the Fairy, embraced her, and renewed his promise to return soon. Then his horse, which was most finely caparisoned, and was as beautiful a creature as any in the Sultan of Indies' stables, was led to him. He mounted him with an extraordinary grace. After he had bid her a last adieu, he set forward on his journey.

As it was not a great way to his father's capital, Prince Ahmed soon arrived there. The people, glad to see him again, received him with acclamations of joy, and followed him in crowds to the Sultan's apartment. The Sultan received and embraced him with great joy. At the same time he complained, with a fatherly tenderness, of the affliction his long absence had been to him. He said this was the more grievous because, fortune having decided in favor of Prince Ali his brother, he was afraid he might have committed some rash action.

The Prince told a story of his adventures without speaking of the Fairy, whom he said that he must not mention, and ended: "The only favor I ask of your Majesty is to give me leave to come often and pay you my respects, and to know how you do."

"Son," answered the Sultan of the Indies, "I cannot refuse you the leave you ask me. But I should much rather you would resolve to stay with me. At least tell me where I may send to you if you should fail to come, or when I may think your presence necessary." "Sir," replied Prince Ahmed, "what your Majesty asks of me is part of the mystery I spoke to your Majesty of. I beg of you to give me leave to remain silent on this head, for I shall come so frequently that I am afraid that I shall sooner be thought troublesome than be accused of negligence in my duty."

The Sultan of the Indies pressed Prince Ahmed no more, but said to him: "Son, I penetrate no farther into your secrets, but leave you at your liberty. But can tell you that you could not do me a greater pleasure than to come, and by your presence restore to me the joy I have not felt this long time. And you shall always be welcome when you come, without interrupting your business or pleasure."

Prince Ahmed stayed but three days at the Sultan his father's Court, and the fourth returned to the Fairy Paribanou, who did not expect him so soon.

A month after Prince Ahmed's return from paying a visit to his father, the Fairy Paribanou had observed that the Prince had never talked of the Sultan. This was since the time that he gave her an account of his journey, his discourse with his father, and the leave he asked to go and see him often. It was as if there had been no such person in the world, whereas before he was always speaking of him. She thought he forebore on her account. Therefore she took an opportunity to say to him one day: "Prince, tell me, have you forgot the Sultan your father? Do you not remember the promise you made to go and see him often? For my part I have not forgot what you told me at your return, and so put you in mind of it, that you may not be long before you acquit yourself of your promise."

So Prince Ahmed went the next morning with the same attendance as before, but much finer, and himself more magnificently mounted, equipped, and dressed, and was received by the Sultan with the same joy and satisfaction. For several months he constantly paid his visits, always in a richer and finer equipage.

At last, some officials close to the Sultan judged Prince Ahmed's power by his appearance. They made the Sultan feel sad and afraid of his son. They said they were afraid the Prince might win the support of the people. They said he might take the Sultan's place as leader.

CHAPTER 8. Part 8

The Sultan of the Indies did not think that Prince Ahmed could plan such a harmful thing as his favorites wanted him to believe. He said to them: "You are wrong. My son loves me. I am sure of his love and loyalty. I have given him no reason to be upset with me."

But the favorites kept saying bad things about Prince Ahmed. At last the Sultan said: "Let it be as it will. I do not believe my son Ahmed is as bad as you want me to think. Still, I thank you for your good advice. I do not doubt that it comes from good intentions."

The Sultan of the Indies said this so that his favorites would not know how their words had affected his mind. Their words had worried him so much that he decided to have Prince Ahmed watched without the grand vizier knowing. So he sent for a woman with magic powers. She was brought in through a back door into his room. "Go at once," he said, "and follow my son. Watch him well and find out where he goes. Then bring me word."

The woman left the Sultan. She knew the place where Prince Ahmed found his arrow. She went there at once and hid near the rocks, so that nobody could see her.

The next morning Prince Ahmed set out at first light. He did not say goodbye to the Sultan or to anyone at Court, as was his habit. The woman saw him coming and followed him with her eyes. Then suddenly she lost sight of him and his men.

The rocks were very steep and rough. They were a barrier that could not be crossed. So the woman judged that there were only two possibilities. Either the Prince went into some cave, or he went to a home of genies or fairies. Then she came out of her hiding place and went straight to the hollow path. She followed it to the far end, looking carefully on all sides. But in spite of all her effort, she could see no opening. She could not even see the iron gate that Prince Ahmed had found. That gate could be seen and opened only by men, and only by those whom the Fairy Paribanou was happy to see.

The woman saw that it was useless to search any farther. She had to be satisfied with what she had found, and she returned to give the Sultan an account.

The Sultan was very pleased with what the woman had done. He said to her: "Do as you think fit. I will wait calmly for the result of your promises." To encourage her, he gave her a diamond of great value.

The Fairy Paribanou had given Prince Ahmed leave to go to the Sultan of the Indies' Court once a month. He never failed to go. The woman knew the time, so she went a day or two before to the foot of the rock where she had lost sight of the Prince and his men. She waited there.

The next morning Prince Ahmed went out as usual at the iron gate, with the same men as before. He passed by the woman. He did not know what she was. He saw her lying with her head against the rock. She was complaining as if she were in great pain. He felt sorry for her. He turned his horse around, went to her, and asked her what was wrong and what he could do to ease her pain.

The clever woman looked at the Prince in a sad way. She did not lift her head. She answered in broken words and sighs, as if she could hardly breathe. She said she was going to the capital city. But on the way there she was taken with such a violent fever that her strength failed her. She was forced to lie down where he saw her, far from any home, and with no hope of help.

"Good woman," replied Prince Ahmed, "you are not so far from help as you imagine. I am ready to help you and carry you where you will meet with a quick cure. Just get up, and let one of my people take you behind him."

At these words the woman, who only pretended to be sick so she could learn where the Prince lived and what he did, did not refuse his kind offer. To make her actions match her words, she made many useless efforts to get up. At the same time two of the Prince's men got off their horses. They helped her up and set her behind another man. Then they mounted their horses again and followed the Prince. He turned back to the iron gate. One of his men who rode in front opened it. When he came into the outer court of the Fairy, he did not get off his horse. He sent to tell her he wanted to speak with her.

The Fairy Paribanou came with all possible speed. She did not know why Prince Ahmed had returned so soon. He did not give her time to ask him the reason. He said: "Princess, I want you to have compassion on this good woman." He pointed to the woman, who was held up by two of his men. "I found her in the condition you see her in. I promised her the help she needs. I am sure that you, out of your own goodness, as well as because I ask you, will not abandon her."

The Fairy Paribanou had kept her eyes on the pretended sick woman all the time the Prince was talking to her. She ordered two of her women who followed her to take the woman from the two men who held her. They were to carry her into a room of the palace and take as much care of her as the Fairy would herself.

While the two women carried out the Fairy's commands, she went up to Prince Ahmed. She whispered in his ear: "Prince, this woman is not as sick as she pretends to be. I am very much mistaken if she is not a liar, who will cause you great trouble. But do not be concerned. Let what will be planned against you. Be sure that I will deliver you out of all the traps that shall be laid for you. Go and continue your journey."

This talk of the Fairy's did not frighten Prince Ahmed at all. "My Princess," said he, "I do not remember that I ever did or planned anybody an injury. I cannot believe anybody can have a thought of doing me one. But if they have, I shall not stop doing good whenever I have an opportunity." Then he went back to his father's palace.

The two women took the magician into a very nice room with fine furniture. First they sat her on a long soft seat. They put a gold cloth cushion behind her back. Then they made a bed for her on the same seat. The bed cover had beautiful silk sewing. The sheets were the finest linen. The top cover was gold cloth. The old sorceress pretended her fever was so bad she could not move at all. Then one woman went out. She came back soon with a china dish full of a certain drink. She gave it to the magician. The other woman helped her sit up. "Drink this," she said. "It is the Water of the Fountain of Lions. It is a sure cure for all fevers. You will feel it work in less than an hour."

The magician wanted to hide her true feelings. So she took it only after much begging. At last she took the china dish. She held back her head and swallowed the drink. Then they laid her down again and covered her up. "Lie quiet," said the one who brought the cup. "Try to sleep a little if you can. We will leave you. We hope to find you fully cured when we come back in an hour."

The two women came back at the time they had said. They found the magician up and dressed, sitting on the long soft seat. "Oh, wonderful drink!" she said. "It cured me much sooner than you told me. Now I can go on with my journey."

The two women were fairies, like their mistress. They told the magician they were glad she was cured so soon. Then they walked in front of her. They led her through several rooms. All were finer than the room where she had lain. They came to a large hall. It was the most richly decorated room in all the palace.

Fairy Paribanou sat in this hall on a throne of solid gold. The throne was set with diamonds, rubies, and pearls of great size. Many beautiful fairies stood on each side of her. They were all dressed in rich clothes. The magician saw so much majesty that she was amazed. She bowed low before the throne. But she could not open her lips to thank the Fairy as she had planned. Paribanou saved her the trouble. She said: "Good woman, I am glad I had a chance to help you. I am glad to see you can go on with your journey. I will not keep you. But perhaps you would like to see my palace. Follow my women, and they will show it to you."

Then the magician went back. She told the Sultan of the Indies all that had happened. She told him how very rich Prince Ahmed was since his marriage with the Fairy. He was richer than all the kings in the world. She said there was danger that he would come and take the throne from his father.

The Sultan of the Indies was sure that Prince Ahmed had a good nature. Still, he could not help worrying about the old sorceress's words. When she was leaving, he said to her: "I thank you for your work and your good advice. I know how important this is to me. I shall think about it with my council."

Now the favorites advised that the Prince should be killed. But the magician gave different advice. "Make him give you all kinds of wonderful things, with the Fairy's help. Do this until she tires of him and sends him away. For example, every time your Majesty goes to war, you must spend a great deal. You need pavilions and tents for your army. You also need mules and camels to carry the baggage. Now, could you not ask him to use his influence with the Fairy? Ask her to get you a tent that a man can carry in his hand. It should be so large that it shelters your whole army from bad weather."

When the magician finished speaking, the Sultan asked his favorites if they had anything better to suggest. They were all silent. So he decided to follow the magician's advice. It seemed the most reasonable plan, and the most agreeable to his mild rule.

Next day the Sultan did as the magician had advised. He asked for the pavilion.

Prince Ahmed never expected his father the Sultan to ask such a thing. At first it seemed very difficult, even impossible. He did not know exactly how great the power of genies and fairies was. He doubted it could reach so far as to make such a tent as his father wanted. At last he replied: "I do this with the greatest reluctance. But I will not fail to ask my wife for the favor your Majesty desires. However, I will not promise to obtain it. If I do not have the honor to come again to pay you my respects, that will be the sign that I have not succeeded. But first, I ask you to forgive me. Remember that you yourself have forced me to this extreme."

"Son," replied the Sultan of the Indies, "I should be very sorry if what I ask of you should cause me the displeasure of never seeing you again. I see you do not know the power a husband has over a wife. Yours would show that her love for you was very weak if she, with the power of a fairy, should refuse you so small a request as this. I desire you to ask it of her for my sake." The Prince went back. He was very sad, for fear of offending the Fairy. She kept pressing him to tell her what was the matter. At last he said: "Madam, you may have seen that until now I have been content with your love. I have never asked you any other favor. Consider then, I beg you, that it is not I, but the Sultan my father, who indiscreetly, or at least I think so, begs of you a pavilion. It must be large enough to shelter him, his Court, and army from bad weather. And a man must be able to carry it in his hand. But remember it is the Sultan my father who asks this favor."

"Prince," replied the Fairy, smiling, "I am sorry that so small a matter should disturb you. I am sorry it should make you so uneasy as you appeared to me."

Then the Fairy sent for her treasurer. When she came, the Fairy said to her: “Nourgihan”—which was her name—“bring me the largest pavilion in my treasury.” Nourgihan returned presently with the pavilion. She could hold it not only in her hand, but in the palm of her hand when she shut her fingers. She presented it to her mistress, who gave it to Prince Ahmed to look at.

When Prince Ahmed saw the pavilion which the Fairy called the largest in her treasury, he thought she wanted to jest with him. Then the marks of his surprise appeared presently on his face. Paribanou saw this and burst out laughing. “What! Prince,” cried she, “do you think I jest with you? You’ll see presently that I am in earnest. Nourgihan,” said she to her treasurer, taking the tent out of Prince Ahmed’s hands, “go and set it up, so the Prince may judge whether it may be large enough for the Sultan his father.”

The treasurer went immediately with it out of the palace. She carried it a great way off. When she had set it up, one end reached to the very palace. At that time the Prince, thinking it small, found it large enough to shelter two greater armies than that of the Sultan his father. Then he said to Paribanou: “I ask my Princess a thousand pardons for my incredulity. After what I have seen, I believe there is nothing impossible to you.” “You see,” said the Fairy, “that the pavilion is larger than what your father may have occasion for. For you must know that it has one property—that it is larger or smaller according to the army it is to cover.”

The treasurer took down the tent again and brought it to the Prince. He took it, and did not stay any longer than till the next day. He mounted his horse and went with the same attendants to the Sultan his father.

The Sultan was sure that there could not be any such thing as such a tent as he asked for. He was in a great surprise at the Prince’s diligence. He took the tent. After he had admired its smallness, his amazement was so great that he could not recover himself. When the tent was set up in the great plain, which we have before mentioned, he found it large enough to shelter an army twice as large as he could bring into the field.

But the Sultan was not yet satisfied. “Son,” said he, “I have already expressed to you how much I am obliged to you for the present of the tent you have procured me. I look upon it as the most valuable thing in all my treasury. But you must do one thing more for me, which will be every whit as agreeable to me. I am informed that the Fairy, your spouse, makes use of a certain water, called the Water of the Fountain of Lions. It cures all sorts of fevers, even the most dangerous. I am perfectly well persuaded my health is dear to you. I don’t doubt but you will ask her for a bottle of that water for me. Bring it me as a sovereign medicine, which I may make use of when I have occasion. Do me this other important piece of service, and thereby complete the duty of a good son toward a tender father.”

The Prince returned and told the Fairy what his father had said. “There’s a great deal of wickedness in this demand?” she answered, “as you will understand by what I am going to tell you. The Fountain of Lions is situated in the middle of a court of a great castle. The entrance into it is guarded by four fierce lions. Two of them sleep alternately, while the other two are awake. But don’t let that frighten you. I’ll give you means to pass by them without any danger.”

The Fairy Paribanou was at that time very hard at work. She had several clews of thread by her. She took up one and presented it to Prince Ahmed. She said: “First take this clew of thread. I’ll tell you presently the use of it. In the second place, you must have two horses. One you must ride yourself, and the other you must lead. It must be loaded with a sheep cut into four quarters, that must be killed to-day. In the third place, you must be provided with a bottle, which I will give you, to bring the water in. Set out early to-morrow morning. When you have passed the iron gate, throw the clew of thread before you. It will roll till it comes to the gates of the castle. Follow it, and when it stops, as the gates will be open, you will see the four lions. The two that are awake will, by their roaring, wake the other two. But don’t be frightened. Throw each of them a quarter of mutton. Then clap spurs to your horse and ride to the fountain. Fill your bottle without alighting, and then return with the same expedition. The lions will be so busy eating they will let you pass by them.”

Prince Ahmed set out the next morning at the time appointed by the Fairy. He followed her directions exactly. When he arrived at the gates of the castle, he distributed the quarters of mutton among the four lions. He passed through the midst of them bravely, got to the fountain, filled his bottle, and returned back as safe and sound as he went. When he had gone a little distance from the castle gates, he turned about. He perceived two of the lions coming after him. He drew his sabre and prepared himself for defense. But as he went forward he saw one of them turned out of the road at some distance. It showed by his head and tail that he did not come to do him any harm, but only to go before him. The other stayed behind to follow. He put his sword up again in its scabbard. Guarded in this manner, he arrived at the capital of the Indies. But the lions never left him till they had conducted him to the gates of the Sultan’s palace. After that they returned the same way they came, though not without frightening all that saw them. For all they went in a very gentle manner and showed no fierceness.

A great many officers came to attend the Prince while he dismounted his horse. Afterward they conducted him into the Sultan’s apartment. The Sultan was at that time surrounded with his favorites. The Prince approached toward the throne. He laid the bottle at the Sultan’s feet and kissed the rich tapestry which covered his footstool. Then he said:

“I have brought you, sir, the healthful water which your Majesty desired so much to keep among your other rarities in your treasury. But at the same time I wish you such extraordinary health as never to have occasion to make use of it.”

After the Prince finished his kind words, the Sultan placed him on his right side. Then he said to him: "Son, I am very grateful to you for this valuable gift. I am also grateful for the great danger you faced for me. A man with magic powers who knows the Fountain of Lions told me about it. But please do me a favor," he continued. "Tell me how you kept yourself safe. Was it by skill, or rather by some amazing power?"

CHAPTER 9. Part 9

"Sir," replied Prince Ahmed, "I have no part in the praise your Majesty is pleased to give me. All the honor belongs to the Fairy my wife. I followed her good advice." Then he told the Sultan what those directions were. By telling this story, he let him know how well he had done. When he had finished, the Sultan showed outwardly all the signs of great joy. But secretly he became more jealous. He went into a private room, where he sent for the magician.

When the magician arrived, she saved the Sultan the trouble of telling her about the success of Prince Ahmed's journey. She had heard of it before she came. So she was ready with a sure method, as she claimed. She told this method to the Sultan. He declared it the next day to the Prince, in the middle of all his courtiers, in these words: "Son," said he, "I have one more thing to ask of you. After this I shall expect nothing more from your obedience, nor from your influence with your wife. This request is to bring me a man not more than a foot and a half high. His beard is thirty feet long. He carries a bar of iron on his shoulders of five hundredweight, which he uses as a quarterstaff."

Prince Ahmed did not believe that such a man existed in the world as his father described. He would gladly have excused himself. But the Sultan insisted on his demand. He told him the Fairy could do more incredible things.

The next day the Prince returned to his dear Paribanou. He told her his father's new demand. He said he looked on it as a thing more impossible than the two first. "For," he added, "I cannot imagine there can be such a man in the world. Without doubt, he wants to try whether I am so silly as to go about it, or he has a plan to ruin me. In short, how can he suppose that I should take hold of a man so well armed, though he is but little? What arms can I use to make him do my will? If there are any means, I beg you will tell them, and let me come off with honor this time."

"Don't be frightened, Prince," replied the Fairy. "You ran a risk in getting the Water of the Fountain of Lions for your father. But there is no danger in finding this man. He is my brother Schaibar. He is not at all like me, though we both had the same father. He is of such a violent nature that nothing can stop him from giving cruel marks of his anger for a slight offense. Yet, on the other hand, he is so good as to help anyone in whatever they desire. He is made exactly as the Sultan your father has described him. He has no other arms than a bar of iron of five hundred pounds weight. Without it he never moves, and it makes him respected. I'll send for him, and you shall judge the truth of what I tell you. But be sure to prepare yourself against being frightened at his extraordinary figure when you see him." "What! my Queen," replied Prince Ahmed, "do you say Schaibar is your brother? Let him be never so ugly or deformed, I shall be so far from being frightened at the sight of him that, as our brother, I shall honor and love him."

The Fairy ordered a gold chafing-dish to be set with a fire in it under the porch of her palace. She also ordered a box of the same metal, which was a present to her. She took a perfume out of it and threw it into the fire. A thick cloud of smoke rose up.

Some moments later the Fairy said to Prince Ahmed: "See, there comes my brother." The Prince immediately saw Schaibar coming gravely with his heavy bar on his shoulder. He saw his long beard, which he held up before him. He saw a pair of thick mustachios, which he tucked behind his ears and almost covered his face. His eyes were very small and deep-set in his head, which was far from being of the smallest size. On his head he wore a grenadier's cap. Besides all this, he was very much hump-backed.

If Prince Ahmed had not known that Schaibar was Paribanou's brother, he would not have been able to look at him without fear. But knowing first who he was, he stood by the Fairy without the least concern.

Schaibar, as he came forward, looked at the Prince earnestly enough to have chilled his blood in his veins. He asked Paribanou, when he first spoke to her, who that man was. To which she replied: "He is my husband, brother. His name is Ahmed. He is son to the Sultan of the Indies. The reason why I did not invite you to my wedding was I was unwilling to divert you from an expedition you were engaged in. I heard with pleasure you returned victorious, and so took the liberty now to call for you."

At these words, Schaibar, looking on Prince Ahmed favorably, said: "Is there anything else, sister, in which I can serve him? It is enough for me that he is your husband to make me do for him whatever he desires." "The Sultan, his father," replied Paribanou, "has a curiosity to see you, and I desire he may be your guide to the Sultan's Court." "He needs but lead me the way I'll follow him." "Brother," replied Paribanou, "it is too late to go today, therefore stay till tomorrow morning. In the meantime I'll inform you of all that has passed between the Sultan of the Indies and Prince Ahmed since our marriage."

The next morning, after Schaibar had been informed of the affair, he and Prince Ahmed set out for the Sultan's Court. When they arrived at the gates of the capital, the people no sooner saw Schaibar than they ran and hid themselves. Some shut up their shops and locked themselves up in their houses. Others flew and passed their fear to all they met. These did not stay to look behind them, but ran too. So Schaibar and Prince Ahmed, as they went along, found the streets all empty till they came to the palaces. There the porters, instead of keeping the gates, ran away too. So the Prince and Schaibar advanced without any obstacle to the council-hall, where the Sultan was seated on his throne, and giving audience. Here likewise the ushers, at the approach of Schaibar, left their posts, and gave them free admittance.

Schaibar went boldly and fiercely up to the throne, without waiting to be presented by Prince Ahmed. He spoke to the Sultan of the Indies in these words: "You have asked for me," said he. "See, here I am. What would you have with me?"

The Sultan did not answer him. Instead, he clapped his hands before his eyes to avoid the sight of so terrible a thing. Schaibar was much angered by this rude and impolite welcome. He had come so far, and this was his thanks. He lifted up his iron bar at once and killed him before Prince Ahmed could speak for him. All that he could do was to stop him from killing the grand vizier, who sat not far from him. He told Schaibar that the grand vizier had always given the Sultan his father good advice. "These are they, then," said Schaibar, "who gave him bad." As he said these words, he killed all the other viziers and the flattering friends of the Sultan who were Prince Ahmed's enemies. Every time he struck, he killed someone. None escaped except those who were not so afraid that they stood staring and gaping. Those ones saved themselves by running away.

When this terrible killing was over, Schaibar came out of the council-hall into the middle of the courtyard with the iron bar upon his shoulder. He looked hard at the grand vizier, who owed his life to Prince Ahmed. He said: "I know there is a certain magician here. She is a greater enemy of my brother-in-law than all these low friends I have punished. Let the magician be brought to me at once." The grand vizier sent for her immediately. As soon as she was brought, Schaibar struck at her with his iron bar and said: "Take the reward of your evil advice, and learn to pretend to be sick again."

After this he said: "This is not yet enough. I will do the same to the whole town if they do not at once accept Prince Ahmed, my brother-in-law, as their Sultan and the Sultan of the Indies." Then all who were there present made the air echo again with repeated shouts of: "Long life to Sultan Ahmed." Right after that he was proclaimed through the whole town. Schaibar had him dressed in the royal clothes and placed him on the throne. After he had made all swear loyalty to him, he went and brought his sister Paribanou. He brought her with all the show and greatness imaginable, and made her be owned as Sultaness of the Indies.

As for Prince Ali and Princess Nouronnihar, they had no part in the plan against Prince Ahmed and knew nothing of any of it. So Prince Ahmed gave them a large province, with its capital, where they spent the rest of their lives. Afterwards he sent an officer to Prince Houssain to tell him about the change and to offer him whichever province he liked best. But that Prince thought himself so happy in his quiet life that he asked the officer to return the Sultan his brother thanks for the kindness he meant him. He assured him of his obedience. The only favor he asked of him was to let him live retired in the place he had chosen for his retreat.

THE HISTORY OF JACK THE GIANT-KILLER

In the time of the famous King Arthur there lived in Cornwall a boy named Jack. He was a boy of a bold temper. He took delight in hearing or reading of conjurers, giants, and fairies. He used to listen eagerly to the deeds of the knights of King Arthur's Round Table.

In those days there lived on St. Michael's Mount, off Cornwall, a huge giant, eighteen feet high and nine feet round. His fierce and wild looks were the terror of all who saw him.

He lived in a dark cave on the top of the mountain. He used to wade over to the mainland in search of prey. He would throw half a dozen oxen upon his back, and tie three times as many sheep and hogs round his waist, and march back to his own home.

The giant had done this for many years when Jack resolved to destroy him.

Jack took a horn, a shovel, a pickaxe, his armor, and a dark lantern. One winter's evening he went to the mount. There he dug a pit twenty-two feet deep and twenty broad. He covered the top over so as to make it look like solid ground. He then blew his horn so loudly that the giant awoke and came out of his den crying out: "You saucy villain! You shall pay for this. I'll broil you for my breakfast!"

He had just finished, when, taking one step further, he tumbled headlong into the pit. Jack struck him a blow on the head with his pickaxe which killed him. Jack then returned home to cheer his friends with the news.

Another giant, called Blunderbore, vowed to be revenged on Jack if ever he should have him in his power. This giant kept an enchanted castle in the middle of a lonely wood. Some time after the death of Cormoran, Jack was passing through a wood. He was weary, so he sat down and went to sleep.

The giant, passing by and seeing Jack, carried him to his castle. There he locked him up in a large room. The floor of the room was covered with the bodies, skulls and bones of men and women.

Soon after, the giant went to fetch his brother, who was likewise a giant, to take a meal off his flesh. Jack saw with terror through the bars of his prison the two giants approaching.

Jack saw a strong cord in one corner of the room. He took courage, and made a slip-knot at each end. He threw them over their heads, and tied it to the window-bars. He then pulled till he had choked them. When they were black in the face he slid down the rope and stabbed them to the heart.

Jack next took a great bunch of keys from the pocket of Blunderbore, and went into the castle again. He made a strict search through all the rooms. In one of them he found three ladies tied up by the hair of their heads, and almost starved to death. They told him that their husbands had been killed by the giants. The giants had then condemned them to be starved to death because they would not eat the flesh of their own dead husbands.

"Ladies," said Jack, "I have put an end to the monster and his wicked brother. And I give you this castle and all the riches it contains, to make some amends for the dreadful pains you have felt." He then very politely gave them the keys of the castle, and went further on his journey to Wales.

As Jack had but little money, he went on as fast as possible. At length he came to a handsome house. Jack knocked at the door, when there came forth a Welsh giant. Jack said he was a traveler who had lost his way. On this the giant made him welcome, and let him into a room where there was a good bed to sleep in.

Jack took off his clothes quickly. He was tired, but he could not sleep. Soon after this, he heard the giant walking backward and forward in the next room. The giant said to himself:

“Though here you stay with me this night, You shall not see the morning light; My club shall hit your head and break it quite.”

“Say you so?” thought Jack. “Are these your tricks on travelers? But I hope to be as clever as you are.” Then he got out of bed. He felt around the room in the dark. At last he found a large thick piece of wood. He laid it in his own place in the bed. Then he hid himself in a dark corner of the room.

The giant came into the room about midnight. He hit the bed many times with his heavy club, right where Jack had laid the log. Then he went back to his own room. He thought he had broken all Jack’s bones.

Early in the morning, Jack looked brave. He walked into the giant’s room to thank him for his lodging. The giant was surprised when he saw him. He began to say with difficulty: “Oh! dear me; is it you? Pray how did you sleep last night? Did you hear or see anything in the dead of the night?”

“Nothing to speak of,” said Jack, without care. “A rat, I believe, hit me three or four times with its tail and troubled me a little. But I soon went to sleep again.”

The giant wondered more and more at this. Yet he did not answer a word. He went to bring two great bowls of thick porridge for their breakfast. Jack wanted to make the giant believe that he could eat as much as the giant. So he found a way to button a bag made of animal skin inside his coat. He slipped the porridge into this bag while he seemed to put it into his mouth.

When breakfast was over, he said to the giant: “Now I will show you a fine trick. I can heal all wounds with a touch. I could cut off my head in one minute, and the next minute put it back safe on my shoulders. You shall see an example.” He then took hold of the knife, cut open the bag of animal skin, and all the porridge fell out onto the floor.

“Ods splutter hur nails!” cried the Welsh giant. He was ashamed to be beaten by such a little fellow as Jack. “Hur can do that hurself.” So he quickly took up the knife, pushed it into his own stomach, and in a moment fell down dead.

Jack had been successful in all his tasks so far. He decided not to be lazy in future. So he got himself a horse, a cap of knowledge, a sword of sharpness, shoes of speed, and a coat that made him invisible. These would help him do the wonderful tasks that lay before him.

He traveled over high hills. On the third day, he came to a large and wide forest. His road lay through it. He had scarcely entered the forest when he saw a huge giant. The giant was dragging a handsome knight and his lady by the hair of their heads. Jack got off his horse and tied him to an oak tree. He put on his invisible coat. Under it, he carried his sword of sharpness.

When he came up to the giant, he made several strokes at him. But he could not reach his body. He wounded his thighs in several places. At last, he put both hands to his sword and aimed with all his might. He cut off both the giant’s legs. Then Jack put his foot on the giant’s neck and pushed his sword into the giant’s body. The monster gave a groan and died.

The knight and his lady thanked Jack for saving them. They invited him to their house to receive a proper reward for his services. “No,” said Jack. “I cannot rest until I find out where this monster lives.” So he took the knight’s directions. He got on his horse and soon after came in sight of another giant. This giant was sitting on a block of wood, waiting for his brother to return.

Jack got off his horse. He put on his invisible coat, came near, and aimed a blow at the giant’s head. But he missed his aim and only cut off his nose. On this, the giant seized his club and hit about him most without mercy.

“Nay,” said Jack. “If this is the case, I had better finish you!” So he jumped onto the block and stabbed him in the back. The giant fell down dead.

Jack then went on his journey. He traveled over hills and valleys until he arrived at the foot of a high mountain. He knocked at the door of a lonely house, and an old man let him in.

When Jack was seated, the hermit spoke to him: “My son, on the top of this mountain is an enchanted castle. It is kept by the giant Galligantus and a vile magician. I am sad about the fate of a duke’s daughter. They seized her as she was walking in her father’s garden and brought her here. She was changed into a deer.”

Jack promised that in the morning, at the risk of his life, he would break the enchantment. After a good sleep, he rose early. He put on his invisible coat and got ready for the attempt.

When he had climbed to the top of the mountain, he saw two fiery griffins. But he passed between them without the least fear of danger. They could not see him because of his invisible coat. On the castle gate, he found a golden trumpet. Under it were written these lines:

“Whoever can this trumpet blow Shall cause the giant’s overthrow.”

As soon as Jack had read this, he seized the trumpet and blew a shrill blast. The gates flew open, and the very castle itself trembled.

The giant and the conjurer now knew that their wicked course was at an end. They stood biting their thumbs and shaking with fear. Jack, with his sword of sharpness, soon killed the giant. The magician was then carried away by a whirlwind. Every knight and beautiful lady who had been changed into birds and beasts returned to their proper shapes. The castle vanished away like smoke. The head of the giant Galligantus was then sent to King Arthur.

The knights and ladies rested that night at the old man’s hermitage. Next day they set out for the Court. Jack then went up to the King and gave his Majesty an account of all his fierce battles.

CHAPTER 10. Part 10

Jack’s fame had now spread through the whole country. At the King’s wish, the duke gave him his daughter in marriage. All the kingdom was full of joy. After this, the King gave him a large estate. He and his lady lived there the rest of their days in joy and contentment.(1)

THE BLACK BULL OF NORROWAY

And many a hunting song they sung, And song of game and glee; Then tuned to plaintive strains their tongue, “Of Scotland’s luve and lee.” To wilder measures next they turn “The Black, Black Bull of Norroway!” Sudden the tapers cease to burn, The minstrels cease to play.

“The Cout of Keeldar,” by J. Leyden.

In Norroway, long ago, there lived a certain lady. She had three daughters. The oldest of them said to her mother: “Mother, bake me a bannock, and roast me a collop, for I’m going away to seek my fortune.” Her mother did so. The daughter went away to an old witch washerwife and told her purpose. The old wife told her to stay that day. She told her to go and look out of her back door and see what she could see. She saw nothing the first day. The second day she did the same, and saw nothing. On the third day she looked again. She saw a coach-and-six coming along the road. She ran in and told the old wife what she saw. “Well,” said the old wife, “that’s for you.” So they took her into the coach, and galloped off.

The second daughter next says to her mother: “Mother, bake me a bannock, and roast me a collop, for I’m going away to seek my fortune.” Her mother did so. Away she went to the old wife, as her sister had done. On the third day she looked out of the back door. She saw a coach-and-four coming along the road. “Well,” said the old wife, “that’s for you.” So they took her in, and off they set.

The third daughter says to her mother: “Mother, bake me a bannock, and roast me a collop, for I’m going away to seek my fortune.” Her mother did so. Away she went to the old witch-wife. She told her to look out of her back door and see what she could see. She did so. When she came back, she said she saw nothing. The second day she did the same, and saw nothing. The third day she looked again. On coming back, she said to the old wife she saw nothing but a big Black Bull coming roaring along the road. “Well,” said the old wife, “that’s for you.” On hearing this she was almost mad with grief and terror. But she was lifted up and set on his back, and away they went.

On they traveled, and on they traveled, until the lady grew faint with hunger. “Eat out of my right ear,” says the Black Bull, “and drink out of my left ear, and set by your leavings.” So she did as he said, and was wonderfully refreshed. And long they went, and hard they rode, until they came in sight of a very big and beautiful castle. “We must be there this night,” said the bull, “for my old brother lives there.” And soon they were at the place. They lifted her off his back, and took her in, and sent him away to a park for the night. In the morning, when they brought the bull home, they took the lady into a fine shining parlor. They gave her a beautiful apple. They told her not to break it until she was in the greatest strait ever mortal was in in the world. That would bring her out of it. Again she was lifted on the bull’s back. After she had ridden far, and farther than I can tell, they came in sight of a far more beautiful castle, and far farther away than the last. Says the bull to her: “We must be there the night, for my second brother lives there.” And they were at the place directly. They lifted her down and took her in, and sent the bull to the field for the night. In the morning they took the lady into a fine and rich room. They gave her the finest pear she had ever seen. They told her not to break it until she was in the greatest strait ever mortal could be in. That would get her out of it. Again she was lifted and set on his back, and away they went. And long they went, and hard they rode, until they came in sight of the far biggest castle, and far farthest off, they had yet seen. “We must be there the night,” says the bull, “for my young brother lives there.” And they were there directly. They lifted her down, took her in, and sent the bull to the field for the night. In the morning they took her into a room, the finest of all. They gave her a plum. They told her not to break it until she was in the greatest strait mortal could be in. That would get her out of it. Soon they brought the bull home, set the lady on his back, and away they went.

And on they went, and on they rode, until they came to a dark and ugly glen. There they stopped, and the lady got down. Says the bull to her: “Here you must stay until I go and fight the devil. You must seat yourself on that stone, and move neither hand nor foot until I come back. Else I’ll never find you again. And if everything round about you turns blue, I have beaten the devil. But should all things turn red, he will have conquered me.” She set herself down on the stone. By-and-by all round her turned blue. Overcome with joy, she lifted the one foot and crossed it over the other. She was so glad that her companion was victorious. The bull returned and sought for her, but never could find her.

She sat for a long time and cried until she was tired. At last she got up and went away. She did not know where to go. She walked on until she came to a great hill of glass. She tried everything she could to climb it, but she was not able. She went around the bottom of the hill, crying and looking for a way over. At last she came to a smith's house. The smith promised that if she would serve him for seven years, he would make her iron shoes. With them she could climb over the glass hill. At the end of seven years she got her iron shoes. She climbed the glass hill and happened to come to the old washerwife's home. There she was told of a brave young knight who had given some bloody shirts to wash. Whoever washed those shirts was to be his wife. The old wife had washed until she was tired. Then she set her daughter to work. Both of them washed and washed and washed better, hoping to get the young knight. But all they could do, they could not get out a stain. At length they set the stranger young woman to work. As soon as she began, the stains came out pure and clean. But the old wife made the knight believe that it was her daughter who had washed the shirts. So the knight and the older daughter were to be married. The stranger young woman was very upset at the thought of it, for she was deeply in love with him. So she thought of her apple. She broke it open and found it filled with gold and precious jewelry, the richest she had ever seen. "All these," she said to the older daughter, "I will give you, if you put off your marriage for one day and let me go into his room alone at night." So the lady agreed. But meanwhile the old wife had prepared a sleeping drink and given it to the knight. He drank it and never woke until the next morning. All through the long night the young woman cried and sang:

"Seven long years I served for you, The glass hill I climbed for you, The bloody shirt I wrung for you; And will you not wake and turn to me?"

The next day she did not know what to do for grief. Then she broke the pear and found it filled with jewelry far richer than what was in the apple. With those jewels she made a deal for permission to be a second night in the young knight's room. But the old wife gave him another sleeping drink, and he again slept until morning. All night she kept sighing and singing as before:

"Seven long years I served for you," etc. Still he slept, and she nearly lost hope altogether. But that day, when he was out hunting, somebody asked him what noise and moaning it was that they heard all last night in his bedroom. He said he heard no noise. But they assured him there was. And he decided to stay awake that night to try what he could hear. That was the third night, and the young woman was between hope and despair. She broke her plum, and it held by far the richest jewelry of the three. She made the same deal as before. And the old wife, as before, took the sleeping drink into the young knight's room. But he told her he could not drink it that night without something sweet in it. And when she went away for some honey to make it sweet, he poured out the drink. So he made the old wife think he had drunk it. They all went to bed again, and the young woman began, as before, singing:

"Seven long years I served for you, The glass hill I climbed for you, The bloody shirt I wrung for you; And will you not wake and turn to me?"

He heard, and turned to her. And she told him all that had happened to her, and he told her all that had happened to him. And he had the old washerwife and her daughter burned. And they were married, and he and she are living happy to this day, as far as I know.(1)

(1) Chambers, Popular Traditions of Scotland.

THE RED ETIN

There were once two widows that lived on a small piece of ground, which they rented from a farmer. One of them had two sons, and the other had one. By and by it was time for the wife that had two sons to send them away to seek their fortune. So one day she told her older son to take a can and bring her water from the well, so that she might bake a cake for him. However much or however little water he might bring, the cake would be big or small accordingly. And that cake was to be all that she could give him when he went on his travels.

The lad went away with the can to the well, and filled it with water, and then came away home again. But the can being broken, most of the water had run out before he got back. So his cake was very small. Yet small as it was, his mother asked if he was willing to take half of it with her blessing. She told him that, if he chose instead to have the whole, he would only get it with her curse. The young man thought he might have to travel a far way, and did not know when or how he might get other provisions. So he said he would like to have the whole cake, come of his mother's curse what like. So she gave him the whole cake, and her curse along with it. Then he took his brother aside, and gave him a knife to keep until he should come back. He asked him to look at it every morning. As long as it continued to be clear, then he might be sure that the owner of it was well. But if it grew dim and rusty, then for certain some ill had befallen him.

So the young man set out to seek his fortune. And he went all that day, and all the next day. And on the third day, in the afternoon, he came up to where a shepherd was sitting with a flock of sheep. And he went up to the shepherd and asked him who the sheep belonged to. And the man answered:

"The Red Etin of Ireland Once lived in Bellygan, And stole King Malcolm's daughter, The King of fair Scotland. He beats her, he binds her, He lays her on a band; And every day he dings her With a bright silver wand Like Julian the Roman He's one that fears no man. It's said there's one predestinate To be his mortal foe; But that man is yet unborn And long may it be so."

The young man then went on his journey. And he had not gone far when he saw an old man with white locks herding a flock of swine. And he went up to him and asked whose swine these were, when the man answered:

"The Red Etin of Ireland"-- (Repeat the verses above.)

Then the young man went on a bit farther. He came to another very old man watching goats. When he asked whose goats they were, the answer was:

"The Red Etin of Ireland"-- (Repeat the verses again.)

This old man also told him to be careful of the next animals that he would meet. They were of a very different kind from any he had yet seen.

So the young man went on. After a while he saw a great number of very terrible animals. Each one of them had two heads, and on every head four horns. And he was very frightened, and ran away from them as fast as he could. He was glad when he came to a castle that stood on a small hill, with the door standing wide to the wall. And he went into the castle for shelter. There he saw an old woman sitting beside the kitchen fire. He asked the woman if he might stay there for the night, as he was tired from a long journey. The woman said he might, but it was not a good place for him to be in. It belonged to the Red Etin, who was a very terrible beast with three heads. He did not spare any living man he could catch. The young man would have gone away, but he was afraid of the animals outside the castle. So he begged the old woman to hide him as well as she could, and not to tell the Etin that he was there. He thought, if he could get through the night, he might get away in the morning without meeting the animals, and so escape. But he had not been long in his hiding place before the awful Etin came in. And as soon as he was in, he was heard crying:

"Snouk but and snouk ben, I find the smell of an earthly man; Be he living, or be he dead, His heart this night shall kitchen(1) my bread."

(1) "Kitchen," that is, "season."

The monster soon found the poor young man, and pulled him from his hole. And when he had got him out he told him that if he could answer him three questions his life should be spared. The first was: Whether Ireland or Scotland was first lived in? The second was: Whether man was made for woman, or woman for man? The third was: Whether men or animals were made first? The young man was not able to answer one of these questions. The Red Etin took a heavy club and knocked him on the head, and turned him into a pillar of stone.

On the morning after this happened the younger brother took out the knife to look at it. He was grieved to find it all brown with rust. He told his mother that the time was now come for him to go away upon his travels also. So she requested him to take the can to the well for water, that she might bake a cake for him. The can being broken, he brought home as little water as the other had done, and the cake was as little. She asked whether he would have the whole cake with her curse, or the half with her blessing. And, like his brother, he thought it best to have the whole cake, come of the curse what might. So he went away. And everything happened to him that had happened to his brother!

The other widow and her son heard of all that had happened from a fairy. The young man determined that he would also go upon his travels, and see if he could do anything to help his two friends. So his mother gave him a can to go to the well and bring home water, that she might bake him a cake for his journey. And he went. And as he was bringing home the water, a raven above his head cried to him to look, and he would see that the water was running out. And he was a young man of sense. Seeing the water running out, he took some clay and patched up the holes, so that he brought home enough water to bake a large cake. When his mother put it to him to take the half-cake with her blessing, he took it in preference to having the whole with her curse. And yet the half was bigger than what the other young men had got altogether.

So he went away on his journey. And after he had traveled a far way he met an old woman. She asked him if he would give her a bit of his bread. And he said he would gladly do that, and so he gave her a piece of the bread. And for that she gave him a magical wand. She said it might yet be of service to him if he took care to use it rightly. Then the old woman, who was a fairy, told him a great deal that would happen to him, and what he ought to do in all circumstances. And after that she vanished in an instant out of his sight. He went on a great way farther, and then he came up to the old man watching the sheep. And when he asked whose sheep these were, the answer was:

"The Red Etin of Ireland Ance lived in Bellygan, And stole King Malcolm's daughter, The King of fair Scotland. He beats her, he binds her, He lays her on a band; And every day he dings her With a bright silver wand. Like Julian the Roman, He's one that fears no man, But now I fear his end is near, And destiny at hand; And you're to be, I plainly see, The heir of all his land."

(Repeat the same questions to the man attending the pigs and the man attending the goats, with the same answer in each case.)

When he came to the place where the monstrous animals were standing, he did not stop nor run away, but went boldly through among them. One came up roaring with open mouth to eat him. He struck it with his wand, and laid it in an instant dead at his feet. He soon came to the Etin's castle, where he knocked, and was admitted. The old woman that sat by the fire warned him of the terrible Etin, and what had been the fate of the two brothers. But he was not to be stopped. The monster soon came in, saying:

"Snouk but and snouk ben, I find the smell of an earthly man; Be he living, or be he dead, His heart shall be kitchen to my bread."

He quickly saw the young man and told him to come out onto the floor. Then he asked him the three questions. But the good fairy had told the young man everything, so he was able to answer all the questions. When the Etin found this, he knew that his power was gone. The young man then picked up the axe and cut off the monster’s three heads. Next he asked the old woman to show him where the King’s daughters were. The old woman took him upstairs and opened many doors. Out of every door came a beautiful lady who had been kept there by the Etin. One of the ladies was the King’s daughter. She also took him down into a low room. There stood two stone pillars. He only had to touch them with his wand, and his two friends and neighbors came to life. All the prisoners were very happy at their freedom. They all said they owed it to the careful young man. The next day they all set out for the King’s Court, and they made a fine company. The King married his daughter to the young man who had saved her. He gave a noble’s daughter to each of the other young men. And so they all lived happily for the rest of their days.
