The Marvelous Land of Oz

by L. Frank Baum

Author’s Note

After “The Wonderful Wizard of OZ” was published, I began to get letters from children. They told me they enjoyed reading the story. They asked me to “write something more” about the Scarecrow and the Tin Woodman. At first I thought these little letters were just nice praise, though they were honest and serious. But the letters kept coming during the months that followed, and even for years.

Finally I made a promise to one little girl. She traveled a long way to see me and make her request—and she is a “Dorothy,” by the way. I promised her that when a thousand little girls had written me a thousand little letters asking for the Scarecrow and the Tin Woodman, I would write the book. Either little Dorothy was a fairy in another form, and waved her magic stick, or the success of the stage show of “The Wizard of OZ” made new friends for the story. For the thousand letters reached their goal long ago—and many more followed them.

And now, although I admit the long delay, I have kept my promise in this book.

L. FRANK BAUM.

Chicago, June, 1904

To those excellent good fellows and funny performers David C. Montgomery and Frank A. Stone, whose smart acting as the Tin Woodman and the Scarecrow has pleased thousands of children throughout the land, this book is thankfully dedicated by THE AUTHOR

Tip Makes a Pumpkinhead

In the Country of the Gillikins, which is at the North of the Land of Oz, lived a youth called Tip. There was more to his name than that. Old Mombi often said his whole name was Tippetarius. But no one was expected to say such a long word when “Tip” would do just as well.

This boy remembered nothing of his parents. He had been brought when quite young to be raised by the old woman known as Mombi. I am sorry to say her reputation was none of the best. The Gillikin people had reason to suspect her of using magical arts. So they hesitated to spend time with her.

[Illustration: image016]

Mombi was not exactly a Witch. The Good Witch who ruled that part of the Land of Oz had forbidden any other Witch to exist in her lands. So Tip’s guardian, however much she might wish to work magic, realized it was against the law to be more than a Sorceress, or at most a Wizardess.

Tip was made to carry wood from the forest so the old woman could boil her pot. He also worked in the corn-fields, digging and removing the outer coverings. And he fed the pigs and milked the four-horned cow that was Mombi’s special pride.

But you must not suppose he worked all the time. He felt that would be bad for him. When sent to the forest, Tip often climbed trees for birds’ eggs. He also had fun chasing the fast white rabbits or fishing in the small streams with bent pins. Then he would quickly gather his load of wood and carry it home. And when he was supposed to be working in the corn-fields, and the tall plants hid him from Mombi’s view, Tip would often dig in the gopher holes. Or if the mood took him, he would lie upon his back between the rows of corn and take a short sleep. So, by taking care not to use up his strength, he grew as strong and tough as a boy may be.

Mombi’s strange magic often scared her neighbors. They treated her in a shy way, yet with respect, because of her weird powers. But Tip openly hated her, and took no pains to hide his feelings. Indeed, he sometimes showed less respect for the old woman than he should have done, considering she was his guardian.

There were pumpkins in Mombi’s corn-fields, lying golden red among the rows of green plants. These had been planted and carefully tended so the four-horned cow might eat them in the winter time. But one day, after the corn had all been cut and stacked, and Tip was carrying the pumpkins to the stable, he took a notion to make a “Jack Lantern” and try to give the old woman a scare with it.

[Illustration: image017]

So he selected a fine, big pumpkin—one with a bright, orange-red color—and began carving it. With the point of his knife he made two round eyes, a three-cornered nose, and a mouth shaped like a new moon. The face, when completed, could not have been considered strictly beautiful. But it wore a smile so big and broad, and was so happy in expression, that even Tip laughed as he looked admiringly at his work.

The child had no playmates, so he did not know that boys often dig out the inside of a “pumpkin-jack.” They put a lighted candle in the space made this way to make the face more surprising. But he thought of his own idea that promised to be just as effective. He decided to make the form of a man, who would wear this pumpkin head, and to stand it in a place where old Mombi would meet it face to face.

“And then,” said Tip to himself, with a laugh, “she’ll squeal louder than the brown pig does when I pull her tail, and shake with fear worse than I did last year when I had the ague!”

He had plenty of time to accomplish this task. Mombi had gone to a village—to buy groceries, she said—and it was a journey of at least two days.

[Illustration: image019]

So he took his axe to the forest, and selected some strong, straight young trees. He cut them down and trimmed off all their twigs and leaves. From these he would make the arms, and legs, and feet of his man. For the body he stripped a sheet of thick bark from around a big tree. With much labor he fashioned it into a cylinder of about the right size, pinning the edges together with wooden pegs. Then, whistling happily as he worked, he carefully joined the limbs and fastened them to the body with pegs whittled into shape with his knife.

By the time this feat had been accomplished it began to grow dark. Tip remembered he must milk the cow and feed the pigs. So he picked up his wooden man and carried it back to the house with him.

During the evening, Tip worked by the light of the fire in the kitchen. He carefully rounded all the edges of the joints. He smoothed the rough places in a clean and skilled way. Then he stood the figure up against the wall and admired it. It seemed very tall, even for a full-grown man. But that was a good point in a small boy's eyes. Tip did not object at all to the size of his creation.

Next morning, Tip looked at his work again. He saw he had forgotten to give the figure a neck. The neck would let him attach the pumpkinhead to the body. So he went again to the forest, which was not far away. He chopped several pieces of wood from a tree to complete his work. When he returned, he attached a cross-piece to the upper end of the body. He made a hole through the center to hold the neck upright. The bit of wood that formed this neck was also made sharp at the upper end. When all was ready, Tip put on the pumpkin head. He pressed it well down onto the neck and found that it fit very well. The head could be turned to one side or the other, as he pleased. The joints of the arms and legs allowed him to place the figure in any position he wanted.

"Now, that," declared Tip, proudly, "is really a very fine man. It ought to scare several screams out of old Mombi! But it would be much more real-looking if it were properly dressed."

To find clothing seemed no easy task. But Tip boldly searched the great chest in which Mombi kept all her keepsakes and treasures. At the very bottom he discovered some purple trousers, a red shirt and a pink vest dotted with white spots. He carried these away to his man. He succeeded in dressing the creature in a stylish fashion, although the clothes did not fit very well. Some knit stockings belonging to Mombi and a much worn pair of his own shoes completed the man's clothing. Tip was so delighted that he danced up and down and laughed aloud in boyish joy.

"I must give him a name!" he cried. "So good a man as this must surely have a name. I believe," he added, after a moment's thought, "I will name the fellow 'Jack Pumpkinhead!'"

The Marvelous Powder of Life

After considering the matter carefully, Tip decided that the best place to put Jack would be at the bend in the road, a little way from the house. So he started to carry his man there. But he found him heavy and rather hard to handle. After dragging the creature a short distance, Tip stood him on his feet. He bent the joints of one leg, and then those of the other, at the same time pushing from behind. In this way the boy managed to make Jack walk to the bend in the road. It was not done without a few falls. Tip really worked harder than he ever had in the fields or forest. But a love of mischief pushed him on. It pleased him to test the cleverness of his work.

"Jack's all right, and works fine!" he said to himself, panting with the unusual effort. But just then he discovered the man's left arm had fallen off in the journey. So he went back to find it. Afterward, he cut a new and stronger pin for the shoulder-joint. He repaired the injury so successfully that the arm was stronger than before. Tip also noticed that Jack's pumpkin head had twisted around until it faced his back. But this was easily fixed. At last, the man was set up facing the turn in the path where old Mombi was to appear. He looked natural enough to be a fair copy of a Gillikin farmer. And he looked unnatural enough to surprise anyone that came on him unawares.

It was yet too early in the day to expect the old woman to return home. So Tip went down into the valley below the farm-house. He began to gather nuts from the trees that grew there.

However, old Mombi returned earlier than usual. She had met a crooked wizard who lived in a lonely cave in the mountains. She had traded several important secrets of magic with him. Having in this way secured three new recipes, four magical powders and a selection of herbs of wonderful power and strength, she hurried home as fast as she could. She wanted to test her new sorceries.

Mombi was so intent on the treasures she had gained that she turned the bend in the road and caught a glimpse of the man. She merely nodded and said:

"Good evening, sir."

But a moment after, she noted that the person did not move or reply. She cast a sharp glance into his face. She discovered his pumpkin head elaborately carved by Tip's jack-knife.

"Heh!" ejaculated Mombi, giving a sort of grunt. "That rascally boy has been playing tricks again! Very good! ve—ry _good!_ I'll beat him black-and-blue for trying to scare me in this fashion!"

Angrily she raised her stick to smash in the grinning pumpkin head of the figure. But a sudden thought made her pause. The uplifted stick left motionless in the air.

"Why, here is a good chance to try my new powder!" said she, eagerly. "And then I can tell whether that crooked wizard has fairly traded secrets, or whether he has fooled me as wickedly as I fooled him."

So she set down her basket and began fumbling in it for one of the precious powders she had obtained.

While Mombi was thus occupied, Tip strolled back with his pockets full of nuts. He discovered the old woman standing beside his man. She was apparently not the least bit scared by it.

At first he was generally disappointed. But the next moment he became curious to know what Mombi was going to do. So he hid behind a hedge, where he could see without being seen. He prepared to watch.

After some search the woman drew from her basket an old pepper-box. On its faded label the wizard had written with a lead-pencil:

"Powder of Life."

"Ah—here it is!" she cried, joyfully. "And now let us see if it is strong. The stingy wizard didn't give me much of it, but I guess there's enough for two or three doses."

[Illustration: image029]

Tip was much surprised when he overheard this speech. Then he saw old Mombi raise her arm. She sprinkled the powder from the box over the pumpkin head of his man Jack. She did this in the same way one would pepper a baked potato. The powder sifted down from Jack's head and scattered over the red shirt and pink waistcoat and purple trousers Tip had dressed him in. A portion even fell upon the patched and worn shoes.

Then she put the pepper-box back into the basket. Mombi lifted her left hand with its little finger pointed up. She said:

"Weaugh!"

Then she lifted her right hand with the thumb pointed up. She said:

"Teaugh!"

Then she lifted both hands with all the fingers and thumbs spread out. She cried:

"Peaugh!"

Jack Pumpkinhead stepped back a pace at this. He said in a critical voice:

"Do not yell like that! Do you think I am unable to hear?"

Old Mombi danced around him. She was wild with joy.

"He lives!" she screamed. "He lives! he lives!"

Then she threw her stick into the air and caught it as it came down. She hugged herself with both arms. She tried to do a step of a lively dance. All the time she repeated, with great joy:

"He lives!—he lives!—he lives!"

Now you may well suppose that Tip watched all this with great surprise.

At first he was so afraid and shocked that he wanted to run away. But his legs shook so badly that he was not able to. Then it seemed to him a very funny thing for Jack to come to life. The expression on his pumpkin face was so strange and funny it caused laughter at once. So Tip got over his first fear and began to laugh. The happy sounds reached old Mombi's ears. They made her move quickly with short steps to the row of bushes. There she seized Tip's neck band and pulled him back to where she had left her basket and the pumpkinheaded man.

"You bad, secret, evil boy!" she cried, angrily. "I'll teach you to watch my secrets and to make fun of me!"

"I was not making fun of you," said Tip. "I was laughing at old Pumpkinhead! Look at him! Is he not a funny sight, though?"

"I hope you are not talking about my personal appearance," said Jack. It was so funny to hear his serious voice while his face continued to wear its happy smile. Tip again burst into a loud laugh.

Even Mombi was not without a curious interest in the man her magic had brought to life. She stared at him closely. Then she soon asked:

"What do you know?"

"Well, that is hard to tell," replied Jack. "For although I feel that I know a huge amount, I am not yet sure how much there is in the world to find out about. It will take me a little time to discover whether I am very wise or very stupid."

"To be sure," said Mombi, thoughtfully.

"But what are you going to do with him, now he is alive?" asked Tip, wondering.

"I must think it over," answered Mombi. "But we must get home at once, for it is growing dark. Help the Pumpkinhead to walk."

"Never mind me," said Jack. "I can walk as well as you can. Do I not have legs and feet, and are they not jointed?"

"Are they?" asked the woman, turning to Tip.

"Of course they are; I made them myself," returned the boy, with pride.

So they started for the house. When they reached the farm yard old Mombi led the pumpkin man to the cow stable. She shut him up in an empty stall and fastened the door securely on the outside.

"I've got to attend to you, first," she said, nodding her head at Tip.

Hearing this, the boy became uneasy. He knew Mombi had a bad and revengeful heart. She would not wait to do any evil thing.

They entered the house. It was a round, domeshaped structure, as are nearly all the farm houses in the Land of Oz.

Mombi told the boy to light a candle. She put her basket in a cupboard and hung her cloak on a peg. Tip obeyed quickly, for he was afraid of her.

After the candle had been lighted Mombi ordered him to build a fire in the hearth. While Tip was busy with this the old woman ate her supper. When the flames began to crackle the boy came to her and asked for a share of the bread and cheese. But Mombi refused him.

"I'm hungry!" said Tip, in a sour tone.

"You will not be hungry long," replied Mombi, with a grim look.

The boy did not like this speech, for it sounded like a threat. But he happened to remember he had nuts in his pocket. So he cracked some of those and ate them. The woman rose, shook the crumbs from her apron, and hung above the fire a small black pot.

Then she measured out equal parts of milk and vinegar and poured them into the pot. Next she produced several packets of herbs and powders. She began adding a portion of each to the contents of the pot. Sometimes she would draw near the candle and read from a yellow paper the recipe of the mix she was making.

As Tip watched her his uneasiness increased.

"What is that for?" he asked.

"For you," returned Mombi, briefly.

Tip moved around on his stool and stared awhile at the pot, which was beginning to bubble. Then he would glance at the stern and wrinkled features of the witch. He wished he were any place but in that dim and smoky kitchen. Even the shadows cast by the candle upon the wall were enough to give one the horrors. So an hour passed away. During that time the silence was only broken by the bubbling of the pot and the hissing of the flames.

Finally, Tip spoke again.

"Have I got to drink that stuff?" he asked, nodding toward the pot.

"Yes," said Mombi.

"What'll it do to me?" asked Tip.

"If it's properly made," replied Mombi, "it will change or transform you into a stone statue."

Tip made a low sound of pain. He wiped the sweat from his forehead with his sleeve.

"I do not want to be a stone statue!" he protested.

"That does not matter I want you to be one," said the old woman, looking at him severely.

"What use'll I be then?" asked Tip. "There will not be any one to work for you."

"I'll make the Pumpkinhead work for me," said Mombi.

Again Tip made a low sound of pain.

"Why do you not change me into a goat, or a chicken?" he asked, worriedly. "You can't do anything with a stone statue."

"Oh, yes, I can," returned Mombi. "I'm going to plant a flower garden, next Spring. I'll put you in the middle of it, for an ornament. I wonder I have not thought of that before. You've been a bother to me for years."

At this terrible speech Tip felt the drops of sweat starting all over his body. But he sat still and shook. He looked worriedly at the pot.

"Perhaps it will not work," he said in a low voice. He sounded weak and without hope.

"Oh, I think it will," answered Mombi in a happy voice. "I almost never make a mistake."

Again there was a long silence. It was so long and dark that when Mombi finally lifted the pot from the fire, it was almost midnight.

"You cannot drink it until it is quite cold," said the old woman. She had admitted that she used magic, even though the law said she must not. "We must both go to bed now. At first light I will call you. Then I will finish changing you into a stone statue."

With this she walked slowly and with difficulty into her room. She carried the hot pot with her. Tip heard her close the door and lock it.

The boy did not go to bed as he had been told to do. He still sat and looked angrily at the last red pieces of the dying fire.

The Flight of the Fugitives

Tip thought about it.

"It is a hard thing, to be a stone statue," he thought, in a angry way. "And I am not going to accept it. For years I have been a problem to her, she says. So she is going to get rid of me. Well, there is an easier way than to become a statue. No boy could have any fun forever standing in the middle of a flower garden! I will run away. That is what I will do. And I may as well go before she makes me drink that bad stuff in the pot." He waited until the old woman's loud sleeping breaths showed she was fast asleep. Then he got up softly and went to the food cabinet to find something to eat.

"There is no use starting on a journey without food," he decided. He looked on the narrow shelves.

He found some hard pieces of bread. But he had to look in Mombi's basket to find the cheese she had brought from the village. While he was turning over the things in the basket, he found the pepper box that held the "Powder of Life."

"I may as well take this with me," he thought. "Or Mombi will use it to make more trouble." So he put the box in his pocket, together with the bread and cheese.

Then he left the house carefully and closed the door behind him. Outside, the moon and stars shone brightly. The night seemed peaceful and welcoming after the closed, bad-smelling kitchen.

"I will be glad to get away," said Tip softly. "For I never did like that old woman. I wonder how I ever came to live with her."

He was walking slowly toward the road when a thought made him stop.

"I do not like to leave Jack Pumpkinhead to the kind care of old Mombi," he said in a low voice. "And Jack belongs to me. I made him, even if the old woman did bring him to life."

He went back to the cow stable and opened the door of the small space where the pumpkin-headed man had been left.

Jack was standing in the middle of the space. In the moonlight Tip could see he was smiling as happily as ever.

"Come on!" said the boy, and moved his hand to call him.

"Where to?" asked Jack.

"You will know as soon as I do," answered Tip. He smiled in a kind way into the pumpkin face.

"All we have to do now is walk."

"Very well," returned Jack. He walked awkwardly out of the stable and into the moonlight.

Tip turned toward the road and the man followed him. Jack walked with a kind of uneven step. Sometimes one of the joints of his legs would turn backward instead of forward. This almost made him fall. But the Pumpkinhead was quick to notice this. He began to take more care to step carefully. So he had few accidents.

[Illustration: image041]

Tip led him along the path without stopping for a moment. They could not go very fast, but they walked steadily. By the time the moon went down and the sun looked over the hills, they had travelled so far that the boy had no reason to fear that the old woman would follow him. Also, he had turned first into one path, and then into another. So if anyone followed them, it would be very hard to guess which way they had gone, or where to look for them.

The boy was fairly sure that he had escaped—for a time, at least—being turned into a stone statue. He stopped his companion and sat down on a rock by the road.

"Let us have some breakfast," he said.

Jack Pumpkinhead watched Tip with interest, but refused to join in the meal. "I do not seem to be made the same way you are," he said.

"I know you are not," returned Tip. "For I made you."

"Oh! Did you?" asked Jack.

[Illustration: image043]

"Certainly. And put you together. And cut your eyes and nose and ears and mouth," said Tip proudly. "And dressed you."

Jack looked at his body and arms and legs carefully.

"It seems to me you made a very good job of it," he remarked.

"Only so-so," replied Tip, in a modest way. For he began to see certain faults in the construction of his man. "If I had known we were going to travel together, I might have been a little more careful."

"Why, then," said the Pumpkinhead, in a tone that showed surprise, "you must be my creator, my parent, my father!"

"Or your inventor," replied the boy with a laugh. "Yes, my son. I really believe I am!"

"Then I owe you obedience," continued the man. "And you owe me—support."

"That is it, exactly," declared Tip, jumping up. "So let us be off."

"Where are we going?" asked Jack, when they had started their journey again.

"I am not exactly sure," said the boy. "But I believe we are headed South. That will bring us, sooner or later, to the Emerald City."

"What city is that?" asked the Pumpkinhead.

"Why, it is the center of the Land of Oz, and the biggest town in all the country. I have never been there myself, but I have heard all about its history. It was built by a mighty and wonderful Wizard named Oz. Everything there is of a green color—just as everything in this Country of the Gillikins is of a purple color."

"Is everything here purple?" asked Jack.

"Of course it is. Can you not see?" returned the boy.

"I believe I must be color-blind," said the Pumpkinhead, after staring about him.

"Well, the grass is purple, and the trees are purple, and the houses and fences are purple," explained Tip. "Even the mud in the roads is purple. But in the Emerald City everything is green that is purple here. And in the Country of the Munchkins, over at the East, everything is blue. And in the South country of the Quadlings everything is red. And in the West country of the Winkies, where the Tin Woodman rules, everything is yellow."

"Oh!" said Jack. Then, after a pause, he asked: "Did you say a Tin Woodman rules the Winkies?"

“Yes; he was one of those who helped Dorothy to destroy the Wicked Witch of the West. The Winkies were so grateful that they invited him to become their ruler. The people of the Emerald City invited the Scarecrow to rule them in the same way.”

“Dear me!” said Jack. “I’m getting confused with all this history. Who is the Scarecrow?”

“Another friend of Dorothy’s,” replied Tip.

“And who is Dorothy?”

“She was a girl who came here from Kansas. Kansas is a place in the big, outside World. She was blown to the Land of Oz by a cyclone. While she was here, the Scarecrow and the Tin Woodman went with her on her travels.”

“And where is she now?” asked the Pumpkinhead.

“Glinda the Good, who rules the Quadlings, sent her home again,” said the boy.

“Oh. And what happened to the Scarecrow?”

“I told you. He rules the Emerald City,” answered Tip.

“I thought you said it was ruled by a wonderful Wizard,” objected Jack. He seemed more and more confused.

“Well, so I did. Now, pay attention, and I’ll explain it,” said Tip. He spoke slowly and looked the smiling Pumpkinhead right in the eye. “Dorothy went to the Emerald City to ask the Wizard to send her back to Kansas. The Scarecrow and the Tin Woodman went with her. But the Wizard could not send her back, because he was not as much of a Wizard as he might have been. Then they got angry at the Wizard and threatened to expose him. So the Wizard made a big balloon and escaped in it. No one has ever seen him since.”

“Now, that is very interesting history,” said Jack, well pleased. “And I understand it perfectly all but the explanation.”

“I’m glad you do,” responded Tip. “After the Wizard was gone, the people of the Emerald City made His Majesty, the Scarecrow, their King. I have heard that he became a very popular ruler.”

“Are we going to see this strange King?” asked Jack, with interest.

“I think we may as well,” replied the boy. “Unless you have something better to do.”

“Oh, no, dear father,” said the Pumpkinhead. “I am quite willing to go wherever you please.”

Tip Makes an Experiment in Magic

The boy was small and rather delicate in appearance. He seemed somewhat embarrassed at being called “father” by the tall, awkward, pumpkinheaded man. But to deny the relationship would involve another long and tedious explanation. So he changed the subject by asking, abruptly:

“Are you tired?”

“Of course not!” replied the other. “But,” he continued, after a pause, “it is quite certain I shall wear out my wooden joints if I keep on walking.”

Tip reflected, as they journeyed on, that this was true. He began to regret that he had not constructed the wooden limbs more carefully and substantially. Yet how could he ever have guessed that the man he had made merely to scare old Mombi with would be brought to life? He had been brought to life by means of a magical powder contained in an old pepper-box.

So he ceased to reproach himself. He began to think how he might yet remedy the deficiencies of Jack’s weak joints.

While thus engaged they came to the edge of a wood. The boy sat down to rest upon an old sawhorse that some woodcutter had left there.

“Why don’t you sit down?” he asked the Pumpkinhead.

“Won’t it strain my joints?” asked the other.

“Of course not. It’ll rest them,” declared the boy.

So Jack tried to sit down. But as soon as he bent his joints farther than usual they gave way altogether. He came clattering to the ground with such a crash that Tip feared he was entirely ruined.

[Illustration: image050]

He rushed to the man, lifted him to his feet, straightened his arms and legs, and felt of his head to see if by chance it had become cracked. But Jack seemed to be in pretty good shape, after all. Tip said to him:

“I guess you’d better remain standing, hereafter. It seems the safest way.”

“Very well, dear father. just as you say,” replied the smiling Jack. He had been in no wise confused by his tumble.

Tip sat down again. Presently the Pumpkinhead asked:

“What is that thing you are sitting on?”

“Oh, this is a horse,” replied the boy, carelessly.

“What is a horse?” demanded Jack.

“A horse? Why, there are two kinds of horses,” returned Tip. He was slightly puzzled how to explain. “One kind of horse is alive, and has four legs and a head and a tail. And people ride upon its back.”

“I understand,” said Jack, cheerfully. “That’s the kind of horse you are now sitting on.”

“No, it isn’t,” answered Tip, promptly.

“Why not? That one has four legs, and a head, and a tail.” Tip looked at the saw-horse more carefully, and found that the Pumpkinhead was right. The body had been formed from a tree-trunk. A branch had been left sticking up at one end that looked very much like a tail. In the other end were two big knots that resembled eyes. A place had been chopped away that might easily be mistaken for the horse’s mouth. As for the legs, they were four straight limbs cut from trees and stuck fast into the body. They were spread wide apart so that the saw-horse would stand firmly when a log was laid across it to be sawed.

“This thing resembles a real horse more than I imagined,” said Tip, trying to explain. “But a real horse is alive, and trots and prances and eats oats. This is nothing more than a dead horse, made of wood, and used to saw logs upon.”

“If it were alive, wouldn’t it trot, and prance, and eat oats?” asked the Pumpkinhead.

“It would trot and prance, perhaps; but it wouldn’t eat oats,” replied the boy, laughing at the idea. “And of course it can’t ever be alive, because it is made of wood.”

“So am I,” answered the man.

Tip looked at him in surprise.

“Why, so you are!” he exclaimed. “And the magic powder that brought you to life is here in my pocket.”

[Illustration: image053]

He brought out the pepper box, and eyed it curiously.

“I wonder,” said he, musingly, “if it would bring the saw-horse to life.”

“If it would,” returned Jack, calmly for nothing seemed to surprise him “I could ride on its back, and that would save my joints from wearing out.”

“I’ll try it!” cried the boy, jumping up. “But I wonder if I can remember the words old Mombi said, and the way she held her hands up.”

He thought it over for a minute. He had watched carefully from the hedge every motion of the old witch, and listened to her words. He believed he could repeat exactly what she had said and done.

So he began by sprinkling some of the magic Powder of Life from the pepper-box upon the body of the saw-horse. Then he lifted his left hand, with the little finger pointing upward, and said: “Weaugh!”

“What does that mean, dear father?” asked Jack, with interest.

The Awakening of the Saw-horse

The Saw-Horse found himself alive. He seemed even more surprised than Tip. He rolled his rough eyes from side to side. He took a first wondering look at the world, where he now had such an important existence. Then he tried to look at himself. But he had no neck to turn. “Whoa! Whoa, there!”

The Saw-Horse paid no attention at all to this command. The next instant it brought one of its wooden legs down on Tip’s foot so hard that the boy danced away in pain to a safer distance. From there he yelled again:

“Whoa! Whoa, I say!”

Jack had now managed to raise himself to a sitting position. He looked at the Saw-Horse with much interest.

“I do not believe the animal can hear you,” he said.

“Yes, but the horse has no ears,” said the smiling Pumpkinhead.

It was really funny to see the creature walk. It moved the legs on its right side together, and those on its left side together, as a pacing horse does. That made its body rock from side to side, like a cradle.

Jack Pumpkinhead’s Ride to the Emerald City

At first light Tip was awakened by the Pumpkinhead. He rubbed the sleep from his eyes, washed in a little brook, and then ate a portion of his bread and cheese. After preparing for a new day in this way the boy said:

“Let us start at once. Nine miles is quite a distance, but we ought to reach the Emerald City by noon if no accidents happen.” So the Pumpkinhead was again placed upon the back of the Saw-Horse and the journey was resumed.

Tip noticed that the purple color of the grass and trees had now faded to a dull pale purple. Before long this pale purple appeared to take on a greenish tinge. It gradually brightened as they drew nearer to the great City where the Scarecrow ruled.

The little group had traveled only a short two miles upon their way when the road of yellow brick was parted by a broad and fast river. Tip was puzzled how to cross over. But after a time he discovered a man in a small boat coming from the other side of the stream.

When the man reached the bank Tip asked:

“Will you row us to the other side?”

“Yes, if you have money,” returned the boatman, whose face looked cross and disagreeable.

“But I have no money,” said Tip.

“None at all?” inquired the man.

“None at all,” answered the boy.

“Then I’ll not break my back rowing you over,” said the boatman, firmly.

“What a nice man!” remarked the Pumpkinhead, with a smile.

The boatman stared at him, but made no reply. Tip was trying to think. It was a great disappointment to him to find his journey so suddenly brought to an end.

“I must certainly get to the Emerald City,” he said to the boatman. “But how can I cross the river if you do not take me?”

The man laughed, and it was not a nice laugh.

“That wooden horse will float,” said he. “And you can ride him across. As for the pumpkinheaded fool who comes with you, let him sink or swim. It will not matter greatly which.”

“Do not worry about me,” said Jack, smiling pleasantly at the bad-tempered boatman. “I am sure I ought to float beautifully.”

Tip thought the experiment was worth making. The Saw-Horse, who did not know what danger meant, offered no objections at all. So the boy led it down into the water and climbed upon its back. Jack also walked in up to his knees and held the tail of the horse so that he might keep his pumpkin head above the water.

“Now,” said Tip, teaching the Saw-Horse, “if you move your legs from side to side you will probably swim. And if you swim we shall probably reach the other side.”

The Saw-Horse at once began to move its legs from side to side. The legs acted as oars and moved the travelers slowly across the river to the opposite side. The trip was so successful that soon they were climbing, wet and dripping, up the grass-covered bank.

Tip’s trouser-legs and shoes were thoroughly soaked. But the Saw-Horse had floated so perfectly that from his knees up the boy was entirely dry. As for the Pumpkinhead, every stitch of his gorgeous clothing dripped water.

“I did not mind swimming at all,” said the horse.

“I’ll do my best,” answered the Saw-Horse, happily.

His Majesty the Scarecrow

I suppose every reader of this book knows what a scarecrow is. But Jack Pumpkinhead had never seen such a creation. He was more surprised at meeting the remarkable King of the Emerald City than by any other one experience of his short life.

His Majesty the Scarecrow was dressed in a suit of faded blue clothes. His head was merely a small bag stuffed with straw. On it, eyes, ears, a nose and a mouth had been roughly painted to represent a face. The clothes were also stuffed with straw. That was done so unevenly or carelessly that his Majesty’s legs and arms seemed more bumpy than was necessary. Upon his hands were gloves with long fingers, and these were padded with cotton. Bits of straw stuck out from the monarch’s coat and also from his neck and boot-tops. Upon his head he wore a heavy golden crown set thick with shining jewels. In all else the Scarecrow King was but a simple scarecrow—thin, awkward, and without substance.

"There!" said she. "It is time for me to go. Carry that basket for me and help yourself to its contents if you are hungry."

Tip seized the basket eagerly and began to eat, following for a time the strange girl without bothering to ask questions. She walked along before him with fast strides, and there was about her an air of decision and importance that led him to suspect she was some great personage.

Finally, when he had satisfied his hunger, he ran up beside her and tried to keep pace with her fast footsteps—a very difficult feat, for she was much taller than he, and evidently in a hurry.

"Thank you very much for the sandwiches," said Tip, as he trotted along. "May I ask your name?"

"I am General Jinjur," was the brief reply.

"Oh!" said the boy surprised. "What sort of a General?"

"I command the Army of Revolt in this war," answered the General, with unnecessary sharpness.

"Oh!" he again cried. "I did not know there was a war."

"You were not supposed to know it," she returned, "for we have kept it a secret. And considering that our army is composed entirely of girls," she added, with some pride, "it is surely a remarkable thing that our Revolt is not yet discovered."

"It is, indeed," agreed Tip. "But where is your army?"

“About a mile from here,” said General Jinjur. “The forces have gathered from all parts of the Land of Oz, at my direct command. For this is the day we will defeat His Majesty the Scarecrow. We will take the throne from him. The Army of Revolt only waits for me to come. Then we will march on the Emerald City.”

“Well!” said Tip. He took a long breath. “This is certainly a surprising thing! May I ask why you want to defeat His Majesty the Scarecrow?”

“For one reason, men have ruled the Emerald City long enough,” said the girl.

“Also, the City shines with beautiful stones. They could be used much better for rings, bracelets and necklaces. And there is enough money in the King’s treasury to buy every girl in our Army a dozen new dresses. So we plan to take the City and run the government to suit ourselves.”

Jinjur spoke these words with an eagerness and decision that showed she was serious.

“But war is a terrible thing,” said Tip, thoughtfully.

“This war will be pleasant,” replied the girl, cheerfully.

“Many of you will be killed!” continued the boy, in a voice full of awe.

“Oh, no,” said Jinjur. “What man would oppose a girl, or dare to harm her? And there is not an ugly face in my entire Army.”

Tip laughed.

“Perhaps you are right,” he said. “But the Guardian of the Gate is considered a faithful Guardian. The King’s Army will not let the City be taken without a fight.”

“The Army is old and weak,” replied General Jinjur, scornfully. “His strength has all been used to grow whiskers. And his wife has such a temper that she has already pulled more than half of them out by the roots. When the Wonderful Wizard ruled, the Soldier with the Green Whiskers was a very good Royal Army. People feared the Wizard then. But no one is afraid of the Scarecrow. So his Royal Army does not count for much in time of war.”

After this conversation they went some distance in silence. Before long they reached a large open space in the forest. There, fully four hundred young women were gathered. They were laughing and talking together as gaily as if they had gathered for a picnic instead of a war of conquest.

They were divided into four companies. Tip noticed that all were dressed in costumes similar to the one worn by General Jinjur. The only real difference was the strip on the front of their skirts. The girls from the Munchkin country had a blue strip. Those from the country of the Quadlings had a red strip. Those from the country of the Winkies had a yellow strip. And the Gillikin girls wore a purple strip. All had green waists, representing the Emerald City they intended to take. The top button on each waist showed by its color which country the wearer came from. The uniforms were jaunty and becoming. They looked quite effective when gathered together.

Tip thought this strange Army carried no weapons at all. But in this he was wrong. For each girl had stuck two long, shining knitting-needles through the knot of her back hair.

General Jinjur immediately climbed onto the stump of a tree and spoke to her army.

“Friends, fellow-citizens, and girls!” she said. “We are about to begin our great Revolt against the men of Oz! We march to take the Emerald City—to dethrone the Scarecrow King—to get thousands of gorgeous gems—to rob the royal treasury—and to obtain power over our former oppressors!”

“Hurrah!” said those who had listened. But Tip thought most of the Army was too busy chattering to pay attention to the words of the General.

The command to march was now given. The girls formed themselves into four bands, or companies. They set off with eager strides toward the Emerald City.

The boy followed after them. He carried several baskets and wraps and packages which various members of the Army of Revolt had placed in his care. It was not long before they came to the green granite walls of the City. They halted before the gateway.

The Guardian of the Gate at once came out and looked at them curiously, as if a circus had come to town. He carried a bunch of keys swung round his neck by a golden chain. His hands were thrust carelessly into his pockets. He seemed to have no idea at all that the City was threatened by rebels. Speaking pleasantly to the girls, he said:

“Good morning, my dears! What can I do for you?”

“Surrender instantly!” answered General Jinjur. She stood before him and frowned as terribly as her pretty face would allow her to.

“Surrender!” echoed the man, astounded. “Why, it’s impossible. It’s against the law! I never heard of such a thing in my life.”

“Still, you must surrender!” exclaimed the General, fiercely. “We are revolting!”

“You don’t look it,” said the Guardian, gazing from one to another, admiringly.

“But we are!” cried Jinjur, stamping her foot, impatiently. “And we mean to take the Emerald City!”

“Good gracious!” returned the surprised Guardian of the Gates. “What a nonsensical idea! Go home to your mothers, my good girls, and milk the cows and bake the bread. Don’t you know it’s a dangerous thing to take a city?”

“We are not afraid!” responded the General. And she looked so determined that it made the Guardian uneasy.

So he rang the bell for the Soldier with the Green Whiskers. The next minute he was sorry he had done so. For immediately he was surrounded by a crowd of girls. They drew the knitting-needles from their hair and began jabbing them at the Guardian. The sharp points came dangerously near his fat cheeks and blinking eyes.

The poor man howled loudly for mercy. He made no resistance when Jinjur drew the bunch of keys from around his neck.

Followed by her Army, the General now rushed to the gateway. There she was confronted by the Royal Army of Oz—which was the other name for the Soldier with the Green Whiskers.

“Halt!” he cried, and pointed his long gun full in the face of the leader.

Some of the girls screamed and ran back. But General Jinjur bravely stood her ground and said, reproachfully:

“Why, how now? Would you shoot a poor, defenceless girl?”

“No,” replied the soldier. “For my gun isn’t loaded.”

“Not loaded?”

“No; for fear of accidents. And I’ve forgotten where I hid the powder and shot to load it with. But if you’ll wait a short time I’ll try to hunt them up.”

“Don’t trouble yourself,” said Jinjur, cheerfully. Then she turned to her Army and cried:

“Girls, the gun isn’t loaded!”

“Hooray,” shrieked the rebels, delighted at this good news. And they rushed upon the Soldier with the Green Whiskers in such a crowd that it was a wonder they didn’t stick the knitting-needles into one another.

But the Royal Army of Oz was too afraid of women to face the attack. He simply turned around and ran as fast as he could through the gate and toward the royal palace. General Jinjur and her crowd rushed into the city. No one was there to protect it.

In this way the Emerald City was taken without a drop of blood being spilled. The Army of Revolt had become an Army of Winners!

The Scarecrow Plans an escape

Tip slipped away from the girls. He followed quickly after the Soldier with the Green Whiskers. The entering army came into the City more slowly. They stopped to dig emeralds out of the walls and stones with the points of their knitting needles. So the Soldier and the boy reached the palace before the news had spread that the City was taken.

The Scarecrow and Jack Pumpkinhead were still playing quoits in the courtyard when the game was interrupted. The Royal Army of Oz came in suddenly. He came flying in without his hat or gun. His clothes were in sad disorder. His long beard floated a yard behind him as he ran.

“One point for me,” said the Scarecrow, calmly. “What’s wrong, my man?” he added, speaking to the Soldier.

“Oh! your Majesty—your Majesty! The City is taken!” gasped the Royal Army, who was all out of breath.

“This is quite sudden,” said the Scarecrow. “But please go and bar all the doors and windows of the palace, while I show this Pumpkinhead how to throw a quoit.”

The Soldier hurried to do this. Tip had arrived at his heels. He stayed in the courtyard to look at the Scarecrow with wondering eyes.

His Majesty continued to throw the quoits as coolly as if no danger threatened his throne. But the Pumpkinhead had caught sight of Tip. He walked toward the boy as fast as his wooden legs would go.

“Good afternoon, noble parent!” he cried, happily. “I’m glad to see you are here. That terrible Saw-Horse ran away with me.”

“I suspected it,” said Tip. “Did you get hurt? Are you cracked at all?”

“No, I arrived safely,” answered Jack, “and his Majesty has been very kind indeed to me.”

At this moment the Soldier with the Green Whiskers returned, and the Scarecrow asked:

“By the way, who has beaten me?”

“A regiment of girls, gathered from the four corners of the Land of Oz,” replied the Soldier, still pale with fear.

“But where was my Standing Army at the time?” asked his Majesty, looking at the Soldier, seriously.

“Your Standing Army was running,” answered the fellow, honestly; “for no man could face the terrible weapons of the invaders.”

“Well,” said the Scarecrow, after a moment’s thought, “I don’t mind much the loss of my throne, for it’s a tiresome job to rule over the Emerald City. And this crown is so heavy that it makes my head ache. But I hope the Winners have no plan of hurting me, just because I happen to be the King.”

“I heard them say” remarked Tip, with some hesitation, “that they plan to make a rag carpet of your outside and stuff their sofa cushions with your inside.”

“Then I am really in danger,” declared his Majesty, firmly, “and it will be wise for me to consider a means to escape.”

“Where can you go?” asked Jack Pumpkinhead.

“Why, to my friend the Tin Woodman, who rules over the Winkies, and calls himself their Emperor,” was the answer. “I am sure he will protect me.”

Tip was looking out the window.

“The palace is surrounded by the enemy,” said he. “It is too late to escape. They would soon tear you to pieces.”

The Scarecrow sighed.

“In an emergency,” he announced, “it is always a good thing to pause and reflect. Please excuse me while I pause and reflect.”

“But we also are in danger,” said the Pumpkinhead, anxiously. “If any of these girls understand cooking, my end is not far off!”

“Nonsense!” exclaimed the Scarecrow. “they’re too busy to cook, even if they know how!”

“But should I remain here a prisoner for any length of time,” protested Jack, “I’m liable to spoil.”

“Ah! then you would not be fit to associate with,” returned the Scarecrow. “The matter is more serious than I suspected.”

“You,” said the Pumpkinhead, gloomily, “are liable to live for many years. My life is necessarily short. So I must take advantage of the few days that remain to me.”

“There, there! Don’t worry,” answered the Scarecrow soothingly; “if you’ll keep quiet long enough for me to think, I’ll try to find some way for us all to escape.”

So the others waited in patient silence while the Scarecrow walked to a corner and stood with his face to the wall for a good five minutes. At the end of that time he faced them with a more cheerful expression upon his painted face.

“Where is the Saw-Horse you rode here?” he asked the Pumpkinhead.

“Why, I said he was a jewel, and so your man locked him up in the royal treasury,” said Jack.

“It was the only place I could think of your Majesty,” added the Soldier, fearing he had made a mistake.

“It pleases me very much,” said the Scarecrow. “Has the animal been fed?”

“Oh, yes; I gave him a heaping peck of sawdust.”

“Excellent!” cried the Scarecrow. “Bring the horse here at once.”

The Soldier hurried away, and presently they heard the clattering of the horse’s wooden legs upon the pavement as he was led into the courtyard.

His Majesty regarded the steed critically. “He doesn’t seem especially graceful!” he remarked, musingly. “but I suppose he can run?”

“He can, indeed,” said Tip, gazing upon the Saw-Horse admiringly.

“Then, bearing us upon his back, he must make a dash through the ranks of the rebels and carry us to my friend the Tin Woodman,” announced the Scarecrow.

“He can’t carry four!” objected Tip.

“No, but he may be induced to carry three,” said his Majesty. “I shall therefore leave my Royal Army Behind. For, from the ease with which he was beaten, I have little confidence in his powers.”

“Still, he can run,” declared Tip, laughing.

“I expected this blow” said the Soldier, sulkily; “but I can bear it. I shall disguise myself by cutting off my lovely green whiskers. And, after all, it is no more dangerous to face those reckless girls than to ride this fiery, untamed wooden horse!”

“Perhaps you are right,” observed his Majesty. “But, for my part, not being a soldier, I am fond of danger. Now, my boy, you must mount first. And please sit as close to the horse’s neck as possible.”

Tip climbed quickly to his place. The Soldier and the Scarecrow managed to lift the Pumpkinhead to a seat just behind him. There was so little space left for the King that he was likely to fall off as soon as the horse started.

“Fetch a clothesline,” said the King to his Army. “Tie us all together. Then if one falls off, we will all fall off.”

While the Soldier was gone for the clothesline, his Majesty continued: “It is good for me to be careful. My very existence is in danger.”

“I have to be as careful as you do,” said Jack.

“Not exactly,” replied the Scarecrow. “If anything happened to me, that would be the end of me. But if anything happened to you, they could use you for seed.”

The Soldier now returned with a long line. He tied all three firmly together. He also tied them to the body of the Saw-Horse. So there seemed little danger of them falling off.

“Now throw open the gates,” commanded the Scarecrow. “We will run to liberty or to death.”

The courtyard where they stood was in the center of the great palace. The palace surrounded it on all sides. But in one place a passage led to an outer gateway. The Soldier had barred it by order of his sovereign. His Majesty planned to escape through this gateway. The Royal Army now led the Saw-Horse along the passage and unbarred the gate. It swung backward with a loud crash.

“Now,” said Tip to the horse, “you must save us all. Run as fast as you can for the gate of the City. Do not let anything stop you.”

“All right!” answered the Saw-Horse in a rough voice. He ran away so suddenly that Tip had to gasp for breath. Tip held firmly to the post he had driven into the creature’s neck.

Several of the girls stood outside guarding the palace. The Saw-Horse’s wild rush knocked some of them over. Others ran away screaming. Only one or two pushed their knitting needles wildly at the escaping prisoners. Tip got one small prick in his left arm. It hurt for an hour afterward. But the needles had no effect on the Scarecrow or Jack Pumpkinhead. They never even knew they were being pushed.

As for the Saw-Horse, he did amazing things. He knocked over a fruit cart. He turned over several quiet-looking men. Finally he knocked over the new Guardian of the Gate. She was a fussy little fat woman appointed by General Jinjur.

The wild horse did not stop then. Once outside the walls of the Emerald City, he ran along the road to the West. His fast, violent jumps shook the breath out of the boy and filled the Scarecrow with wonder.

Jack had ridden at this wild speed once before. So he tried his best to hold his pumpkin head on its stick with both hands. He endured the terrible shaking with the courage of a philosopher.

“Slow him up! Slow him up!” shouted the Scarecrow. “My straw is all shaking down into my legs.”

But Tip had no breath to speak. So the Saw-Horse continued his wild run, unchecked and with unabated speed.

Soon they came to the banks of a wide river. Without a pause, the wooden horse gave one final jump and launched them all into the air.

A second later they were rolling, splashing and bouncing about in the water. The horse struggled wildly to find a rest for its feet. Its riders were first plunged beneath the rapid current and then floated on the surface like corks.

The Journey to the Tin Woodman

Tip was well soaked and dripping water from every part of his body. But he managed to lean forward and shout in the ear of the Saw-Horse:

“Keep still, you fool! Keep still!”

The horse at once stopped struggling and floated calmly on the surface. Its wooden body was as buoyant as a raft.

“What does that word ‘fool’ mean?” asked the horse.

“It is a term of reproach,” answered Tip. He was somewhat ashamed of the expression. “I only use it when I am angry.”

“Then it pleases me to be able to call you a fool in return,” said the horse. “I did not make the river, nor put it in our way. So only a term of reproach is fit for one who becomes angry with me for falling into the water.”

“That is quite evident,” replied Tip. “So I will admit I am wrong.” Then he called out to the Pumpkinhead: “Are you all right, Jack?”

There was no reply. So the boy called to the King: “Are you all right, your majesty?”

The Scarecrow groaned.

“I’m all wrong, somehow,” he said in a weak voice. “How very wet this water is!”

Tip was tied so tightly by the cord that he could not turn his head to look at his companions. So he said to the Saw-Horse:

“Paddle with your legs toward the shore.”

The horse obeyed. Their progress was slow, but they finally reached the opposite river bank. It was low enough there for the creature to climb onto dry land.

With some difficulty the boy managed to get his knife out of his pocket. He cut the cords that tied the riders to one another and to the wooden horse. He heard the Scarecrow fall to the ground with a soft wet sound. Then he himself quickly got down and looked at his friend Jack.

The wooden body, with its gorgeous clothing, still sat upright on the horse’s back. But the pumpkin head was gone. Only the sharpened stick that served for a neck was visible. As for the Scarecrow, the straw in his body had shaken down with the shaking. It had packed itself into his legs and the lower part of his body. That part appeared very plump and round, while his upper half seemed like an empty sack. The Scarecrow still wore the heavy crown on his head. It had been sewed on to prevent him losing it. But the head was now so damp and limp that the weight of the gold and jewels pulled it forward. This crushed the painted face into a mass of wrinkles. It made him look exactly like a Japanese pug dog.

Tip would have laughed—if he had not been so anxious about his man Jack. But the Scarecrow, however damaged, was all there. The pumpkin head that was so necessary to Jack’s existence was missing. So the boy seized a long pole that fortunately lay near at hand. He anxiously turned again toward the river.

Far out on the water he saw the golden color of the pumpkin. It moved gently up and down with the waves. At that moment it was too far away for Tip to reach. But after a time it floated nearer and nearer. Then the boy was able to reach it with his pole and pull it to the shore. He brought it to the top of the bank. He carefully wiped the water from its pumpkin face with his handkerchief. Then he ran with it to Jack and put the head back on the man's neck.

"Dear me!" were Jack's first words. "What a terrible experience! I wonder if water can ruin pumpkins?"

Tip did not think a reply was necessary. He knew that the Scarecrow also needed his help. So he carefully removed the straw from the King's body and legs. He spread it out in the sun to dry. He hung the wet clothing over the body of the Saw-Horse.

"If water ruins pumpkins," observed Jack, with a deep sigh, "then my days are numbered."

"I've never noticed that water ruins pumpkins," returned Tip. "Unless the water happens to be boiling. If your head isn't cracked, my friend, you must be in fairly good condition."

"Oh, my head isn't cracked at all," declared Jack, more cheerfully.

"Then don't worry," replied the boy. "Care once killed a cat."

"Then," said Jack, seriously, "I am very glad indeed that I am not a cat."

The sun was quickly drying their clothing. Tip stirred up his Majesty's straw so that the warm rays might soak up the wetness and make it as dry and fresh as ever. When this was done, he stuffed the Scarecrow into a neat shape. He smoothed out his face so that he wore his usual happy and pleasing expression.

"Thank you very much," said the monarch, brightly, as he walked about and found himself well balanced. "There are several clear advantages in being a Scarecrow. If one has friends nearby to repair damages, nothing very serious can happen to you."

"I wonder if hot sunshine can crack pumpkins," said Jack, with an anxious ring in his voice.

"Not at all—not at all!" replied the Scarecrow, gaily. "All you need to fear, my boy, is old age. When your golden youth has faded we shall quickly part company. But you needn't look forward to it. We'll discover the fact ourselves, and notify you. But come! Let us continue our journey. I am eager to greet my friend the Tin Woodman."

So they got back on the Saw-Horse. Tip held to the post. The Pumpkinhead held on to Tip. The Scarecrow put both arms around the wooden form of Jack.

"Go slowly, for now there is no danger of pursuit," said Tip to his steed.

"All right!" responded the creature, in a voice rather rough.

"Aren't you a little hoarse?" asked the Pumpkinhead politely.

The Saw-Horse gave an angry jump and rolled one knotty eye backward toward Tip.

"See here," he growled, "can't you protect me from insult?"

"To be sure!" answered Tip, soothingly. "I am sure Jack meant no harm. And it will not do for us to quarrel, you know. We must all remain good friends."

"I'll have nothing more to do with that Pumpkinhead," declared the Saw-Horse, viciously. "He loses his head too easily to suit me."

There seemed no fitting reply to this speech, so for a time they rode along in silence.

After a while the Scarecrow remarked:

"This reminds me of old times. It was upon this grassy hill that I once saved Dorothy from the Stinging Bees of the Wicked Witch of the West."

"Do Stinging Bees injure pumpkins?" asked Jack, glancing around fearfully.

"They are all dead, so it doesn't matter," replied the Scarecrow. "And here is where Nick Chopper destroyed the Wicked Witch's Grey Wolves."

"Who was Nick Chopper?" asked Tip.

"That is the name of my friend the Tin Woodman," answered his Majesty. "And here is where the Winged Monkeys captured and tied us, and flew away with little Dorothy," he continued, after they had traveled a little way farther.

"Do Winged Monkeys ever eat pumpkins?" asked Jack, with a shiver of fear.

"I do not know. But you have little cause to worry, for the Winged Monkeys are now the slaves of Glinda the Good. She owns the Golden Cap that commands their services," said the Scarecrow, thoughtfully.

Then the stuffed monarch became lost in thought, recalling the days of past adventures. And the Saw-Horse rocked and rolled over the flower-covered fields and carried its riders swiftly on their way.

Twilight fell, by and by, and then the dark shadows of night. So Tip stopped the horse and they all got off.

"I'm tired out," said the boy, yawning wearily. "And the grass is soft and cool. Let us lie down here and sleep until morning."

"I can't sleep," said Jack.

"I never do," said the Scarecrow.

"I do not even know what sleep is," said the Saw-Horse.

"Still, we must have consideration for this poor boy. He is made of flesh and blood and bone, and gets tired," suggested the Scarecrow, in his usual thoughtful manner. "I remember it was the same way with little Dorothy. We always had to sit through the night while she slept."

"I'm sorry," said Tip, meekly, "but I can't help it. And I'm dreadfully hungry, too!"

"Here is a new danger!" remarked Jack, gloomily. "I hope you are not fond of eating pumpkins."

"Not unless they're stewed and made into pies," answered the boy, laughing. "So have no fears of me, friend Jack."

"What a coward that Pumpkinhead is!" said the Saw-Horse, scornfully.

"You might be a coward yourself, if you knew you could be ruined!" replied Jack, angrily.

"There!—there!" interrupted the Scarecrow. "Don't let us quarrel. We all have our weaknesses, dear friends. So we must try to be considerate of one another. And since this poor boy is hungry and has nothing whatever to eat, let us all remain quiet and allow him to sleep. For it is said that in sleep a mortal may forget even hunger."

"Thank you!" exclaimed Tip, gratefully. "Your Majesty is fully as good as you are wise—and that is saying a good deal!"

He then stretched himself upon the grass. He used the stuffed form of the Scarecrow for a pillow, and was soon fast asleep.

A Nickel-Plated Emperor

Tip awoke soon after dawn. But the Scarecrow had already risen. With his clumsy fingers he had picked a double handful of ripe berries from some bushes nearby. The boy ate these greedily, finding them an ample breakfast. Afterward the little party continued its journey.

After an hour's ride they reached the top of a hill. From there they saw the City of the Winkies. They noted the tall domes of the Emperor's palace rising from the clusters of more modest dwellings.

The Scarecrow became greatly excited at this sight, and exclaimed:

“How happy I shall be to see my old friend the Tin Woodman again! I hope he rules his people better than I have ruled mine!”

“Is the Tin Woodman the Emperor of the Winkies?” asked the horse.

“Yes, indeed. They invited him to rule over them soon after the Wicked Witch was destroyed. Nick Chopper has the best heart in all the world. I am sure he has proved an excellent and able emperor.”

“I thought that ‘Emperor’ was the title of a person who rules an empire,” said Tip. “And the Country of the Winkies is only a Kingdom.”

“Don’t mention that to the Tin Woodman!” said the Scarecrow, seriously. “You would hurt his feelings very much. He is a proud man, as he has every reason to be. It pleases him to be called Emperor rather than King.”

“I’m sure it makes no difference to me,” replied the boy.

The Saw-Horse now moved forward at a pace so fast that its riders had hard work to stay on its back. So there was little more conversation until they stopped beside the palace steps.

An old Winkie, dressed in a uniform of silver cloth, came forward to help them get down. The Scarecrow said to this man:

“Show us at once to your master, the Emperor.”

The man looked from one to another of the party in an uncomfortable way. At last he answered:

“I fear I must ask you to wait for a time. The Emperor is not receiving this morning.”

“How is that?” asked the Scarecrow, worriedly. “I hope nothing has happened to him.”

“Oh, no; nothing serious,” returned the man. “But this is his Majesty’s day for being polished. And just now his great presence is thickly covered with putz-pomade.”

“Oh, I see!” cried the Scarecrow, much relieved. “My friend was always inclined to be a dandy. And I suppose he is now more proud than ever of his personal appearance.”

“He is, indeed,” said the man, with a polite bow. “Our mighty Emperor has lately caused himself to be nickel-plated.”

“Good Gracious!” the Scarecrow cried at hearing this. “If his wit has the same polish, how sparkling it must be! But show us in—I’m sure the Emperor will receive us, even in his present state.”

“The Emperor’s state is always magnificent,” said the man. “But I will venture to tell him of your arrival, and will receive his commands concerning you.”

So the party followed the servant into a splendid ante-room. The Saw-Horse moved awkwardly after them, having no knowledge that a horse might be expected to remain outside.

The travelers were at first somewhat awed by their surroundings. Even the Scarecrow seemed impressed as he examined the rich hangings of silver cloth caught up into knots and fastened with tiny silver axes. Upon a handsome center-table stood a large silver oil-can. It was richly engraved with scenes from the past adventures of the Tin Woodman, Dorothy, the Cowardly Lion and the Scarecrow. The lines of the engraving were traced upon the silver in yellow gold. On the walls hung several portraits. That of the Scarecrow seemed to be the most prominent and carefully executed. A large painting of the famous Wizard of Oz, in the act of presenting the Tin Woodman with a heart, covered almost one entire end of the room.

While the visitors gazed at these things in silent admiration they suddenly heard a loud voice in the next room exclaim:

“Well! well! well! What a great surprise!”

And then the door burst open and Nick Chopper rushed into their midst. He caught the Scarecrow in a close and loving embrace that creased him into many folds and wrinkles.

“My dear old friend! My noble comrade!” cried the Tin Woodman, joyfully. “How delighted I am to meet you once again.”

And then he released the Scarecrow and held him at arms’ length while he surveyed the beloved, painted features.

But, alas! the face of the Scarecrow and many portions of his body bore great blotches of putz-pomade. For the Tin Woodman, in his eagerness to welcome his friend, had quite forgotten the condition of his toilet. He had rubbed the thick coating of paste from his own body to that of his comrade.

“Dear me!” said the Scarecrow sadly. “What a mess I’m in!”

“Never mind, my friend,” returned the Tin Woodman. “I’ll send you to my Imperial Laundry, and you’ll come out as good as new.”

“Won’t I be mangled?” asked the Scarecrow.

“No, indeed!” was the reply. “But tell me, how came your Majesty here? And who are your companions?”

The Scarecrow, with great politeness, introduced Tip and Jack Pumpkinhead. The latter personage seemed to interest the Tin Woodman greatly.

“You are not very substantial, I must admit,” said the Emperor. “But you are certainly unusual, and therefore worthy to become a member of our select society.”

“I thank your Majesty,” said Jack, humbly.

“I hope you are enjoying good health?” continued the Woodman.

“At present, yes;” replied the Pumpkinhead, with a sigh. “But I am in constant terror of the day when I shall spoil.”

“Nonsense!” said the Emperor—but in a kindly, sympathetic tone. “Do not, I beg of you, dampen today’s sun with the showers of tomorrow. For before your head has time to spoil you can have it canned, and in that way it may be preserved indefinitely.”

Tip, during this conversation, was looking at the Woodman with open amazement. He noticed that the celebrated Emperor of the Winkies was composed entirely of pieces of tin, neatly soldered and riveted together into the form of a man. The Astonishing Flight of the Gump

When the adventurers met again upon the roof, they found that the different members of the party had chosen a very strange mixed group of articles. No one seemed to have a very clear idea of what was needed. But all had brought something.

“They are,” said Tip. “We didn’t bring ’em to life.”

“You’re expected to fly,” explained the Scarecrow, “not to walk.”

“We can walk ourselves,” said the Woggle-Bug.

“I begin to understand what is required of me,” remarked the Gump. “So I will do my best to please you.” And he flew on for a time in silence.

Presently Jack Pumpkinhead became uneasy.

“I wonder if riding through the air is likely to spoil pumpkins,” he said.

“Not unless you carelessly drop your head over the side,” answered the Woggle-Bug. “In that event your head would no longer be a pumpkin, for it would become a squash.”

“Have I not asked you to hold back these unfeeling jokes?” demanded Tip, looking at the Woggle-Bug with a severe expression.

“You have; and I’ve held back a good many of them,” replied the insect. “But there are chances for so many excellent puns in our language. To an educated person like myself, the temptation to express them is almost irresistible.”

“People with more or less education discovered those puns centuries ago,” said Tip.

“Are you sure?” asked the Woggle-Bug, with a startled look.

“Of course I am,” answered the boy. “An educated Woggle-Bug may be a new thing. But a Woggle-Bug education is as old as the hills, judging from the display you make of it.”

The insect seemed much impressed by this remark, and for a time kept a meek silence.

The Scarecrow, in shifting his seat, saw upon the cushions the pepper-box which Tip had cast aside. He began to examine it.

“Throw it overboard,” said the boy. “It’s quite empty now, and there’s no use keeping it.”

“Is it really empty?” asked the Scarecrow, looking curiously into the box.

“Of course it is,” answered Tip. “I shook out every grain of the powder.”

“Then the box has two bottoms,” announced the Scarecrow. “The bottom on the inside is fully an inch away from the bottom on the outside.”

“Let me see,” said the Tin Woodman, taking the box from his friend. “Yes,” he declared, after looking it over. “The thing certainly has a false bottom. Now, I wonder what that is for?”

“Can’t you get it apart, and find out?” enquired Tip, now quite interested in the mystery.

“Why, yes; the lower bottom unscrews,” said the Tin Woodman. “My fingers are rather stiff. Please see if you can open it.”

He handed the pepper-box to Tip. Tip had no trouble taking off the bottom. In the space below were three silver pills. A carefully folded paper lay under them.

The boy began to open the paper. He was careful not to spill the pills. He found several lines clearly written in red ink.

"Read it out loud," said the Scarecrow. So Tip read:

"DR. NIKIDIK'S CELEBRATED WISHING PILLS.

"Directions for Use: Swallow one pill. Count seventeen by twos. Then make a Wish. The Wish will be granted at once.

CAUTION: Keep in a Dry and Dark Place."

"Why, this is a very valuable discovery!" cried the Scarecrow.

"It is, indeed," replied Tip, seriously. "These pills may be of great use to us. I wonder if old Mombi knew they were in the bottom of the pepper-box. I remember hearing her say she got the Powder of Life from this same Nikidik."

"He must be a powerful Sorcerer!" cried the Tin Woodman. "And since the powder proved a success, we ought to trust the pills."

"But how," asked the Scarecrow, "can anyone count seventeen by twos? Seventeen is an odd number."

"That is true," replied Tip, very disappointed. "No one can possibly count seventeen by twos."

"Then the pills are of no use to us," cried the Pumpkinhead. "And this fact fills me with grief. For I had meant to wish that my head would never spoil."

"Nonsense!" said the Scarecrow, sharply. "If we could use the pills at all, we would make far better wishes than that."

"I do not see how anything could be better," protested poor Jack. "If you could spoil at any time, you would understand my worry."

"For my part," said the Tin Woodman, "I feel for you in every way. But since we cannot count seventeen by twos, sympathy is all you are likely to get."

By this time it had become quite dark. The travelers found a cloudy sky above them. The rays of the moon could not get through.

The Gump flew on without stopping. For some reason the huge sofa-body rocked more and more dizzily every hour.

The Woggle-Bug said he was sea-sick. Tip was also pale and somewhat troubled. But the others held on to the backs of the sofas. They did not seem to mind the motion as long as they were not tipped out.

The night grew darker and darker. On and on sped the Gump through the black heavens. The travelers could not even see one another. A heavy silence settled down upon them.

After a long time Tip, who had been thinking deeply, spoke.

"How are we to know when we come to the palace of Glinda the Good?" he asked.

"It's a long way to Glinda's palace," answered the Woodman. "I've traveled it."

"But how are we to know how fast the Gump is flying?" the boy asked again. "We cannot see a single thing down on the earth. Before morning we may be far beyond the place we want to reach."

"That is all true enough," the Scarecrow replied, a little uneasily. "But I do not see how we can stop just now. We might come down in a river, or on the top of a steeple. And that would be a great disaster."

So they let the Gump fly on, with regular flops of its great wings. They waited patiently for morning.

Then Tip's fears were proven to be well founded. With the first streaks of gray dawn they looked over the sides of the sofas. They discovered rolling plains dotted with strange villages. The houses were not dome-shaped, as they all are in the Land of Oz. They had slanting roofs that rose to a peak in the center. Odd looking animals were also moving about upon the open plains. The country was unfamiliar to both the Tin Woodman and the Scarecrow. They had visited Glinda the Good's domain before and knew it well.

"We are lost!" said the Scarecrow, sadly. "The Gump must have carried us entirely out of the Land of Oz. It carried us over the sandy deserts and into the terrible outside world that Dorothy told us about."

"We must get back," cried the Tin Woodman, earnestly. "We must get back as soon as possible!"

"Turn around!" cried Tip to the Gump. "Turn as quickly as you can!"

"If I do, I shall upset," answered the Gump. "I'm not at all used to flying. The best plan would be for me to come down in some place. Then I can turn around and take a fresh start."

Just then, however, there seemed to be no stopping-place that would answer their purpose. They flew over a village so big that the Woggle-Bug said it was a city. Then they came to a range of high mountains. Many deep gorges and steep cliffs showed plainly.

"Now is our chance to stop," said the boy. He found they were very close to the mountain tops. Then he turned to the Gump and commanded: "Stop at the first level place you see!"

"Very well," answered the Gump. It came down upon a table of rock that stood between two cliffs.

But the Gump was not experienced in such matters. He did not judge his speed correctly. Instead of coming to a stop upon the flat rock, he missed it by half the width of his body. He broke off both his right wings against the sharp edge of the rock. Then he tumbled over and over down the cliff.

Our friends held on to the sofas as long as they could. But the Gump caught on a projecting rock. The Thing stopped suddenly—bottom side up—and all were dumped out at once.

By good fortune they fell only a few feet. Below them was a monster nest, built by a colony of Jackdaws in a hollow ledge of rock. So none of them—not even the Pumpkinhead—was injured by the fall. Jack found his precious head resting on the soft breast of the Scarecrow, which made an excellent cushion. Tip fell on a mass of leaves and papers, which saved him from injury. The Woggle-Bug had bumped his round head against the Saw-Horse. But it caused him no more than a moment's inconvenience.

The Tin Woodman was at first much alarmed. But he found he had escaped without even a scratch upon his beautiful nickle-plate. He at once regained his usual cheerfulness and turned to address his comrades.

"Our Journey had ended rather suddenly," said he. "And we cannot justly blame our friend the Gump for our accident. He did the best he could under the circumstances. But how we are ever to escape from this nest I must leave to someone with better brains than I possess."

He looked at the Scarecrow here. The Scarecrow crawled to the edge of the nest and looked over. Below them was a steep rock wall several hundred feet deep. Above them was a smooth cliff with no breaks. The only thing was the point of rock where the broken body of the Gump still hung from the end of one of the sofas. There really seemed to be no way to escape. They understood their helpless situation. The small group of adventurers gave in to their confusion.

“This is a worse prison than the palace,” the Woggle-Bug said sadly.

“I wish we had stayed there,” Jack moaned.

“I’m afraid the mountain air isn’t good for pumpkins.”

“It won’t be when the Jackdaws come back,” the Saw-Horse growled. It lay waving its legs in a useless attempt to get on its feet again. “Jackdaws are especially fond of pumpkins.”

“Do you think the birds will come here?” asked Jack, very upset.

“Of course they will,” said Tip. “This is their nest. And there must be hundreds of them,” he continued. “See what a lot of things they have brought here!”

Indeed, the nest was half filled with a very strange collection of small articles. The birds could have no use for them. But the thieving Jackdaws had stolen them over many years from the homes of men. The nest was safely hidden where no human being could reach it. So this lost property would never be found.

The Woggle-Bug searched among the rubbish. The Jackdaws stole useless things as well as valuable ones. He turned up a beautiful diamond necklace with his foot. The Tin Woodman admired it so much that the Woggle-Bug gave it to him with a graceful speech. After that, the Woodman hung it around his neck with much pride. He rejoiced exceedingly when the big diamonds glittered in the sun’s rays.

But now they heard a great jabbering and flapping of wings. The sound grew nearer to them. Tip exclaimed:

“The Jackdaws are coming! And if they find us here, they will surely kill us in their anger.”

“I was afraid of this!” the Pumpkinhead moaned. “My time has come!”

“And mine, also!” said the Woggle-Bug. “Jackdaws are the greatest enemies of my race.”

The others were not afraid at all. But the Scarecrow at once decided to save those of the party who might be hurt by the angry birds. So he commanded Tip to take off Jack’s head and lie down with it in the bottom of the nest. When this was done, he ordered the Woggle-Bug to lie beside Tip. Nick Chopper knew from past experience just what to do. He then took the Scarecrow to pieces (all except his head) and scattered the straw over Tip and the Woggle-Bug. It completely covered their bodies.

Hardly had this been done when the flock of Jackdaws reached them. The birds saw the intruders in their nest and flew down upon them with screams of rage.

The Tin Woodman was usually a peaceful man. But when occasion required, he could fight as fiercely as a Roman gladiator. The Jackdaws nearly knocked him down in their rush of wings. Their sharp beaks and claws threatened to damage his brilliant plating. So the Woodman picked up his axe and made it whirl swiftly around his head.

Many were beaten off in this way. But the birds were so numerous and so brave that they continued the attack as furiously as before. Some of them pecked at the eyes of the Gump, which hung over the nest in a helpless condition. But the Gump’s eyes were of glass and could not be injured. Others of the Jackdaws rushed at the Saw-Horse. But that animal was still upon his back. He kicked out so viciously with his wooden legs that he beat off as many assailants as did the Woodman’s axe.

Finding themselves thus opposed, the birds fell upon the Scarecrow’s straw. It lay at the center of the nest, covering Tip and the Woggle-Bug and Jack’s pumpkin head. The birds began tearing it away and flying off with it. They only let it drop, straw by straw, into the great gulf beneath.

The Scarecrow’s head saw with dismay this wanton destruction of his interior. He cried to the Tin Woodman to save him. That good friend responded with renewed energy. His axe fairly flashed among the Jackdaws. Fortunately, the Gump began wildly waving the two wings remaining on the left side of its body. The flutter of these great wings filled the Jackdaws with terror. The Gump by its exertions freed itself from the peg of rock on which it hung. It sank flopping into the nest. The alarm of the birds knew no bounds, and they fled screaming over the mountains.

When the last foe had disappeared, Tip crawled from under the sofas. He assisted the Woggle-Bug to follow him.

“We are saved!” shouted the boy, delightedly.

“We are, indeed!” responded the Educated Insect. He fairly hugged the stiff head of the Gump in his joy. “And we owe it all to the flopping of the Thing, and the good axe of the Woodman!”

“If I am saved, get me out of here!” called Jack. His head was still beneath the sofas. Tip managed to roll the pumpkin out and place it upon its neck again. He also set the Saw-Horse upright and said to it:

“We owe you many thanks for the gallant fight you made.”

“I really think we have escaped very nicely,” remarked the Tin Woodman, in a tone of pride.

“Not so!” exclaimed a hollow voice.

At this they all turned in surprise to look at the Scarecrow’s head. It lay at the back of the nest.

“I am completely ruined!” declared the Scarecrow, as he noted their astonishment. “For where is the straw that stuffs my body?”

The awful question startled them all. They gazed around the nest with horror, for not a vestige of straw remained. The Jackdaws had stolen it to the last wisp. They had flung it all into the chasm that yawned for hundreds of feet beneath the nest.

“My poor, poor friend!” said the Tin Woodman. He took up the Scarecrow’s head and caressed it tenderly. “Whoever could imagine you would come to this untimely end?”

“I did it to save my friends,” returned the head. “And I am glad that I perished in so noble and unselfish a manner.”

“But why are you all so despondent?” inquired the Woggle-Bug. “The Scarecrow’s clothing is still safe.”

“Yes,” answered the Tin Woodman. “But our friend’s clothes are useless without stuffing.”

“Why not stuff him with money?” asked Tip.

“Money!” they all cried, in an amazed chorus.

“To be sure,” said the boy. “In the bottom of the nest are thousands of dollar bills—and two-dollar bills—and five-dollar bills—and tens, and twenties, and fifties. There are enough of them to stuff a dozen Scarecrows. Why not use the money?”

The Tin Woodman began to turn over the rubbish with the handle of his axe. Sure enough, what they had first thought were only worthless papers were found to be all bills of various values. The mischievous Jackdaws had been stealing them for years from the villages and cities they visited.

There was an immense fortune lying in that hard-to-reach nest. Tip's suggestion was quickly acted upon, with the Scarecrow's consent.

They selected all the newest and cleanest bills and sorted them into various piles. The Scarecrow's left leg and boot were stuffed with five-dollar bills. His right leg was stuffed with ten-dollar bills. His body was so closely filled with fifties, one-hundreds and one-thousands that he could scarcely button his jacket with comfort.

Although at first glance I might be mistaken for a Safety Deposit Vault, I beg you to remember that my Brains are still composed of the same old material. He claimed anything could be done with x's and y's and a's, and such things, by mixing them up with plenty of plusses and minuses and equals, and so forth. But he never said anything, so far as I can remember, about counting up to the odd number of seventeen by the even numbers of twos."

"Yes," said Tip. "old Mombi couldn't use x's and minuses, for she never went to school."

They looked at each other in surprise, for the Saw-Horse was considered the most stupid of the entire party.

“Hooray!” shouted the Scarecrow, happily. “We can now leave this terrible Jackdaws’ nest whenever we please.”

“But it is nearly dark,” said the Tin Woodman. “And unless we wait until morning to make our flight, we may get into more trouble. I do not like these night trips, for one never knows what will happen.”

So it was decided to wait until daylight. The adventurers amused themselves in the twilight by searching the Jackdaws’ nest for treasures.

The Woggle-Bug found two handsome bracelets of wrought gold. They fitted his slender arms very well. The Scarecrow took a fancy for rings, of which there were many in the nest. Before long he had fitted a ring to each finger of his padded gloves. Not being content with that display, he added one more to each thumb. He carefully chose those rings set with sparkling stones, such as rubies, amethysts and sapphires. The Scarecrow’s hands now presented a most brilliant appearance.

“This nest would be a picnic for Queen Jinjur,” said he, musingly. “For as nearly as I can make out, she and her girls conquered me merely to rob my city of its emeralds.”

The Tin Woodman was content with his diamond necklace and refused to accept any additional decorations. But Tip secured a fine gold watch, which was attached to a heavy fob, and placed it in his pocket with much pride. He also pinned several jeweled brooches to Jack Pumpkinhead’s red waistcoat. “It’s very pretty,” said the creature, regarding the lorgnette approvingly. “But what is it for?”

That none of the party might be slighted, they ended by placing several large seal rings upon the points of the Gump’s antlers. But that odd personage seemed by no means gratified by the attention.

Darkness soon fell upon them. Tip and the Woggle-Bug went to sleep while the others sat down to wait patiently for the day.

But our adventurers did not wait for the assault. They tumbled into the cushioned seats of the sofas as quickly as possible. Tip gave the word to the Gump to start.

She listened to their foolish talk and sent a sharp challenge to Glinda the Good and Princess Ozma. The result was a declaration of war. The very next day, Glinda marched on the Emerald City. Flags flew and bands played. A forest of shining spears sparkled brightly in the sun.

But when they came to the walls, this brave group suddenly stopped. Jinjur had closed and barred every gate. The walls of the Emerald City were built high and thick with many blocks of green marble. Glinda found her advance blocked. She bent her brows in deep thought. Then the Woggle-Bug said in his most certain tone:

"We must surround the city and wait. We must starve it into submission. It is the only thing we can do."

"Not so," answered the Scarecrow. "We still have the Gump. And the Gump can still fly."

The Sorceress turned quickly at these words. Her face now wore a bright smile.

"You are right," she exclaimed. "And you certainly have reason to be proud of your brains. Let us go to the Gump at once!"

They passed through the ranks of the army. They came to the place near the Scarecrow's tent where the Gump lay. Glinda and Princess Ozma got on first and sat on the sofas. Then the Scarecrow and his friends climbed aboard. There was still room for a Captain and three soldiers. Glinda thought this was enough for a guard.

The Princess gave a word. The strange Thing they called the Gump flapped its palm-leaf wings and rose into the air. It carried the group of adventurers high above the walls. They hovered over the palace. Soon they saw Jinjur lying in a hammock in the courtyard. She was comfortably reading a novel with a green cover and eating green chocolates. She was sure the walls would protect her from her enemies. The Gump obeyed a quick command and landed safely in this very courtyard. Jinjur had time only to scream. The Captain and three soldiers jumped out and made the former Queen a prisoner. They locked strong chains on both her wrists.

That act really ended the war. The Army of Revolt submitted as soon as they knew Jinjur was a captive. The Captain marched safely through the streets and up to the gates of the city. She threw them wide open. Then the bands played their most stirring music while Glinda's army marched into the city. Heralds proclaimed the conquest of the bold Jinjur. They also proclaimed the rise of the beautiful Princess Ozma to the throne of her royal ancestors.

At once the men of the Emerald City took off their aprons. It is said the women were so tired of eating their husbands' cooking that they all welcomed the conquest of Jinjur with joy. Certain it is that the good wives rushed to the kitchens of their houses. They prepared such a delicious feast for the weary men that harmony was immediately restored in every family.

Ozma's first act was to make the Army of Revolt return every emerald or other gem stolen from the public streets and buildings. These vain girls had picked so many precious stones from their settings that every one of the royal jewelers worked steadily for more than a month to put them back.

Meanwhile the Army of Revolt was disbanded and the girls were sent home to their mothers. Jinjur was likewise released on promise of good behavior.

Ozma made the loveliest Queen the Emerald City had ever known. Although she was so young and inexperienced, she ruled her people with wisdom and justice. Glinda gave her good advice on all occasions. The Woggle-Bug was appointed to the important post of Public Educator. He was quite helpful to Ozma when her royal duties grew perplexing.

The girl was grateful to the Gump for its services. She offered the creature any reward it might name.

"Then," replied the Gump, "please take me to pieces. I did not wish to be brought to life. I am very sorry about my mixed-up self. Once I was a king of the forest, as my horns clearly show. But now, in my present padded condition of service, I am forced to fly through the air. My legs are of no use to me at all. So I beg to be taken apart."

So Ozma ordered the Gump taken apart. The head with horns was again hung over the fireplace in the hall. The couches were untied and placed in the reception rooms. The broom tail went back to its usual duties in the kitchen. Finally, the Scarecrow put back all the lines and ropes on the hooks from which he had taken them on that important day when the Thing was built.

You might think that was the end of the Gump. And so it was, as a flying machine. But the head over the fireplace kept talking whenever it felt like it. It often surprised people with its sudden questions as they waited in the hall to see the Queen.

The Saw-Horse was Ozma's own property, and she cared for it gently. She often rode the strange creature along the streets of the Emerald City. She had its wooden legs covered with gold to keep them from wearing out. The sound of these golden shoes on the street always filled the Queen's people with wonder. They thought of this as proof of her magical powers.

"The Wonderful Wizard was never as wonderful as Queen Ozma," the people said to each other in whispers. "He claimed to do many things he could not do. But our new Queen does many things no one would ever expect her to do."

Jack Pumpkinhead stayed with Ozma to the end of his days. He did not go bad as soon as he had feared, though he always stayed as stupid as ever. The Woggle-Bug tried to teach him several arts and sciences. But Jack was such a poor student that any attempt to educate him was soon given up.

After Glinda's army had marched back home, and peace had returned to the Emerald City, the Tin Woodman announced his plan to return to his own Kingdom of the Winkies.

"It is not a very big Kingdom," he said to Ozma. "But for that very reason it is easier to rule. I have called myself an Emperor because I am an Absolute Monarch, and no one interferes in any way with my conduct of public or personal affairs. When I get home I shall have a new coat of nickel plate. For I have become somewhat damaged and scratched lately. And then I shall be glad to have you visit me."

"Thank you," replied Ozma. "Some day I may accept the invitation. But what is to become of the Scarecrow?"

"I shall return with my friend the Tin Woodman," said the stuffed one seriously. "We have decided never to be parted in the future."

"And I have made the Scarecrow my Royal Treasurer," explained the Tin Woodman. "For it has occurred to me that it is a good thing to have a Royal Treasurer who is made of money. What do you think?"

"I think," said the little Queen, smiling, "that your friend must be the richest man in all the world."

"I am," returned the Scarecrow. "But not because of my money. For I consider brains far better than money, in every way. You may have noticed that if one has money without brains, he cannot use it to advantage. But if one has brains without money, they will help him live comfortably to the end of his days."

"At the same time," declared the Tin Woodman, "you must agree that a good heart is a thing that brains cannot create, and that money cannot buy. Perhaps, after all, it is I who am the richest man in all the world."

"You are both rich, my friends," said Ozma gently. "And your riches are the only riches worth having—the riches of content!"
