THE ADVENTURES OF BUSTER BEAR

I

BUSTER BEAR GOES FISHING

Buster Bear yawned as he lay on his comfortable bed of leaves. He watched the first early morning sunbeams moving through the Green Forest to chase out the Black Shadows. Once more he yawned. Then he slowly got to his feet and shook himself. Then he walked over to a big pine-tree. He stood up on his back legs. He reached as high up on the trunk of the tree as he could. He scratched the bark with his great claws. After that he yawned until it seemed as if his jaws would crack. Then he sat down to think what he wanted for breakfast.

While he sat there, he tried to make up his mind what would taste best. He was listening to the sounds that told of the waking of all the little people who live in the Green Forest. He heard Sammy Jay way off in the distance screaming, "Thief! Thief!" and grinned. "I wonder," thought Buster, "if some one has stolen Sammy's breakfast, or if he has stolen the breakfast of some one else. Probably he is the thief himself."

He heard Chatterer the Red Squirrel scolding as fast as he could make his tongue go. Chatterer was working himself into a terrible rage. "Must be that Chatterer got out of bed the wrong way this morning," thought he.

He heard Blacky the Crow cawing at the top of his lungs. He knew by the sound that Blacky was getting into mischief of some kind. He heard the sweet voices of happy little singers, and they were good to hear. But most of all he listened to a merry, low, silvery laugh. It never stopped but went on and on, until he just felt as if he must laugh too. It was the voice of the Laughing Brook. And as Buster listened it suddenly came to him just what he wanted for breakfast.

"I'm going fishing," said he in his deep grumbly-rumbly voice to no one in particular. "Yes, Sir, I'm going fishing. I want some fat trout for my breakfast."

He shuffled along over to the Laughing Brook, and straight to a little pool of which he knew. As he drew near he took the greatest care not to make the teeniest, weeniest bit of noise. Now it just happened that early as he was, some one was before Buster Bear. When he came in sight of the little pool, who should he see but another fisherman there, who had already caught a fine fat trout. Who was it? Why, Little Joe Otter to be sure. He was just climbing up the bank with the fat trout in his mouth. Buster Bear's own mouth watered as he saw it. Little Joe sat down on the bank and prepared to enjoy his breakfast. He hadn't seen Buster Bear, and he didn't know that he or any one else was anywhere near.

Buster Bear tiptoed up very softly until he was right behind Little Joe Otter. "Woof, woof!" said he in his deepest, most grumbly-rumbly voice. "That's a very fine looking trout. I wouldn't mind if I had it myself."

Little Joe Otter gave a frightened squeal. Without even turning to see who was speaking, he dropped his fish and dived headfirst into the Laughing Brook. Buster Bear sprang forward and with one of his big paws caught the fat trout just as it was slipping back into the water.

"Here's your trout, Mr. Otter," said he, as Little Joe put his head out of water to see who had frightened him so. "Come and get it."

[Illustration: "Here's your trout, Mr. Otter," said he. _Page 5._]

But Little Joe wouldn't. The fact is, he was afraid to. He snarled at Buster Bear and called him a thief and everything bad he could think of. Buster didn't seem to mind. He chuckled as if he thought it all a great joke. He repeated his invitation to Little Joe to come and get his fish. But Little Joe just turned his back and went off down the Laughing Brook in a great rage.

"It's too bad to waste such a fine fish," said Buster thoughtfully. "I wonder what I'd better do with it." And while he was wondering, he ate it all up. Then he started down the Laughing Brook to try to catch some for himself.

II

LITTLE JOE OTTER GETS EVEN WITH BUSTER BEAR

Little Joe Otter was in a terrible rage. It was a bad beginning for a beautiful day and Little Joe knew it. But who wouldn't be in a rage if his breakfast was taken from him just as he was about to eat it? Anyway, that is what Little Joe told Billy Mink. Perhaps he didn't tell it quite exactly as it was, but you know he was very badly frightened at the time.

"I was sitting on the bank of the Laughing Brook beside one of the little pools," he told Billy Mink. "I was just going to eat a fat fish I had caught. Then who should come along but that great big mean one, Buster Bear. He took that fat fish away from me and ate it just as if it belonged to him! I hate him! If I live long enough, I am going to get even with him!"

Of course that was not nice talk and it was not a nice spirit. But Little Joe Otter's anger is sometimes very short. This is especially true when he is hungry. And this time he had had no breakfast, you know.

Buster Bear had not really taken the fish away from Little Joe. But if you look at the matter as Little Joe did, it was the same thing. You see, Buster knew very well when he invited Little Joe to come back and get it that Little Joe would not dare do anything of the kind.

"Where is he now?" asked Billy Mink.

"He is somewhere up the Laughing Brook. I wish he would fall in and get drowned!" said Little Joe.

Billy Mink just had to laugh. The idea of great big Buster Bear getting drowned in the Laughing Brook was too funny. There was not enough water in it anywhere except down in the Smiling Pool. And that was on the Green Meadows, where Buster had never been known to go. "Let us go see what he is doing," said Billy Mink.

At first Little Joe did not want to. But at last his curiosity was stronger than his fear, and he agreed. So the two little brown-coated ones turned down the Laughing Brook. They took the greatest care to keep out of sight. They had gone only a little way when Billy Mink whispered: "Sh-h! There he is."

Sure enough, there was Buster Bear sitting close beside a little pool and looking into it very carefully.

"What is he doing?" asked Little Joe Otter. Buster Bear sat for the longest time without moving.

Just then one of Buster's big feet went into the water as quick as a flash. It pulled out a fish that had come too near.

"He is fishing!" said Billy Mink.

And that is just what Buster Bear was doing. It was very plain to see that he was having great fun. When he had eaten the fish he had caught, he moved along to the next little pool.

"They are _our_ fish!" said Little Joe in a fierce way. "He has no business catching _our_ fish!"

"I do not see how we are going to stop him," said Billy Mink.

"I do!" cried Little Joe. A new idea had just come into his head. "I am going to drive all the fish out of the little pools and make the water muddy. Then we will see how many fish he will get! Just you watch me get even with Buster Bear."

Little Joe slipped quickly into the water and swam straight to the little pool that Buster Bear would try next. He made the fish afraid so that they fled in every direction. Then he stirred up the mud until the water was so dirty that Buster could not have seen a fish right under his nose. He did the same thing in the next pool and the next. Buster Bear's fishing was spoiled for that day.

III

BUSTER BEAR IS GREATLY PUZZLED

Buster Bear had not enjoyed himself so much since he came to the Green Forest to live. His fun began when he surprised Little Joe Otter on the bank of a little pool in the Laughing Brook. Little Joe was so afraid that he dropped a fat fish he had just caught. It had seemed like a great joke to Buster Bear. He had laughed over it all the time he was eating the fat fish. When he had finished it, he started on to do some fishing himself.

Presently he came to another little pool. He moved up to it very, very softly, so as not to make the fish afraid. Then he sat down close to the edge of it and did not move. Buster learned a long time ago that a fisherman must be patient. This is true unless, like Little Joe Otter, he is just as much at home in the water as the fish themselves. Then he can swim fast enough to catch them by chasing them. So he did not move so much as an eye lash. He was so still that he looked almost like the stump of an old tree. Perhaps that is what the fish thought he was. Pretty soon, two or three swam right in close to where he was sitting. Now Buster Bear may be big and clumsy looking. But there is nothing that can move much quicker than one of those big feet of his when he wants it to. One of them moved now. Quicker than a wink, it had pulled one of those foolish fish out on to the bank.

Buster's little eyes twinkled. He smacked his lips as he moved on to the next little pool. He knew that it was of no use to stay longer at the first one. The fish were so afraid that they would not come back for a long, long time. At the next little pool the same thing happened. By this time Buster Bear was in fine spirits. It was fun to catch the fish, and it was still more fun to eat them. What finer breakfast could any one have than fresh-caught fish? No wonder he felt good! But it takes more than three fish to fill Buster Bear's stomach, so he kept on to the next little pool.

But this little pool was not beautiful and clear. Buster could not see right to the bottom of it and tell if there were any fish there. It was so muddy that he could not see into it at all. It looked as if some one had just stirred up all the mud at the bottom.

"Huh!" said Buster Bear. "It is of no use to try to fish here. I would just waste my time. I will try the next pool."

So he went on to the next little pool. He found this just as muddy as the other. Then he went on to another, and this was no better. Buster sat down and scratched his head. It was puzzling. Yes, Sir, it was puzzling. He looked this way and he looked that way with doubt, but there was no one to be seen. Everything was still except for the laughter of the Laughing Brook. Somehow, it seemed to Buster as if the Brook were laughing at him.

"It is very strange," said Buster in a low voice, "very strange indeed. It looks as if my fishing is spoiled for to-day. I do not understand it at all. It is lucky I caught what I did. It looks as if somebody is trying to--ha!" A sudden thought had popped into his head. Then he began to laugh softly and finally to laugh. "I do believe that Joe Otter is trying to get even with me for eating that fat fish!"

And then, because Buster Bear always enjoys a good joke even when it is on himself, he laughed until he had to hold his sides. This is a whole lot better than going off in a rage as Little Joe Otter had done. "You're pretty smart, Mr. Otter! You're pretty smart, but there are other people who are smart too," said Buster Bear. And still laughing, he went off to think up a plan to get the best of Little Joe Otter.

IV

LITTLE JOE OTTER SUPPLIES BUSTER BEAR WITH A BREAKFAST

Getting even just for spite does not always pay. In fact, it very often works the other way.

That is just how it came about that Little Joe Otter gave Buster Bear the best breakfast he had had for a long time. He did not mean to do it. Oh, my, no! The truth is, he thought all the time that he was stopping Buster Bear from getting a breakfast. You see he did not know Buster well enough to know that Buster is quite as smart as he is, and perhaps a little bit smarter. Spite and selfishness were at the bottom of it. You see Little Joe and Billy Mink had had all the fishing in the Laughing Brook to themselves so long that they thought no one else had any right to fish there. To be sure Bobby Coon caught a few little fish there, but they did not mind Bobby. Farmer Brown's boy fished there too, sometimes, and this always made Little Joe and Billy Mink very angry. But they were so afraid of him that they did not dare do anything about it. But when they discovered that Buster Bear was a fisherman, they made up their minds that something had to be done. At least, Little Joe did.

"He'll try it again to-morrow morning," said Little Joe. "I'll keep watch, and as soon as I see him coming, I'll drive out all the fish, just as I did to-day. I guess that will teach him to let our fish alone."

So the next morning Little Joe hid before sunrise close by the little pool where Buster Bear had given him such a scare. Sure enough, just as the Jolly Sunbeams began to come through the Green Forest, he saw Buster Bear coming straight over to the little pool. Little Joe slipped into the water and chased all the fish out of the little pool. He stirred up the mud on the bottom so that the water was so muddy that the bottom could not be seen at all. Then he hurried down to the next little pool and did the same thing.

Now Buster Bear is very smart. You know he had guessed the day before who had spoiled his fishing. So this morning he only went far enough to make sure that if Little Joe were watching for him, as he was sure he would be, he would see him coming. Then, instead of keeping on to the little pool, he hurried to a place way down the Laughing Brook, where the water was very shallow, hardly over his feet. There he sat laughing to himself. Things happened just as he had expected. The scared fish Little Joe chased out of the little pools up above swam down the Laughing Brook, because, you know, Little Joe was behind them, and there was nowhere else for them to go. When they came to the place where Buster was waiting, all he had to do was to scoop them out on to the bank. It was great fun. It did not take Buster long to catch all the fish he could eat. Then he saved a nice fat trout and waited.

By and by along came Little Joe Otter, laughing to think how he had spoiled Buster Bear's fishing. He was so intent on looking behind him to see if Buster was coming that he did not see Buster waiting there until he spoke.

"I'm much obliged for the fine breakfast you have given me," said Buster in his deepest, most grumbly-rumbly voice. "I've saved a fat trout for you to make up for the one I ate yesterday. I hope we will go fishing together often."

Then he went off laughing fit to kill himself. Little Joe could not find a word to say. He was so surprised and angry that he went off by himself and sulked. And Billy Mink, who had been watching, ate the fat trout.

V

GRANDFATHER FROG'S COMMON-SENSE

There is nothing quite like common sense to smooth out troubles. People who have plenty of just plain common sense are often thought to be very wise. Their neighbors look up to them and are forever running to them for advice, and they are very much respected. That is the way with Grandfather Frog. He is very old and very wise. Anyway, that is what his neighbors think. The truth is, he simply has a lot of common sense, which after all is the very best kind of wisdom.

Now when Little Joe Otter found that Buster Bear had been too smart for him, and that instead of spoiling Buster's fishing in the Laughing Brook he had really made it easier for Buster to catch all the fish he wanted, Little Joe went off down to the Smiling Pool in a great rage.

Billy Mink stopped long enough to eat the fat fish Buster had left on the bank and then he too went down to the Smiling Pool.

When Little Joe Otter and Billy Mink reached the Smiling Pool, they climbed up on the Big Rock. There Little Joe sulked and sulked, until finally Grandfather Frog asked what the matter was. Little Joe would not tell, but Billy Mink told the whole story. When he told how Buster had been too smart for Little Joe, it tickled him so that Billy had to laugh in spite of himself. So did Grandfather Frog. So did Jerry Muskrat, who had been listening. Of course this made Little Joe angrier than ever. He said a lot of unkind things about Buster Bear and about Billy Mink and Grandfather Frog and Jerry Muskrat, because they had laughed at the smartness of Buster.

"He's nothing but a great big bully and thief!" declared Little Joe.

"Chug-a-rum! He may be a bully, because great big people are very often bullies, and though I have not seen him, I guess Buster Bear is big enough from all I have heard. But I do not see how he is a thief," said Grandfather Frog.

"Did he not catch my fish and eat them?" snapped Little Joe. "Does that not make him a thief?"

"They were no more your fish than mine," protested Billy Mink.

"Well, _our_ fish, then! He stole _our_ fish, if you like that any better. That makes him just as much a thief, does it not?" growled Little Joe.

Grandfather Frog looked up at the happy, round, bright Mr. Sun. He slowly closed and opened one of his big, round eyes. "There comes a silly green fly," he said. "Who does he belong to?"

"Nobody!" said Little Joe quickly. "What do silly green flies have to do with my--I mean _our_ fish?"

"Nothing, nothing at all," replied Grandfather Frog in a soft voice. "I was just hoping that he would come near enough for me to catch him. Then he would belong to me. As long as he does not, he does not belong to anyone. I suppose that if Buster Bear should come along and catch him, he would be stealing from me, according to Little Joe."

"Of course not! What a silly idea! You are getting silly in your old age," Little Joe answered back.

"Can you tell me the difference between the fish that you have not caught and the silly green flies that I have not caught?" asked Grandfather Frog.

Little Joe could not find a word to say.

"You take my advice, Little Joe Otter," continued Grandfather Frog. "Always make friends with those who are bigger and stronger and smarter than you are. You will find it pays."

VI

LITTLE JOE OTTER TAKES GRANDFATHER FROG'S ADVICE

Who makes an enemy a friend, To fear and worry puts an end.

Little Joe Otter found that out when he took Grandfather Frog's advice. He would not have admitted that he was afraid of Buster Bear. No one ever likes to admit being afraid, least of all Little Joe Otter. And really Little Joe has a great deal of courage. Very few of the little people of the Green Forest or the Green Meadows would gladly quarrel with him, for Little Joe is a great fighter when he has to fight. As for all those who live in or along the Laughing Brook or in the Smiling Pool, they let Little Joe have his own way in everything.

Now having your own way too much is a bad thing. It is likely to make you selfish and thoughtless of other people and very hard to get along with. Little Joe Otter had his way too much. Grandfather Frog knew it and shook his head very seriously when Little Joe had been rude to him.

"Too bad. Too bad! Too bad! Chug-a-rum! It is too bad that such a fine young fellow as Little Joe should ruin a good character by such selfish carelessness. Too bad," said he.

So, though he did not let on that it was so, Grandfather Frog really was very pleased when he heard how Buster Bear had been too smart for Little Joe Otter. It made him so happy that he had hard work to keep a straight face. But he did and was as serious and solemn as you please as he advised Little Joe always to make friends with anyone who was bigger and stronger and smarter than he. That was good common sense advice, but Little Joe just made a small sound of dislike and went off declaring that he would get even with Buster Bear yet. Now Little Joe is easy-going and full of fun as a rule. After he had reached home and his anger had cooled off a little, he began to see the joke on himself. He had worked so hard to frighten the fish in the little pools of the Laughing Brook so that Buster Bear should not catch any. But all the time he had been driving them right into Buster's feet. By and by he smiled. It was a little shy smile at first, but at last it grew into a laugh.

"I believe," said Little Joe as he wiped tears of laughter from his eyes, "that Grandfather Frog is right, and that the best thing I can do is to make friends with Buster Bear. I will try it tomorrow morning."

So very early the next morning Little Joe Otter went to the best fishing pool he knew of in the Laughing Brook. There he caught the biggest trout he could find. It was so big and fat that it made Little Joe's mouth water, for you know fat trout are his favorite food. But he did not take so much as one bite. Instead he carefully laid it on an old log where Buster Bear would be sure to see it if he should come along that way. Then he hid near by, where he could watch. Buster was late that morning. It seemed to Little Joe that he never would come. Once he nearly lost the fish. He had turned his head for just a minute, and when he looked back again, the trout was nowhere to be seen. Buster could not have stolen up and taken it, because such a big fellow could not possibly have gotten out of sight again.

Little Joe ran quickly over to the log and looked on the other side. There was the fat trout, and there also was Little Joe's smallest cousin, Shadow the Weasel, who is a great thief and altogether bad. Little Joe jumped at him angrily, but Shadow was too quick and ran away. Little Joe put the fish back on the log and waited. This time he did not take his eyes off it. At last, when he was almost ready to give up, he saw Buster Bear moving slowly along towards the Laughing Brook. Suddenly Buster stopped and smelled the air. One of the Merry Little Breezes had carried the smell of that fat trout over to him. Then he came straight over to where the fish lay, his nose wrinkling, and his eyes shining with pleasure.

"Now I wonder who was so kind as to leave this fine breakfast ready for me," said he out loud.

"Me," said Little Joe in a rather weak voice. "I caught it especially for you."

"Thank you," replied Buster, and his eyes shone more than ever. "I think we are going to be friends."

"I--I hope so," replied Little Joe.

VII

FARMER BROWN'S BOY HAS NO LUCK AT ALL

Farmer Brown's boy walked through the Green Forest, whistling happily. He always whistles when he feels happy, and he always feels happy when he goes fishing. You see, he is just as fond of fishing as is Little Joe Otter or Billy Mink or Buster Bear. And now he was making his way through the Green Forest to the Laughing Brook. He was sure that by the time he had followed it down to the Smiling Pool he would have a fine lot of trout to take home. He knew every pool in the Laughing Brook where the trout love to hide, did Farmer Brown's boy. And it was just the kind of a morning when the trout should be hungry. So he whistled as he walked along, and his whistle was good to hear.

When he reached the first little pool, he put bait on his hook very carefully. Then he kept out of sight of any trout that might be in the little pool. He began to fish. Farmer Brown's boy learned a long time ago that a good fisherman must have a lot of patience. He did not get a bite right away as he had expected. But he was not discouraged at all. He kept very quiet and fished and fished. He waited with patience for a foolish trout to take his hook. But he did not get even a small bite. "Either the trout have lost their hunger or they have grown very wise," said Farmer Brown's boy to himself. After a long time, he moved on to the next little pool.

There the same thing happened. He was very patient, very, very patient. But his patience brought no reward, not even the smallest bite. Farmer Brown's boy walked on to the next pool. There was a puzzled frown on his face with freckles. Such a thing never had happened before. He did not know what to make of it. All the night before, he had dreamed about the delicious dinner of fried trout he would have the next day. And now—well, if he did not catch some trout pretty soon, that splendid dinner would never be anything but a dream.

"If I did not know that nobody else comes fishing here, I should think that somebody had been here this very morning and caught all the fish. Or else they frightened them so that they are all in hiding," he said. He walked on to the next little pool. "I never had such bad luck in all my life before. Hello! What's this?"

There, on the bank beside the little pool, were the heads of three trout. Farmer Brown's boy scowled down at them. He was more puzzled than ever. "Somebody _has_ been fishing here, and they have had better luck than I have," he thought. He looked up the Laughing Brook and down the Laughing Brook and this way and that way. But no one was to be seen. Then he picked up one of the little heads and looked at it sharply. "It was not cut off with a knife; it was bitten off!" he exclaimed. "I wonder now if Billy Mink is the scamp who has spoiled my fun."

After that, he kept a sharp lookout for signs of Billy Mink. He found two or three more trout heads. But he saw no other signs and he caught no fish. This puzzled him more than ever. It did not seem possible that such a little fellow as Billy Mink could have caught or frightened all the fish. It did not seem possible that he could have eaten so many. Besides, he did not remember ever having known Billy to leave heads around that way. Billy sometimes catches more fish than he can eat. But then he usually hides them. The farther he went down the Laughing Brook, the more puzzled Farmer Brown's boy grew. It made him feel very strange. He would have felt still more strange if he had known that all the time two other fishermen who had been before him were watching him. They were laughing quietly to themselves. They were Little Joe Otter and Buster Bear.

VIII

FARMER BROWN'S BOY FEELS HIS HAIR RISE

It was just a sudden odd surprise that made Farmer Brown's boy's hair rise.

That is a funny thing for hair to do—rise up all of a sudden—is it not? But that is just what the hair on Farmer Brown's boy's head did the day he went fishing in the Laughing Brook and had no luck at all. There are just two things that make hair rise—anger and fear. Anger sometimes makes the hair on the back and neck of Bowser the Hound and of some other little people stand up. And you know the hair on the tail of Black Pussy stands on end until her tail looks twice as big as it really is. Both anger and fear make it do that. But there is only one thing that can make the hair on the head of Farmer Brown's boy rise. It is not anger, so of course it must be fear.

It never had happened before. You see, there is not much of anything that Farmer Brown's boy is really afraid of. Perhaps he would not have been afraid this time if it had not been for the surprise of what he found. You see, when he had found the heads of those trout on the bank, he knew right away that someone else had been fishing. That was why he could not catch any. But it did not seem possible that little Billy Mink could have eaten all those trout. Farmer Brown's boy did not once think of Little Joe Otter. So he was very, very much puzzled.

He was turning it all over in his mind and studying what it could mean. Then he came to a little muddy place on the bank of the Laughing Brook. There he saw something that made his eyes look as if they would pop right out of his head. It was right then that he felt his hair rise. Anyway, that is what he said when he told about it afterward. What was it he saw? What do you think? Why, it was a footprint in the soft mud. Yes, Sir, that is what it was, and all it was. But it was the biggest footprint Farmer Brown's boy ever had seen. It looked as if it had been made only a few minutes before. It was the footprint of Buster Bear.

Now Farmer Brown's boy did not know that Buster Bear had come down to the Green Forest to live. He never had heard of a Bear being in the Green Forest. And so he was so surprised that he had hard work to believe his own eyes. He had a strange feeling all over—a little chilly feeling, although it was a warm day. Somehow, he did not feel like meeting Buster Bear. If he had had his terrible gun with him, it might have been different. But he did not, and so he suddenly made up his mind that he did not want to fish any more that day. He had a funny feeling, too, that he was being watched, although he could not see anyone. He _was_ being watched. Little Joe Otter and Buster Bear were watching him and taking the greatest care to keep out of his sight.

All the way home through the Green Forest, Farmer Brown's boy kept looking behind him. He did not draw a long breath until he reached the edge of the Green Forest. He had not run, but he had wanted to.

"Huh!" said Buster Bear to Little Joe Otter, "I believe he was afraid!"

And Buster Bear was just exactly right.

IX

LITTLE JOE OTTER HAS GREAT NEWS TO TELL

Little Joe Otter was full of excitement. He could hardly hold himself still. He felt he had the greatest news to tell since Peter Rabbit first found the tracks of Buster Bear in the Green Forest. He could not keep it to himself one minute longer than he had to. So he hurried to the Smiling Pool. He was sure he would find Billy Mink and Jerry Muskrat and Grandfather Frog and Spotty the Turtle there. BUSTER BEAR BECOMES A HERO

Little Joe Otter told the news at the Smiling Pool. Farmer Brown's boy had run away from Buster Bear without even seeing him. XII

FARMER BROWN'S BOY AND BUSTER BEAR GROW CURIOUS

Ever since it was light enough to see at all, Blacky the Crow had been sitting in the top of the tallest tree on the edge of the Green Forest nearest to Farmer Brown's house. A SURPRISING THING HAPPENS

XVII

BUSTER BEAR GOES BERRYING

Buster Bear often talks to himself when he thinks no one is around to hear. It is a habit. However, it is not a bad habit unless it is carried too far. Any habit becomes bad, if it is carried too far. Suppose you had a secret, a real secret, something that nobody else knew and that you did not want anybody else to know. And suppose you had the habit of talking to yourself. You might, without thinking, you know, tell that secret out loud to yourself. And some one might, just might happen to hear! Then there would not be any secret. That is the way that a habit which is not bad in itself can become bad when it is carried too far.

Now Buster Bear had lived by himself in the Great Woods so long that this habit of talking to himself had grown and grown. He did it just to keep from being lonely. Of course, when he came down to the Green Forest to live, he brought all his habits with him. That is one thing about habits: you always take them with you wherever you go. So Buster brought this habit of talking to himself down to the Green Forest, where he had many more neighbors than he had in the Great Woods.

"Let me see, let me see, what is there to make me want to eat?" said Buster in his deep, low voice. "I find my hunger is not what it ought to be. I need a change. Yes, Sir, I need a change. There is something I ought to have at this time of year, and I have not got it. There is something that I used to have and do not have now. Ha! I know! I need some fresh fruit. That is it: fresh fruit! It must be about berry time now, and I had forgotten all about it. My, my, my, how good some berries would taste! Now if I were back up there in the Great Woods I could have all I could eat. Um-m-m-m! Makes my mouth water just to think of it. There ought to be some up in the Old Pasture. There ought to be a lot of them up there. If I was not afraid that some one would see me, I would go up there."

Buster sighed. Then he sighed again. The more he thought about those berries he felt sure were growing in the Old Pasture, the more he wanted some. It seemed to him that never in all his life had he wanted berries as he did now. He walked about without rest. He was hungry, hungry for berries and nothing else. By and by he began talking to himself again.

"If I was not afraid of being seen, I would go up to the Old Pasture this very minute. It seems as if I could taste those berries." He licked his lips hungrily as he spoke. Then his face became happy. "I know what I'll do! I'll go up there at the very first light of day to-morrow. There is room in it for many people to pick berries all day without even seeing each other, unless they roam about a great deal. You see, the bushes grow very thick there, and you cannot see very far in any direction. Jolly, round, red Mr. Sun had climbed a little way up in the sky by the time Farmer Brown's boy reached the Old Pasture. He was smiling down on all the Great World, and all the Great World seemed to be smiling back. Farmer Brown's boy started to whistle, and then he stopped.

"If I whistle," thought he, "everybody will know just where I am. They will keep out of sight, and I never can get acquainted with folks if they keep out of sight."

You see, Farmer Brown's boy was just beginning to understand something that Peter Rabbit and the other little people of the Green Meadows and the Green Forest learned almost as soon as they learned to walk. That is, if you do not want to be seen, you must not be heard. So he did not whistle as he felt like doing. He tried not to make a bit of noise as he followed an old cow-path towards a place where he knew the berries grew thick and oh, so big. All the time he kept his eyes wide open, and he kept his ears open too.

That is how he happened to hear a little cry, a very faint little cry. If he had been whistling, he would not have heard it at all. He stopped to listen. He never had heard a cry just like it before. At first he could not make out just what it was or where it came from. But one thing he was sure of, and that was that it was a cry of fear. He stood perfectly still and listened with all his might. There it was again--"Help! Help! Help"--and it was very faint and sounded terribly afraid. He waited a minute or two, but heard nothing more. Then he put down his pail and began a hurried look here, there, and everywhere. He was sure that it had come from somewhere on the ground. So he looked behind bushes and looked behind logs and stones. Then just as he had about given up hope of finding where it came from, he went around a little turn in the old cow-path. There right in front of him was little Mr. Gartersnake, and what do you think he was doing? Well, I do not like to tell you, but he was trying to swallow one of the children of Stickytoes the Tree Toad. Of course Farmer Brown's Boy did not let him. He made little Mr. Gartersnake set Master Stickytoes free and held Mr. Gartersnake until Master Stickytoes was safely out of reach.

XIX

BUSTER BEAR HAS A FINE TIME

Buster Bear was having the finest time he had had since he came down from the Great Woods to live in the Green Forest. To be sure, he was not in the Green Forest now, but he was not far from it. He was in the Old Pasture, one edge of which touches one edge of the Green Forest. And where do you think he was, in the Old Pasture? Why, right in the middle of the biggest patch of the biggest blueberries he ever had seen in all his life! Now if there is any one thing that Buster Bear had rather have above another, it is all the berries he can eat, unless it be honey. Nothing can quite equal honey in Buster's mind. But next to honey give him berries. He is not particular what kind of berries. Raspberries, blackberries, or blueberries, either kind, will make him perfectly happy.

"Um-m-m, my, my, but these are good!" he mumbled in his deep grumbly-rumbly voice, as he sat on his haunches stripping off the berries greedily. His little eyes twinkled with enjoyment, and he did not mind at all if now and then he got leaves, and some green berries in his mouth with the big ripe berries. He did not try to get them out. Oh, my, no! He just chomped them all up together and patted his stomach from sheer delight. Now Buster had reached the Old Pasture just as jolly, round, red Mr. Sun had crept out of bed. He had fully made up his mind that he would be back in the Green Forest before Mr. Sun had climbed very far up in the blue, blue sky. You see, big as he is and strong as he is, Buster Bear is very shy and bashful. He has no desire to meet Farmer Brown, or Farmer Brown's boy, or any other of those two-legged creatures called men. It seems funny but he actually is afraid of them. And he had a feeling that he was a great deal more likely to meet one of them in the Old Pasture than deep in the Green Forest.

So when he started to look for berries, he decided he would eat what he could very quickly. He wanted to get back to the Green Forest before Farmer Brown's boy was out of bed. But when he found those berries, he was so hungry that he forgot his fears and everything else. They tasted so good that he just had to eat and eat and eat. Now you know that Buster is a very big fellow. It takes a lot to fill him up. He kept eating and eating and eating. The more he ate, the more he wanted. You know how it is. So he wandered from one patch of berries to another in the Old Pasture. He never once thought of the time. Somehow, time is the hardest thing in the world to remember when you are having a good time.

Jolly, round, red Mr. Sun climbed higher and higher in the blue, blue sky. He looked down on all the Great World and saw all that was going on. He saw Buster Bear in the Old Pasture. He smiled as he saw what a perfectly wonderful time Buster was having. And he saw something else in the Old Pasture that made his smile still broader. He saw Farmer Brown's boy filling a great metal container with blueberries. He knew that Farmer Brown's boy did not know that Buster Bear was anywhere about. He knew that Buster Bear did not know that Farmer Brown's boy was anywhere about. Somehow he felt very sure that he would see something funny happen if they should chance to meet.

"Um-m-m, um-m-m," said Buster Bear in a low voice with his mouth full, as he moved along to another patch of berries. And then he gave a little quick breath of surprise and joy. Right in front of him was a bright thing just full of the finest, biggest, bluest berries! There were no leaves or green ones there. Buster moved his eyes quickly again and again and looked again. No, he was not dreaming. They were real berries. All he had got to do was to help himself. Buster looked sharply at the bright thing that held the berries. It seemed perfectly safe. He reached out a big foot and pushed it gently. It tipped over and spilled out a lot of the berries. Yes, it was perfectly safe. Buster gave a little sigh of pure happiness. He would eat those berries to the last one. Then he would go home to the Green Forest.

XX

BUSTER BEAR CARRIES OFF THE PAIL OF FARMER BROWN'S BOY

The question is, did Buster Bear steal Farmer Brown's boy's pail? To steal is to take something which belongs to some one else. There is no doubt that he stole the berries that were in the pail when he found it, for he ate them on purpose. He knew well enough that some one must have picked them--for whoever heard of blueberries growing in metal containers? So there is no doubt that when Buster took them, he stole them. But with the pail it was different. He took the pail, but he did not mean to take it. In fact, he did not want that pail at all.

You see it was this way. When Buster found that big metal container full to the top of wonderful berries in the shade of that big bush in the Old Pasture, he did not stop to think whether or not he had a right to them. Buster is so fond of berries that from the very second that his eager little eyes saw that full container, he forgot everything but the big meal that was waiting for him right under his very nose. He did not think anything about the right or wrong of helping himself. There before him were more berries than he had ever seen together at one time in all his life. All he had to do was to eat and eat and eat. And that is just what he did do. Of course he upset the pail, but he did not mind a little thing like that. When he had eaten up all the berries that rolled out, he pushed his nose into the pail to get all that were left in it. Just then he heard a little noise, as if some one were coming. He threw up his head to listen. Somehow, he never did know just how, the handle of the pail slipped back over his ears and caught there.

This was bad enough, but to make matters worse, just at that very minute he heard a high, angry voice shout, "Hi, there! Get out of there!" He did not need to be told whose voice that was. It was the voice of Farmer Brown's boy. Right then and there Buster Bear nearly had a fit. There was that awful pail fast over his head so that he could not see a thing. Of course, that meant that he could not run away. That was the thing of all things he most wanted to do. For big as he is and strong as he is, Buster is very shy and quiet when human beings are around. He made low angry sounds and high sad sounds. He tried to back out of the pail and could not. He tried to shake it off and could not. He tried to pull it off, but somehow he could not get hold of it. Then there was another yell. If Buster had not been so afraid himself, he might have recognized that second yell as one of fear, for that is what it was. You see Farmer Brown's boy had just discovered Buster Bear. When he had yelled the first time, he had supposed that it was one of the young cattle who live in the Old Pasture all summer. But when he saw Buster, he was just as badly afraid as Buster himself. In fact, he was too surprised and afraid even to run. After that second yell he just stood still and stared.

Buster hit at that awful thing on his head more wildly than ever. Suddenly it slipped off, so that he could see. He gave one afraid look at Farmer Brown's boy. Then with a mighty "Woof!" he started for the Green Forest as fast as his legs could take him. And this was very fast indeed, let me tell you. He did not stop to pick out a path. He just crashed through the bushes as if they were nothing at all, just nothing at all. But the funniest thing of all is this--he took that pail with him! Yes, Sir, Buster Bear ran away with the big metal container of Farmer Brown's boy! You see when it slipped off his head, the handle was still around his neck. There he was running away with a pail hanging from his neck! He did not want it. He would have given anything to get rid of it. But he took it because he could not help it. And that brings us back to the question, did Buster steal Farmer Brown's boy's pail? What do you think?

XXI

SAMMY JAY MAKES THINGS WORSE FOR BUSTER BEAR

"Bad man, bad man, bad man! Bad man, bad man, bad man!" Sammy Jay was shouting as loud as he could. He was following Buster Bear across the Old Pasture towards the Green Forest. He had never shouted so loud before. His voice had never sounded so full of strong feeling. The little people of the Green Forest, the Green Meadows, and the Smiling Pool often hear Sammy cry bad man. So they usually think very little about it. But every single one who heard Sammy this morning stopped whatever he was doing. They raised their ears to listen.

CHAPTER 5. Chapter 1（5）

Sammy's cousin, Blacky the Crow, happened to be flying along the edge of the Old Pasture. The minute he heard Sammy's voice, he turned and flew over to see what it was all about. As soon as he caught sight of Buster Bear running for the Green Forest as hard as he could, he understood what had made Sammy so worked up. He was so surprised that he almost forgot to keep his wings moving. Buster Bear had what looked to Blacky very much like a tin pail hanging from his neck! No wonder Sammy was so worked up. Blacky beat his wings hard and started after Sammy.

And so they reached the edge of the Green Forest. Buster Bear was running as hard as he could. Sammy Jay was flying just behind him and screaming, "Thief, thief, thief!" at the top of his lungs. Behind him was Blacky the Crow, trying to catch up and yelling as loud as he could, "Caw, caw, caw! Come on, everybody! Come on! Come on!"

Poor Buster! It was bad enough to be almost scared to death as he had been up in the Old Pasture when the pail had caught over his head just as Farmer Brown's boy had yelled at him. Then the handle of the pail slipped down around his neck. He could not get rid of the pail and had to take it with him as he ran. That made a bad matter worse. Now all his neighbors of the Green Forest saw him in such a fix and made fun of him. That was more than he could stand. He felt shamed. That is just another way of saying put to shame. Yes, Sir, Buster felt that he was shamed in the eyes of his neighbors. He wanted nothing so much as to get away by himself, where no one could see him, and try to get rid of that awful pail. But Buster is so big that it is not easy for him to find a hiding place. So when he reached the Green Forest, he kept right on to the deepest, darkest, most lonely part. He went slowly under the thickest hemlock-tree he could find.

But it was of no use. The sharp eyes of Sammy Jay and Blacky the Crow saw him. They actually flew into the very tree under which he was hiding, and how they did scream! Pretty soon Ol' Mistah Buzzard came dropping down out of the blue, blue sky. He took a seat on a handy dead tree, where he could see all that went on. Ol' Mistah Buzzard began to grin as soon as he saw that tin pail on Buster's neck. Then came others: Redtail the Hawk, Scrapper the Kingbird, Redwing the Blackbird, Drummer the Woodpecker, Welcome Robin, Tommy Tit the Chickadee, Jenny Wren, Redeye the Vireo, and ever so many more. They came from the Old Orchard, the Green Meadows, and even down by the Smiling Pool. The voices of Sammy Jay and Blacky the Crow carried far. At the sound of them everybody hurried over, sure that something exciting was going on.

Presently Buster heard light footsteps. Peeping out, he saw Billy Mink and Peter Rabbit and Jumper the Hare and Prickly Porky and Reddy Fox and Jimmy Skunk. Even shy little Whitefoot the Wood Mouse was where he could peer out and see without being seen. Of course, Chatterer the Red Squirrel and Happy Jack the Gray Squirrel were there. There they all sat in a great circle around him, each where he felt safe, but where he could see. Every one of them was laughing and making fun of Buster.

"Thief, thief, thief!" screamed Sammy until his throat was sore. The worst of it was that Buster knew that everybody knew that it was true. That awful pail was proof of it.

"I wish I never had thought of berries," growled Buster to himself.

XXII

BUSTER BEAR HAS A FIT OF TEMPER

A temper is a bad, bad thing When once it gets away. There's nothing quite at all like it To spoil a pleasant day.

Buster Bear was in a terrible temper. Yes, Sir, Buster Bear was having the worst fit of temper ever seen in the Green Forest. The worst part of it all was that all his neighbors of the Green Forest and a whole lot from the Green Meadows and the Smiling Pool were also there to see it. It is bad enough to give way to temper when you are all alone, and there is no one to watch you. But when you let temper get the best of you right where others see you, oh, dear, dear, it certainly is a sorry sight.

Now usually Buster is one of the most easy-going persons in the world. It takes a great deal to stir his temper. He is not one tenth so quick tempered as Chatterer the Red Squirrel, or Sammy Jay, or Reddy Fox. But when his temper is stirred and gets away from him, then watch out! It seemed to Buster that he had had all that he could stand that day and a little more. First had come the fear back there in the Old Pasture. Then the pail had slipped down behind his ears and held fast. So he had run all the way to the Green Forest with it hanging about his neck. This was bad enough, for he knew just how funny he must look. Besides, it was very uncomfortable. But to have Sammy Jay call everybody within hearing to come and see him was more than he could stand. It seemed to Buster as if everybody who lives in the Green Forest, on the Green Meadows, or around the Smiling Brook was sitting around his hiding place, laughing and making fun of him. It was more than any self-respecting Bear could stand.

With a roar of anger Buster Bear charged out of his hiding place. He rushed this way and that way! He roared with all his might! He was very terrible to see. Those who could fly, flew. Those who could climb, climbed. And those who were fast of foot, ran. A few who could neither fly nor climb nor run fast hid and lay shaking and trembling for fear that Buster would find them. In less time than it takes to tell about it, Buster was alone. At least, he couldn't see any one.

[Illustration: Those who could fly, flew. Those who could climb, climbed. _Page 112._]

Then he let out his anger on the tin pail. He hit at it and pulled at it, all the time making angry sounds. He lay down and scratched at it with his back feet. At last the handle broke, and he was free! He shook himself. Then he jumped on the pail that could not fight back. With a blow of a big foot he sent it hitting against a tree. He tried to bite it. Then he once more began knocking it this way and that way, until it was beaten flat, and no one would ever have guessed that it had once been a pail.

Then, and only then, did Buster recover his usual good nature. Little by little, as he thought it all over, a look of shame came into his face. "I--I guess it was not the fault of that thing. I should have known enough to keep my head out of it," he said slowly and with thought.

"You got no more than you deserve for stealing Farmer Brown's boy's berries," said Sammy Jay, who had come back and was looking on from the top of a tree. "You should know by this time that no good comes of stealing."

Buster Bear looked up and grinned, and there was a light in his eyes. "You should know, Sammy Jay," said he. "I hope you will always remember it."

"Thief, thief, thief!" screamed Sammy, and flew away.

XXIII

FARMER BROWN'S BOY LUNCHES ON BERRIES

When things go wrong in spite of you To smile is the best thing you can do-- To smile and say, "I'm very glad They are no worse; they're not so bad!"

That is what Farmer Brown's boy said when he found that Buster Bear had stolen the berries he had worked so hard to pick and then had run off with the pail. You see, Farmer Brown's boy is learning to be something of a thinker, one of those people who accept bad things with a good heart and right away see how they are better than they might have been. When he had first heard some one in the small trees where he had hidden his pail of berries, he had been very sure that it was one of the cows or young cattle who live in the Old Pasture during the summer. He had been afraid that they might foolishly kick over the pail and spill the berries, and he had hurried to drive whoever it was away. It had not entered his head that it could be anybody who would eat those berries.

When he had yelled and Buster Bear had suddenly appeared, struggling to get off the pail which had caught over his head, Farmer Brown's boy had been too afraid to even move. Then he had seen Buster run away through the small trees even more afraid than he was, and right away his courage had begun to come back.

"If he is so afraid of me, I guess I do not need to be afraid of him," said he. "I've lost my berries, but it is worth it to find out that he is afraid of me. There are plenty more on the small trees, and all I've got to do is to pick them. It might be worse."

He walked over to the place where the pail had been, and then he remembered that when Buster ran away he had carried the pail with him, hanging about his neck. He made a little sound with his lips. It was a funny little sound of disappointment as he realized that he had nothing in which to put more berries, even if he picked them. "It's worse than I thought," cried he. "That bear has cheated me out of that berry pie my mother promised me." Then he began to laugh, as he thought of how funny Buster Bear had looked with the pail about his neck, and then because, you know he is learning to be a thinker, he once more repeated, "It might have been worse. Yes, indeed, it might have been worse. That bear might have tried to eat me instead of the berries. I guess I'll go eat that lunch I left back by the spring, and then I'll go home. I can pick berries some other day."

Laughing happily over Buster Bear's great fear, Farmer Brown's boy walked back to the spring where he had left two thick sandwiches on a flat stone when he started to save his pail of berries. "My, but those sandwiches will taste good," thought he. "I'm glad they are big and thick. I never was hungrier in my life. Hello!" This he said right out loud, for he had just come in sight of the flat stone where the sandwiches should have been, and they were not there. No, Sir, there was not so much as a tiny piece left of those two thick sandwiches. You see, Old Man Coyote had found them and eaten them up while Farmer Brown's boy was away.

But Farmer Brown's boy did not know anything about Old Man Coyote. He rubbed his eyes and stared everywhere, even up in the trees, as if he thought those sandwiches might be hanging up there. They had disappeared as completely as if they never had been, and Old Man Coyote had taken care to leave no sign of his visit. Farmer Brown's boy gaped foolishly this way and that way. Then, instead of growing angry, a slow smile came over his face with its freckles. "I guess some one else was hungry too," he muttered. "Wonder who it was? Guess this Old Pasture is no place for me to-day. I'll fill up on berries and then I'll go home."

So Farmer Brown's boy made his lunch on blueberries and then rather shyly he started for home to tell of all the strange things that had happened to him in the Old Pasture. Two or three times, as he walked along, he stopped to scratch his head with thought. "I guess," said he at last, "that I'm not so smart as I thought I was, and I've got a lot to learn yet."

This is the end of the adventures of Buster Bear in this book because--guess why. Because Old Mr. Toad insists that I must write a book about his adventures, and Old Mr. Toad is such a good friend of all of us that I am going to do it.

THE END
