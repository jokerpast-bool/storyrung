CHAPTER 1. Why the Princess Has a Story About Her

Why the Princess Has a Story About Her

There was once a little princess. Her father was king over a great country full of mountains and valleys. His palace was built upon one of the mountains. It was very grand and beautiful. The princess, whose name was Irene, was born there. But she was sent soon after her birth to be raised by country people. Her mother was not very strong. So she went to a large house on the side of another mountain. The house was half castle and half farmhouse. It stood about half-way between the base and the peak.

The princess was a sweet little creature. At the time my story begins she was about eight years old, I think. But she got older very fast. Her face was fair and pretty. Her eyes were like two bits of night sky, each with a star dissolved in the blue. You would have thought those eyes must have known they came from there. They were turned up in that direction so often. The ceiling of her nursery was blue, with stars in it. It was as like the sky as they could make it. But I doubt if she ever saw the real sky with the stars in it. I had better mention the reason at once.

These mountains were full of hollow places underneath. There were huge caverns and winding ways. Some had water running through them. Some shone with all colours of the rainbow when a light was taken in. Not much would have been known about them if there had not been mines there. These were great deep pits, with long galleries and passages running off from them. They had been dug to get at the ore that filled the mountains. In the course of digging, the miners came upon many of these natural caverns. A few of them had far-off openings out on the side of a mountain, or into a ravine.

Now in these subterranean caverns lived a strange race of beings. Some called them gnomes, some kobolds, some goblins. There was a legend in the country that at one time they lived above ground. They were very like other people then. But for some reason or other, the king had laid taxes on them that they thought too severe. Or he had required observances of them they did not like. Or he had begun to treat them with more severity in some way and impose stricter laws. There were different legendary theories about it. The consequence was that they had all disappeared from the face of the country. According to the legend, however, they had not gone to some other country. They had all taken refuge in the subterranean caverns. From there they never came out but at night. And then they seldom showed themselves in any numbers. They never showed themselves to many people at once. It was only in the least frequented and most difficult parts of the mountains that they were said to gather even at night in the open air. Those who had caught sight of any of them said they had greatly changed in the course of generations. And no wonder, since they lived away from the sun, in cold and wet and dark places. They were now not ordinarily ugly. They were either absolutely hideous, or ludicrously grotesque in both face and form. There was no invention, they said, of the most lawless imagination expressed by pen or pencil, that could surpass the extravagance of their appearance. But I suspect those who said so had mistaken some of their animal companions for the goblins themselves. I will say more about that by and by. The goblins themselves were not so far removed from the human as such a description would imply. And as they grew misshapen in body, they had grown in knowledge and cleverness. Now they were able to do things no mortal could see the possibility of. But as they grew in cunning, they grew in mischief. Their great delight was to annoy the people who lived in the open-air storey above them, in every way they could think of. They had enough of affection left for each other to keep them from being absolutely cruel for cruelty's sake to those that came in their way. But still they so heartily cherished the ancestral grudge against those who occupied their former possessions. They felt it especially against the descendants of the king who had caused their expulsion. So they sought every opportunity of tormenting them in ways that were as odd as their inventors. And although dwarfed and misshapen, they had strength equal to their cunning. In the process of time they had got a king and a government of their own. Beyond their own simple affairs, their chief business was to devise trouble for their neighbours. It will now be pretty evident why the little princess had never seen the sky at night. They were much too afraid of the goblins to let her out of the house then, even in company with ever so many attendants. And they had good reason, as we shall see by and by.

CHAPTER 2. The Princess Loses Herself

The Princess Loses Herself

I have said that the Princess Irene was about eight years old when my story begins. And this is how it begins.

One very wet day, the mountain was covered with mist. The mist kept gathering together into raindrops and pouring down on the roofs of the great old house. From there it fell in a line of water from the eaves all around the house. The princess could not go out, of course. She got very tired. She was so tired that even her toys could no longer amuse her. You would wonder at that if I had time to describe to you one half of the toys she had. But then, you would not have the toys themselves, and that makes all the difference. You cannot get tired of a thing before you have it. It was a picture worth seeing, though: the princess sitting in the nursery with the sky ceiling over her head, at a great table covered with her toys. If an artist would like to draw this, I should advise him not to meddle with the toys. I am afraid of attempting to describe them, and I think he had better not try to draw them. He had better not. He can do a thousand things I cannot, but I do not think he could draw those toys. No man could better make the princess herself than he could, though. She leaned with her back bowed into the back of the chair. Her head hung down, and her hands were in her lap. She was very miserable, as she would say herself. She did not even know what she would like, except to go out and get thoroughly wet. She wanted to catch a particularly nice cold, and have to go to bed and take gruel. The next moment after you see her sitting there, her nurse goes out of the room.

Even that is a change. The princess wakes up a little and looks about her. Then she tumbles off her chair and runs out of the door. It is not the same door the nurse went out of. It is one which opened at the foot of a curious old stair of worm-eaten oak. It looked as if never anyone had set foot upon it. She had once before been up six steps. That was sufficient reason, on such a day, for trying to find out what was at the top of it.

Up and up she ran. It seemed such a long way to her! At last she came to the top of the third flight. There she found the landing was the end of a long passage. Into this she ran. It was full of doors on each side. There were so many that she did not care to open any. She ran on to the end, where she turned into another passage, also full of doors. She turned twice more. She still saw doors and only doors about her. Then she began to get frightened. It was so silent! And all those doors must hide rooms with nobody in them! That was dreadful. Also the rain made a great trampling noise on the roof. She turned and started at full speed back for the stairs and her safe nursery. Her little footsteps echoed through the sounds of the rain. So she thought, but she had lost herself long ago. It does not follow that she was lost, because she had lost herself, though.

She ran for some distance and turned several times. Then she began to be afraid. Very soon she was sure that she had lost the way back. Rooms everywhere, and no stair! Her little heart beat as fast as her little feet ran. A lump of tears was growing in her throat. But she was too eager and perhaps too frightened to cry for some time. At last her hope failed her. Nothing but passages and doors everywhere! She threw herself on the floor and burst into a wailing cry broken by sobs.

She did not cry long, however. She was as brave as could be expected of a princess of her age. After a good cry, she got up and brushed the dust from her frock. Oh, what old dust it was! Then she wiped her eyes with her hands. Princesses do not always have their handkerchiefs in their pockets, any more than some other little girls I know of. Next, like a true princess, she resolved to go wisely to work to find her way back. She would walk through the passages and look in every direction for the stair. This she did, but without success. She went over the same ground again and again without knowing it, for the passages and doors were all alike. At last, in a corner, through a half-open door, she did see a stair. But alas! it went the wrong way. Instead of going down, it went up. Frightened as she was, however, she could not help wishing to see where yet further the stair could lead. It was very narrow and so steep that she went on like a four-legged creature on her hands and feet.

CHAPTER 3. The Princess and--We Shall See Who

The Princess and--We Shall See Who

When she came to the top, she found herself in a little square place. It had three doors. Two were opposite each other, and one was opposite the top of the stair. She stood for a moment. She had no idea in her little head what to do next. But as she stood, she began to hear a strange humming sound. Could it be the rain? No. It was much softer than the sound of the rain. It was even more steady than the rain, which now she hardly heard. The low sweet humming sound went on. Sometimes it stopped for a little while and then began again. It was more like the hum of a very happy bee. That bee had found a rich well of honey in some round flower. That was the best thing I can think of at this moment. Where could it come from? She put her ear first to one of the doors to listen if it was there. Then she put her ear to another. When she put her ear against the third door, there could be no doubt where it came from. It must be from something in that room. What could it be? She was rather afraid. But her curiosity was stronger than her fear. She opened the door very gently and looked in. What do you think she saw? A very old lady who sat spinning.

Perhaps you will wonder how the princess could tell that the old lady was an old lady. I tell you that she was beautiful, and her skin was smooth and white. I will tell you more. Her hair was combed back from her forehead and face. It hung loose far down and all over her back. That is not much like an old lady--is it? Ah! but it was white almost as snow. And although her face was so smooth, her eyes looked so wise. You could not have helped seeing she must be old. The princess did think her very old indeed. She could not have told you why. She thought she was quite fifty, she said to herself. But she was rather older than that, as you shall hear.

While the princess stared, confused, with her head just inside the door, the old lady lifted hers. She said, in a sweet, but old and rather shaky voice, which mixed very pleasantly with the continued hum of her wheel:

'Come in, my dear; come in. I am glad to see you.'

That the princess was a real princess you might see now quite plainly. She did not hold on to the handle of the door, and stare without moving. I have known some do that who ought to have been princesses but were only rather common little girls. She did as she was told. She stepped inside the door at once, and shut it gently behind her.

'Come to me, my dear,' said the old lady.

And again the princess did as she was told. She approached the old lady--rather slowly, I confess--but did not stop until she stood by her side. She looked up in her face with her blue eyes and the two melted stars in them.

'Why, what have you been doing with your eyes, child?' asked the old lady.

'Crying,' answered the princess.

'Why, child?'

'Because I could not find my way down again.'

'But you could find your way up.'

'Not at first--not for a long time.'

'But your face is streaked like the back of a zebra. Had you no handkerchief to wipe your eyes with?'

'No.'

'Then why did you not come to me to wipe them for you?'

'Please, I did not know you were here. I will next time.'

'There's a good child!' said the old lady.

Then she stopped her wheel, and rose. She went out of the room, and returned with a little silver basin and a soft white towel. With these she washed and wiped the bright little face. And the princess thought her hands were so smooth and nice!

When she carried away the basin and towel, the little princess wondered to see how straight and tall she was. Although she was so old, she did not stoop a bit. She was dressed in black velvet with thick white heavy-looking lace about it. And on the black dress her hair shone like silver. There was hardly any more furniture in the room than there might have been in that of the poorest old woman who made her bread by her spinning. There was no carpet on the floor. There was no table anywhere. There was nothing but the spinning-wheel and the chair beside it. When she came back, she sat down and without a word began her spinning once more. Irene, who had never seen a spinning-wheel, stood by her side and looked on. When the old lady had got her thread fairly going again, she said to the princess, but without looking at her:

'Do you know my name, child?'

'No, I do not know it,' answered the princess.

'My name is Irene.'

'That is my name!' cried the princess.

'I know that. I let you have mine. I have not got your name. You have got mine.'

'How can that be?' asked the princess, confused. 'I have always had my name.'

'Your papa, the king, asked me if I had any objection to your having it. And, of course, I had not. I let you have it with pleasure.'

'It was very kind of you to give me your name--and such a pretty one,' said the princess.

'Oh, not so very kind!' said the old lady. 'A name is one of those things one can give away and keep all the same. I have a good many such things. Would you not like to know who I am, child?'

'Yes, that I should--very much.'

'I am your great-great-grandmother,' said the lady.

'What is that?' asked the princess.

'I am your father's mother's father's mother.'

'Oh, dear! I cannot understand that,' said the princess.

'I dare say not. I did not expect you would. But that is no reason why I should not say it.'

'Oh, no!' answered the princess.

'I will explain it all to you when you are older,' the lady went on. 'But you will be able to understand this much now: I came here to take care of you.'

'Is it long since you came? Was it yesterday? Or was it today, because it was so wet that I could not get out?'

'I have been here ever since you came yourself.'

'What a long time!' said the princess. 'I do not remember it at all.'

'No. I suppose not.'

'But I never saw you before.'

'No. But you shall see me again.'

'Do you live in this room always?'

'I do not sleep in it. I sleep on the opposite side of the landing. I sit here most of the day.'

'I should not like it. My nursery is much prettier. You must be a queen too, if you are my great big grand-mother.'

'Yes, I am a queen.'

'Where is your crown, then?' 'In my bedroom.'

'I should like to see it.'

'You shall some day--not today.'

'I wonder why nursie never told me.'

'Nursie does not know. She never saw me.'

'But somebody knows that you are in the house?'

'No; nobody.'

'How do you get your dinner, then?'

'I keep some birds--of a kind.'

'Where do you keep them?'

'I will show you.'

'And who makes the chicken soup for you?'

'I never kill any of MY chickens.'

'Then I do not understand.'

'What did you have for breakfast this morning?' asked the lady.

'Oh! I had bread and milk, and an egg--I think you eat their eggs.'

'Yes, that is it. I eat their eggs.'

'Is that what makes your hair so white?'

'No, my dear. It is old age. I am very old.'

'I thought so. Are you fifty?'

'Yes--more than that.'

'Are you a hundred?'

'Yes--more than that. I am too old for you to guess. Come and see my chickens.'

Again she stopped her spinning. She rose, took the princess by the hand, led her out of the room, and opened the door opposite the steps. The princess expected to see a lot of chickens, but instead of that, she saw the blue sky first, and then the roofs of the house. On the roofs were many of the loveliest birds, mostly white, but of all colors. They walked about, made bows to each other, and talked in a language she could not understand. She hit her hands together with joy, and up rose such a beating of wings that she in her turn was surprised.

'You have scared my birds,' said the old lady, smiling.

'And they have scared me,' said the princess, smiling too. 'But what very nice birds! Are the eggs nice?'

'Yes, very nice.' 'What a small egg-spoon you must have! Would it not be better to keep chickens, and get bigger eggs?'

'How should I feed them, though?'

'I see,' said the princess. 'The birds feed themselves. They have wings.'

'Just so. If they could not fly, I could not eat their eggs.'

'But how do you get to the eggs? Where are their nests?'

The lady took hold of a little loop of string in the wall at the side of the door. She lifted a cover and showed a great many small holes with nests, some with young ones and some with eggs in them. The birds came in at the other side, and she took out the eggs on this side. She closed it again quickly, so the young ones would not be scared.

'Oh, what a nice way!' cried the princess. 'Will you give me an egg to eat? I am rather hungry.'

'I will some day, but now you must go back, or your nurse will be sad about you. I think she is looking for you everywhere.'

'Except here,' answered the princess. 'Oh, how surprised she will be when I tell her about my great big grand-grand-mother!'

'Yes, that she will!' said the old lady with a strange smile. 'Be sure you tell her all about it exactly.'

'That I will. Please will you take me back to her?'

'I cannot go all the way, but I will take you to the top of the steps, and then you must run down quite fast into your own room.'

The little princess put her hand in the old lady's. The old lady looked this way and that, and brought her to the top of the first step. Then she brought her to the bottom of the second, and did not leave her until she saw her half-way down the third. When she heard the cry of her nurse's joy at finding her, she turned and walked up the steps again. She went very fast indeed for such a very great grandmother, and sat down to her spinning with another strange smile on her sweet old face.

About this spinning of hers I will tell you more another time.

Guess what she was spinning.

CHAPTER 4. What the Nurse Thought of It

What the Nurse Thought of It

'Why, where can you have been, princess?' asked the nurse, taking her in her arms. 'It is very unkind of you to hide away so long. I began to be afraid--' Here she stopped herself.

'What were you afraid of, nursie?' asked the princess.

'Never mind,' she answered. 'Perhaps I will tell you another day. Now tell me where you have been.'

'I have been up a long way to see my very great, huge, old grandmother,' said the princess.

'What do you mean by that?' asked the nurse, who thought she was making fun.

'I mean that I have been a long way up and up to see my GREAT grandmother. Ah, nursie, you do not know what a beautiful mother of grandmothers I have got upstairs. She is such an old lady, with such lovely white hair--as white as my silver cup. Now, when I think of it, I think her hair must be silver.'

'What silly talk you are saying, princess!' said the nurse.

'I am not saying silly talk,' returned Irene, rather hurt. 'I will tell you all about her. She is much taller than you, and much prettier.'

'Oh, I dare say!' remarked the nurse.

'And she lives upon pigeons' eggs.'

'Most likely,' said the nurse.

'And she sits in an empty room, spin-spinning all day long.'

'Not a doubt of it,' said the nurse.

'And she keeps her crown in her bedroom.'

'Of course--quite the proper place to keep her crown in. She wears it in bed, I will be bound.'

'She did not say that. And I do not think she does. That would not be comfortable--would it? I do not think my papa wears his crown for a night-cap. Does he, nursie?'

'I never asked him. I dare say he does.'

'And she has been there ever since I came here--ever so many years.'

'Anybody could have told you that,' said the nurse, who did not believe a word Irene was saying.

'Why did you not tell me, then?'

'There was no need. You could make it all up for yourself.'

'You do not believe me, then!' cried the princess, surprised and angry, as she well might be.

'Did you expect me to believe you, princess?' asked the nurse coldly. 'I know princesses are in the habit of telling make-believes, but you are the first I ever heard of who expected to have them believed,' she added, seeing that the child was strangely in earnest.

The princess burst into tears.

'Well, I must say,' remarked the nurse, now thoroughly angry with her for crying, 'it is not at all becoming in a princess to tell stories and expect to be believed just because she is a princess.'

'But it is quite true, I tell you.'

'You have dreamt it, then, child.'

'No, I did not dream it. I went upstairs, and I lost myself, and if I had not found the beautiful lady, I should never have found myself.'

'Oh, I dare say!'

'Well, you just come up with me, and see if I am not telling the truth.'

'Indeed I have other work to do. It is your dinnertime, and I will not have any more such silly talk.'

The princess wiped her eyes, and her face grew so hot that they were soon quite dry. She sat down to her dinner, but ate next to nothing. Not to be believed does not at all agree with princesses: for a real princess cannot tell a lie. So all the afternoon she did not speak a word. Only when the nurse spoke to her, she answered her, for a real princess is never rude--even when she does well to be hurt.

Of course the nurse was not comfortable in her mind--not that she suspected the least truth in Irene's story, but that she loved her dearly, and was angry with herself for having been cross to her. She thought her crossness was the cause of the princess's unhappiness, and had no idea that she was really and deeply hurt at not being believed. But, as it became more and more plain during the evening in her every motion and look, that, although she tried to amuse herself with her toys, her heart was too angry and troubled to enjoy them, her nurse's discomfort grew and grew. When bedtime came, she undressed and laid her down, but the child, instead of holding up her little mouth to be kissed, turned away from her and lay still. Then nursie's heart gave way altogether, and she began to cry. At the sound of her first sob the princess turned again, and held her face to kiss her as usual. But the nurse had her handkerchief to her eyes, and did not see the movement.

'Nursie,' said the princess, 'why will you not believe me?'

'Because I cannot believe you,' said the nurse, getting angry again.

'Ah! then, you cannot help it,' said Irene, 'and I will not be angry with you any more. I will give you a kiss and go to sleep.'

'You little angel!' cried the nurse, and caught her out of bed, and walked about the room with her in her arms, kissing and hugging her.

'You will let me take you to see my dear old great big grandmother, will you not?' said the princess, as she laid her down again.

'And you will not say I am ugly, any more--will you, princess?' 'Nursie, I never said you were ugly. What can you mean?'

'Well, if you did not say it, you meant it.'

'Indeed, I never did.'

'You said I was not so pretty as that--'

'As my beautiful grandmother--yes, I did say that; and I say it again, for it is quite true.'

'Then I do think you are unkind!' said the nurse, and put her handkerchief to her eyes again.

'Nursie, dear, everybody cannot be as beautiful as every other body, you know. You are very nice-looking, but if you had been as beautiful as my grandmother--'

'Bother your grandmother!' said the nurse.

'Nurse, that is very rude. You are not fit to be spoken to till you can behave better.'

The princess turned away once more, and again the nurse was ashamed of herself.

'I am sure I beg your pardon, princess,' she said, though still in a hurt tone. But the princess let the tone pass, and heeded only the words.

'You will not say it again, I am sure,' she answered, once more turning towards her nurse. 'I was only going to say that if you had been twice as nice-looking as you are, some king or other would have married you, and then what would have become of me?'

'You are an angel!' repeated the nurse, again embracing her. 'Now,' insisted Irene, 'you will come and see my grandmother--will you not?'

'I will go with you anywhere you like, my cherub,' she answered; and in two minutes the weary little princess was fast asleep.

CHAPTER 5. The Princess Lets Well Alone

The Princess Lets Well Alone

When she woke the next morning, the first thing she heard was the rain still falling. Indeed, this day was so like the last that it was hard to tell one from the other. The first thing she thought of, however, was not the rain, but the lady in the tower. And the first question in her mind was this: should she not ask the nurse to keep her promise this very morning? Should she not go with her to find her grandmother as soon as she had eaten her breakfast? But she decided that perhaps the lady would not be pleased if she brought someone to see her without first asking. It was also quite clear, since she lived on birds' eggs and cooked them herself, that she did not want the household to know she was there. So the princess decided to take the first chance to run up alone and ask if she might bring her nurse. She believed her grandmother would care a lot about one fact: without the nurse, she could not prove she was telling the truth.

The princess and her nurse were the best of friends all through dressing time. As a result, the princess ate a very large little breakfast.

'I wonder, Lootie'--that was her pet name for her nurse--'what birds' eggs taste like?' she said, as she was eating her egg. It was not quite a common one, for they always picked out the light red ones for her.

'We'll get you a bird's egg, and you shall judge for yourself,' said the nurse.

'Oh, no, no!' returned Irene. She suddenly thought they might bother the old lady in getting it. And even if they did not, she would have one less egg because of it.

'What a strange creature you are,' said the nurse. 'First you want a thing, and then you refuse it!'

But she did not say it in an angry way, and the princess never minded any remarks that were not unkind.

'Well, you see, Lootie, there are reasons,' she returned, and said no more. She did not want to bring up the subject of their earlier quarrel. She was afraid her nurse would offer to go before she had her grandmother's permission to bring her. Of course she could refuse to take her, but then the nurse would believe her even less.

Now the nurse, as she said herself later, could not be in the room every moment. And since the princess had never before yesterday given her the smallest reason to worry, it had not yet come into her head to watch her more closely. So she soon gave her a chance, and at the very first one that came, Irene was off and up the stairs again.

This day's adventure, however, did not turn out like yesterday's, although it began the same way. Indeed, today is very rarely like yesterday, if people would notice the differences--even when it rains. The princess ran through passage after passage and could not find the stair of the tower. My own guess is that she had not gone up high enough. She was searching on the second floor instead of the third. When she turned to go back, she failed just as much in her search for the stair. She was lost once more.

Something made it even worse to bear this time, and it was no wonder that she cried again. Suddenly it came to her that it was after crying before that she had found her grandmother's stair. She got up at once, wiped her eyes, and started on a fresh search.

This time, although she did not find what she hoped, she found what was next best. She did not come on a stair that went up, but she came upon one that went down. It was clearly not the stair she had come up, yet it was much better than none. So down she went, and she was singing happily before she reached the bottom. There, to her surprise, she found herself in the kitchen. She was not allowed to go there alone, but her nurse had often taken her, and the servants liked her a lot. So there was a general rush at her the moment she appeared, for everyone wanted to have her. And the news of where she was soon reached the nurse's ears. She came at once to get her, but she never guessed how she had got there, and the princess kept her own secret.

Her failure to find the old lady not only disappointed her, but made her very thoughtful. Sometimes she came almost to the nurse's opinion that she had dreamed all about her. But that idea never lasted very long. She wondered a lot whether she should ever see her again. She thought it very sad not to have been able to find her when she particularly wanted her. She decided to say nothing more to her nurse on the subject, since it was so little in her power to prove her words.

CHAPTER 6. The Little Miner

The next day the great cloud still hung over the mountain. The rain poured like water from a full sponge. The princess loved being outdoors very much. She almost cried when she saw that the weather was no better. But the mist was not such a dark, dirty grey. There was light in it. As the hours went on, it grew brighter and brighter. Soon it was almost too bright to look at. Late in the afternoon the sun broke out so beautifully that Irene clapped her hands and cried:

'See, see, Lootie! The sun has had his face washed. Look how bright he is! Please get my hat, and let us go out for a walk. Oh, dear! oh, dear! how happy I am!'

Lootie was very glad to please the princess. She got her hat and cloak, and they set out together for a walk up the mountain. The road was so hard and steep that the water could not stay on it. It was always dry enough for walking a few minutes after the rain stopped. The clouds were rolling away in broken pieces, like great, too-woolly sheep. The sun had bleached their wool until it was almost too white for the eyes to bear. Between them the sky shone with a deeper and purer blue because of the rain. The trees by the road were hung all over with drops. They sparkled in the sun like jewels. The only things that were no brighter for the rain were the brooks that ran down the mountain. They had changed from clear crystal to a muddy brown. But what they lost in colour they gained in sound—or at least in noise. A brook when it is swollen is not so musical as before. But Irene was full of joy at the great brown streams falling down everywhere. Lootie shared in her delight, for she too had been kept in the house for three days.

At last she saw that the sun was getting low. She said it was time to go back. She said this again and again. But every time, the princess begged her to go on just a little farther and a little farther. She reminded her that it was much easier to go downhill. She said that when they did turn, they would be home in a moment. So on and on they went. Now they looked at a group of ferns with a stream pouring over their tops in a watery arch. Now they picked a shining stone from a rock by the road. Now they watched some bird fly. Suddenly the shadow of a great mountain peak came up from behind and shot in front of them. When the nurse saw it, she started and shook. She caught hold of the princess's hand, turned, and began to run down the hill.

'What's all the hurry, nursie?' asked Irene, running beside her.

'We must not be out a moment longer.'

'But we can't help being out a good many moments longer.'

It was too true. The nurse almost cried. They were much too far from home. It was against strict orders to be out with the princess one moment after the sun was down. And they were nearly a mile up the mountain! If His Majesty, Irene's papa, were to hear of it, Lootie would certainly lose her job. And to leave the princess would break her heart. It was no wonder she ran. But Irene was not frightened at all. She did not know anything to be frightened at. She kept on talking as well as she could, but it was not easy.

'Lootie! Lootie! why do you run so fast? It shakes my teeth when I talk.'

'Then don't talk,' said Lootie.

But the princess went on talking. She was always saying: 'Look, look, Lootie!' But Lootie paid no more attention to anything she said. She only ran on.

'Look, look, Lootie! Don't you see that funny man looking over the rock?'

Lootie only ran faster. They had to pass the rock. When they came nearer, the princess saw it was only a lump of the rock itself that she had taken for a man.

'Look, look, Lootie! There's such a curious creature at the foot of that old tree. Look at it, Lootie! It's making faces at us, I do think.'

Lootie gave a stifled cry and ran faster still. She ran so fast that Irene's little legs could not keep up with her, and she fell with a crash. It was a hard downhill road, and she had been running very fast. So it was no wonder she began to cry. This put the nurse almost beside herself. But all she could do was to run on the moment she got the princess on her feet again.

'Who's that laughing at me?' said the princess. She tried to keep in her sobs and ran too fast for her grazed knees.

'Nobody, child,' said the nurse, almost angrily.

But that instant there came a burst of rough laughter from somewhere near. A hoarse, unclear voice seemed to say: 'Lies! lies! lies!'

'Oh!' cried the nurse with a sigh that was almost a scream. She ran on faster than ever.

'Nursie! Lootie! I can't run any more. Do let us walk a bit.'

'What am I to do?' said the nurse. 'Here, I will carry you.'

She caught her up. But she found her much too heavy to run with, and had to set her down again. Then she looked wildly about her, gave a great cry, and said:

'We've taken the wrong turning somewhere, and I don't know where we are. We are lost, lost!'

The terror she was in had quite confused her. It was true enough they had lost the way. They had been running down into a little valley with no house to be seen.

Now Irene did not know what good reason there was for her nurse's terror. The servants had all strict orders never to mention the goblins to her. But it was very upsetting to see her nurse in such a fright. Before she had time to grow thoroughly alarmed like her, she heard the sound of whistling. That revived her. Presently she saw a boy coming up the road from the valley to meet them. He was the whistler. But before they met, his whistling changed to singing. And this is something like what he sang:

'Ring! dod! bang! Go the hammers' clang! Hit and turn and bore! Whizz and puff and roar! Thus we rive the rocks, Force the goblin locks.— See the shining ore! One, two, three— Bright as gold can be! Four, five, six— Shovels, mattocks, picks! Seven, eight, nine— Light your lamp at mine. Ten, eleven, twelve— Loosely hold the helve. We're the merry miner-boys, Make the goblins hold their noise.'

'I wish YOU would stop making that noise,' said the nurse in a rough way. The very word GOBLIN at such a time and in such a place made her shake with fear. She thought it would bring the goblins to them for sure if they spoke against them in that way. But the boy may or may not have heard her. He did not stop his singing.

'Thirteen, fourteen, fifteen-- This is worth the picking; Sixteen, seventeen, eighteen-- There is the match, and put it in. Nineteen, twenty-- Goblins in a plenty.'

'Please be quiet,' cried the nurse, in a loud whisper. But the boy, who was now close to them, still went on.

'Hush! scush! scurry! There you go in a hurry! Gobble! gobble! goblin! There you go a wobblin'; Hobble, hobble, hobblin'-- Cobble! cobble! cobblin'! Hob-bob-goblin!-- Huuuuuh!'

'There!' said the boy, as he stood still in front of them. 'There! that will do for them. They cannot bear singing, and they cannot stand that song. They cannot sing themselves, for they have no more voice than a crow; and they do not like other people to sing.'

The boy was dressed in a miner's dress, with a strange cap on his head. He was a very nice-looking boy. His eyes were as dark as the mines in which he worked and as bright as the crystals in their rocks. He was about twelve years old. His face was almost too pale for beauty. This came from being so little in the open air and the sunlight--for even vegetables grown in the dark are white. But he looked happy, merry indeed--perhaps at the thought of having driven away the goblins. And his manner as he stood before them had nothing foolish or rough about it.

'I saw them,' he went on, 'as I came up; and I am very glad I did. I knew they were after somebody, but I could not see who it was. They will not touch you so long as I am with you.'

'Why, who are you?' asked the nurse, angry at the freedom with which he spoke to them.

'I am Peter's son.'

'Who is Peter?'

'Peter the miner.'

'I do not know him.'

'I am his son, though.'

'And why should the goblins mind you, pray?'

'Because I do not mind them. I am used to them.'

'What difference does that make?'

'If you are not afraid of them, they are afraid of you. I am not afraid of them. That is all. But it is all that is wanted--up here, that is. It is a different thing down there. They will not always mind that song even, down there. And if anyone sings it, they stand grinning at him awfully; and if he gets frightened, and misses a word, or says a wrong one, they--oh! do they not give it to him!'

'What do they do to him?' asked Irene, with a shaking voice.

'Do not go frightening the princess,' said the nurse.

'The princess!' repeated the little miner, taking off his strange cap. 'I beg your pardon; but you ought not to be out so late. Everybody knows that is against the law.'

'Yes, indeed it is!' said the nurse, beginning to cry again. 'And I shall have to suffer for it.'

'What does that matter?' said the boy. 'It must be your fault. It is the princess who will suffer for it. I hope they did not hear you call her the princess. If they did, they are sure to know her again: they are awfully sharp.'

'Lootie! Lootie!' cried the princess. 'Take me home.'

'Do not go on like that,' said the nurse to the boy, almost fiercely. 'How could I help it? I lost my way.'

'You should not have been out so late. You would not have lost your way if you had not been frightened,' said the boy. 'Come along. I will soon set you right again. Shall I carry your little Highness?'

'Impertinence!' murmured the nurse, but she did not say it aloud. She thought if she made him angry he might take his revenge by telling someone belonging to the house, and then it would be sure to come to the king's ears. 'No, thank you,' said Irene. 'I can walk very well, though I cannot run so fast as nursie. If you will give me one hand, Lootie will give me another, and then I shall get on famously.'

They soon had her between them, holding a hand of each.

'Now let us run,' said the nurse.

'No, no!' said the little miner. 'That is the worst thing you can do. If you had not run before, you would not have lost your way. And if you run now, they will be after you in a moment.'

'I do not want to run,' said Irene.

'You do not think of me,' said the nurse.

'Yes, I do, Lootie. The boy says they will not touch us if we do not run.'

'Yes, but if they know at the house that I have kept you out so late I shall be turned away, and that would break my heart.'

'Turned away, Lootie! Who would turn you away?'

'Your papa, child.'

'But I will tell him it was all my fault. And you know it was, Lootie.'

'He will not mind that. I am sure he will not.'

'Then I will cry, and go down on my knees to him, and beg him not to take away my own dear Lootie.'

The nurse was comforted at hearing this, and said no more. They went on, walking pretty fast, but taking care not to run a step.

'I want to talk to you,' said Irene to the little miner; 'but it is so awkward! I do not know your name.'

'My name is Curdie, little princess.'

'What a funny name! Curdie! What more?'

'Curdie Peterson. What is your name, please?'

'Irene.'

'What more?'

'I do not know what more. What more is my name, Lootie?'

'Princesses have not got more than one name. They do not want it.'

'Oh, then, Curdie, you must call me just Irene and no more.'

'No, indeed,' said the nurse indignantly. 'He shall do no such thing.'

'What shall he call me, then, Lootie?'

'Your Royal Highness.'

'My Royal Highness! What is that? No, no, Lootie. I will not be called names. I do not like them. You told me once yourself it is only rude children that call names; and I am sure Curdie would not be rude. Curdie, my name is Irene.'

'Well, Irene,' said Curdie, with a glance at the nurse which showed he enjoyed teasing her; 'it is very kind of you to let me call you anything. I like your name very much.'

He expected the nurse to interfere again; but he soon saw that she was too frightened to speak. She was staring at something a few yards before them in the middle of the path. There the path narrowed between rocks so that only one could pass at a time.

'It is very much kinder of you to go out of your way to take us home,' said Irene.

'I am not going out of my way yet,' said Curdie. 'It is on the other side of those rocks the path turns off to my father's.'

'You would not think of leaving us till we are safe home, I am sure,' gasped the nurse.

'Of course not,' said Curdie.

'You dear, good, kind Curdie! I will give you a kiss when we get home,' said the princess.

The nurse gave her a great pull by the hand she held. But at that moment, the something in the middle of the way began to move. It had looked like a great lump of earth brought down by the rain. One after another it shot out four long things, like two arms and two legs. But it was now too dark to tell what they were. The nurse began to shake from head to foot. Irene held Curdie's hand even faster. And Curdie began to sing again:

'One, two-- Hit and cut! Three, four-- Blast and bore! Five, six-- There's a fix! Seven, eight-- Hold it straight! Nine, ten-- Hit again! Hurry! Run! Bother! Cover! There's a toad In the road! Smash it! Squash it! Fry it! Dry it! You're another! Up and off! There's enough!-- Huuuuuh!'

As he said the last words, Curdie let go of his companion. He rushed at the thing in the road as if he would step on it hard under his feet. It gave a great jump. It ran straight up one of the rocks like a huge spider. Curdie turned back laughing and took Irene's hand again. She held his very tight but said nothing until they had passed the rocks. A few yards more and she found herself on a part of the road she knew. She was able to speak again.

'Do you know, Curdie, I do not quite like your song. It sounds to me rather rude,' she said.

'Well, perhaps it is,' answered Curdie. 'I never thought of that. It is a way we have. We do it because they do not like it.'

'Who do not like it?'

'The cobs, as we call them.'

'Do not!' said the nurse.

'Why not?' said Curdie.

'I beg you will not. Please do not.'

'Oh! if you ask me that way, of course, I will not. Though I do not at all know why. Look! there are the lights of your great house down below. You will be at home in five minutes now.'

Nothing more happened. They reached home in safety. Nobody had missed them or even known they had gone out. They arrived at the door belonging to their part of the house without anyone seeing them. The nurse was rushing in with a hurried and not very kind good night to Curdie. But the princess pulled her hand from hers. She was just throwing her arms round Curdie's neck when she caught her again and dragged her away.

'Lootie! Lootie! I promised a kiss,' cried Irene.

'A princess must not give kisses. It is not at all proper,' said Lootie.

'But I promised,' said the princess.

'There is no need. He is only a miner-boy.'

'He is a good boy and a brave boy. He has been very kind to us. Lootie! Lootie! I promised.'

'Then you should not have promised.'

'Lootie, I promised him a kiss.'

'Your Royal Highness,' said Lootie, suddenly grown very respectful, 'must come in directly.'

'Nurse, a princess must not break her word,' said Irene. She drew herself up and stood stock-still.

Lootie did not know which the king might count the worst. Was it worse to let the princess be out after sunset? Or to let her kiss a miner-boy? She did not know that he was a gentleman, as many kings have been. He would have counted neither of them the worse. However much he might have disliked his daughter to kiss the miner-boy, he would not have had her break her word for all the goblins in creation. But, as I say, the nurse was not lady enough to understand this. So she was in a great difficulty. If she insisted, someone might hear the princess cry and run to see. Then all would come out. But here Curdie came again to the rescue.

'Never mind, Princess Irene,' he said. 'You must not kiss me tonight. But you shall not break your word. I will come another time. You may be sure I will.'

'Oh, thank you, Curdie!' said the princess, and stopped crying.

'Good night, Irene; good night, Lootie,' said Curdie. He turned and was out of sight in a moment.

'I should like to see him!' muttered the nurse, as she carried the princess to the nursery.

'You will see him,' said Irene. 'You may be sure Curdie will keep his word. He is sure to come again.'

'I should like to see him!' repeated the nurse, and said no more. She did not want to open a new cause of strife with the princess. She did not want to say more plainly what she meant. She was glad enough that she had succeeded in getting home unseen. She was also glad that she had kept the princess from kissing the miner's boy. She resolved to watch her far better in future. Her carelessness had already doubled the danger she was in. Formerly the goblins were her only fear. Now she had to protect her charge from Curdie as well.

CHAPTER 7. The Mines

The Mines

Curdie went home whistling. He decided to say nothing about the princess. He did not want to get the nurse into trouble. He liked to make fun of her because of her silly ideas. But he was careful not to do her any harm. He saw no more of the goblins. Soon he was fast asleep in his bed.

He woke in the middle of the night. He thought he heard strange noises outside. He sat up and listened. Then he got up and opened the door very quietly. He went out. When he looked around the corner, he saw a group of short, thick creatures under his own window. He knew them at once by their shape. He had hardly begun his "One, two, three!" when they broke apart and ran away. They were out of sight in a moment. He went back laughing. He got into bed again and was fast asleep in a moment.

In the morning he thought a little about the matter. He decided that nothing like this had ever happened before. So they must be angry with him for stepping in to protect the princess. By the time he was dressed, however, he was thinking of something quite different. He did not care at all about the goblins' anger. As soon as they had eaten breakfast, he set off with his father for the mine.

They entered the hill by a natural opening under a huge rock. A little stream rushed out there. They followed its course for a few yards. Then the passage turned and sloped sharply down into the heart of the hill. It had many angles and turns and side passages. Sometimes there were steps where it came to a natural deep hollow. It led them deep into the hill before they reached the place where they were digging out the precious ore. The ore was of various kinds, for the mountain was very rich in the better metals. With flint and steel and a tinder-box, they lit their lamps. Then they fixed the lamps on their heads. Soon they were hard at work with their pickaxes and shovels and hammers. Father and son worked near each other, but not in the same gang. The passages out of which the ore was dug were called gangs. When the lode, or vein of ore, was small, one miner had to dig alone in a passage just big enough to give him room to work. Sometimes he had to work in cramped, uncomfortable positions. If they stopped for a moment, they could hear sounds all around them. Some were nearer, some farther off. Their companions were digging away in all directions inside the great mountain. Some were boring holes in the rock to blow it up with gunpowder. Others were shovelling the broken ore into baskets to be carried to the mouth of the mine. Others were hitting away with their pickaxes. Sometimes, if a miner was in a very lonely part, he would hear only a tap-tapping. It was no louder than a woodpecker, for the sound came from a great distance through the solid mountain rock.

The work was hard at best, for it is very warm under the ground. But it was not particularly unpleasant. Some of the miners, when they wanted to earn a little more money for a special purpose, would stay behind the rest and work all night. But you could not tell night from day down there, except by feeling tired and sleepy. No light of the sun ever came into those dark regions. Some who had stayed behind during the night were sure that none of their companions were at work. Yet the next morning they would say that every time they stopped for a moment to take breath, they heard a tap-tapping all about them. It was as if the mountain were then more full of miners than ever during the day. Some, as a result, would never stay overnight, for all knew those were the sounds of the goblins. The goblins worked only at night, for the miners' night was the goblins' day. Indeed, most of the miners were afraid of the goblins. Strange stories were well known among them about how some men had been treated when the goblins surprised them at work during the night. The more courageous of them, however, including Peter Peterson and Curdie, who took after his father in this, had stayed in the mine all night again and again. They had met a few stray goblins several times, but had never yet failed to drive them away. As I have said already, the chief defence against them was verse. The goblins hated verse of every kind, and some kinds they could not bear at all. I suspect they could not make any themselves, and that was why they disliked it so much. At all events, those who were most afraid of them were those who could neither make verses themselves nor remember the verses that other people made for them. Those who were never afraid were those who could make verses for themselves. There were certain old rhymes which were very effective. Yet it was well known that a new rhyme, if of the right sort, was even more hateful to them. So it was even more effective in driving them away.

Perhaps my readers may wonder what the goblins could be doing, working all night long, since they never carried up the ore and sold it. But when I have told them what Curdie learned the very next night, they will be able to understand.

Curdie had decided to stay there alone this night, if his father would let him. He had two reasons. First, he wanted to get extra wages so he could buy a very warm red petticoat for his mother. She had begun to complain of the cold mountain air sooner than usual this autumn. Second, he had just a faint hope of finding out what the goblins were doing under his window the night before.

When he told his father, his father made no objection, for he had great confidence in his boy's courage and cleverness.

"I'm sorry I can't stay with you," said Peter. "But I want to go and pay the parson a visit this evening. Besides, I've had a bit of a headache all day."

"I'm sorry for that, father," said Curdie.

"Oh, it's not much. You'll be sure to take care of yourself, won't you?"

"Yes, father; I will. I'll keep a sharp look-out, I promise you." Curdie was the only one who remained in the mine. About six o'clock the rest went away. Everyone said good night to him and told him to take care of himself, for he was a great favourite with them all.

"Don't forget your rhymes," said one.

"No, no," answered Curdie.

"It's no matter if he does," said another, "for he'll only have to make a new one."

'Yes, but he may not be able to make it fast enough,' said another. 'And while it was cooking in his head, they may take a mean advantage and set upon him.'

'I'll do my best,' said Curdie. 'I'm not afraid.'

'We all know that,' they answered, and left him.

CHAPTER 8. The Goblins

The Goblins

For some time Curdie worked away quickly. He threw all the ore he had cut loose on one side behind him. It would be ready to carry out in the morning. He heard a good deal of goblin tapping. But it all sounded far away in the hill. He paid it little attention. Towards midnight he began to feel rather hungry. So he dropped his pickaxe. He got out a lump of bread. In the morning he had laid it in a wet hole in the rock. He sat down on a heap of ore and ate his supper. Then he leaned back for five minutes' rest before he began his work again. He laid his head against the rock. He had not kept that position for one minute before he heard something. It made him listen carefully. It sounded like a voice inside the rock. After a while he heard it again. It was a goblin voice. There could be no doubt about that. And this time he could make out the words.

"Hadn't we better be moving?" it said.

A rougher and deeper voice replied:

"There's no hurry. That poor little mole won't be through tonight, if he work ever so hard. He's not by any means at the thinnest place."

"But you still think the lode does come through into our house?" said the first voice.

"Yes, but a good bit farther on than he has got to yet. If he had struck one more stroke to the side just here," said the goblin, tapping the very stone, as it seemed to Curdie, against which his head lay, "he would have been through. But he's a couple of yards past it now. And if he follow the lode it will be a week before it leads him in. You see it back there, a long way. Still, perhaps, in case of accident it would be as well to be getting out of this. Helfer, you'll take the great chest. That's your business, you know."

"Yes, dad," said a third voice. "But you must help me to get it on my back. It's awfully heavy, you know."

"Well, it isn't just a bag of smoke, I admit. But you're as strong as a mountain, Helfer."

"You say so, dad. I think myself I'm all right. But I could carry ten times as much if it wasn't for my feet."

"That is your weak point, I confess, my boy."

"Ain't it yours too, father?"

"Well, to be honest, it's a goblin weakness. Why they come so soft, I declare I haven't an idea."

"Specially when your head's so hard, you know, father."

"Yes my boy. The goblin's glory is his head. To think how the fellows up above there have to put on helmets and things when they go fighting! Ha! ha!"

"But why don't we wear shoes like them, father? I should like it, especially when I've got a chest like that on my head."

"Well, you see, it's not the fashion. The king never wears shoes."

"The queen does."

"Yes, but that's for distinction. The first queen, you see, I mean the king's first wife, wore shoes, of course, because she came from upstairs. And so, when she died, the next queen would not be inferior to her, as she called it, and would wear shoes too. It was all pride. She is the hardest in forbidding them to the rest of the women."

"I'm sure I wouldn't wear them, no, not for that I wouldn't!" said the first voice, which was evidently that of the mother of the family. "I can't think why either of them should."

"Didn't I tell you the first was from upstairs?" said the other. "That was the only silly thing I ever knew His Majesty guilty of. Why should he marry an outlandish woman like that, one of our natural enemies too?"

"I suppose he fell in love with her."

"Pooh! pooh! He's just as happy now with one of his own people."

"Did she die very soon? They didn't tease her to death, did they?"

"Oh, dear, no! The king worshipped her very footmarks."

"What made her die, then? Didn't the air agree with her?"

"She died when the young prince was born."

"How silly of her! We never do that. It must have been because she wore shoes."

"I don't know that."

"Why do they wear shoes up there?"

"Ah, now that's a sensible question, and I will answer it. But in order to do so, I must first tell you a secret. I once saw the queen's feet."

"Without her shoes?"

"Yes, without her shoes."

"No! Did you? How was it?"

"Never you mind how it was. She didn't know I saw them. And what do you think! They had toes!"

"Toes! What's that?"

"You may well ask! I should never have known if I had not seen the queen's feet. Just imagine! The ends of her feet were split up into five or six thin pieces!"

"Oh, horrid! How could the king have fallen in love with her?"

"You forget that she wore shoes. That is just why she wore them. That is why all the men, and women too, upstairs wear shoes. They can't bear the sight of their own feet without them."

"Ah! now I understand. If ever you wish for shoes again, Helfer, I'll hit your feet, I will."

"No, no, mother; pray don't."

"Then don't you."

"But with such a big box on my head—"

A horrid scream followed. Curdie interpreted it as a reply to a blow from his mother upon the feet of her eldest goblin.

"Well, I never knew so much before!" remarked a fourth voice.

"Your knowledge is not universal quite yet," said the father. "You were only fifty last month. Mind you see to the bed and bedding. As soon as we've finished our supper, we'll be up and going. Ha! ha! ha!"

"What are you laughing at, husband?"

"I'm laughing to think what a mess the miners will find themselves in, somewhere before this day ten years."

"Why, what do you mean?"

"Oh, nothing."

"Oh, yes, you do mean something. You always do mean something."

"It's more than you do, then, wife."

"That may be; but it's not more than I find out, you know."

"Ha! ha! You're a sharp one. What a mother you've got, Helfer!"

"Yes, father."

"Well, I suppose I must tell you. They're all at the palace consulting about it tonight. And as soon as we've got away from this thin place I'm going there to hear what night they fix upon. I should like to see that young ruffian there on the other side, struggling in the agonies of—"

He dropped his voice so low that Curdie could hear only a growl. The growl went on in the low bass for a good while. It was as unclear as if the goblin's tongue had been a sausage. And it was not until his wife spoke again that it rose to its former pitch.

"But what shall we do when you are at the palace?" she asked.

'I will see you safe in the new house I have been digging for you for the last two months. Podge, you take care of the table and chairs. I give them to you. The table has seven legs--each chair three. I shall want them all back from you.'

After this came a confused talk about the various household goods and how to move them. Curdie heard nothing more that was important.

He now knew at least one reason for the constant sound of hammers and picks at night. They were making new houses for themselves. They could go there when the miners tried to break into their homes. But he had learned two things of far greater importance. The first was that some terrible disaster was being prepared. It was almost ready to fall on the miners. The second was the one weak point of a goblin's body. He had not known that their feet were so tender. Now he had reason to think so. He had heard it said that they had no toes. He had never had a chance to look at them closely enough. They always appeared in the dusk, and he could not be sure if the report was correct. Indeed, he had not even been able to be sure whether they had no fingers, although people also commonly said that was true. One of the miners, who had more schooling than the rest, used to argue that this must have been the early condition of humanity. He said that education and skilled work had developed both toes and fingers. Curdie had once heard his father agree with this idea in a mocking way. His father said that babies' gloves were probably a traditional leftover from the old state of things. He also said that stockings of all ages paid no attention to the toes, which pointed the same way. But what was important was the fact about the softness of the goblin feet. Curdie saw that this might be useful to all miners. What he had to do in the meantime was to discover, if possible, the special evil plan the goblins now had in mind.

He knew all the gangs and all the natural passages that connected them in the mined part of the mountain. But he had no idea where the palace of the king of the gnomes was. If he had known, he would have set out at once to discover what that plan was. He judged, and rightly, that it must be in a farther part of the mountain. Between that part and the mine there was still no connection. But one must be almost finished. The wall that now separated them could only be thin. If only he could get through in time to follow the goblins as they went back! A few blows would surely be enough, right where his ear now lay. But if he tried to strike there with his pick, he would only hurry the family's departure. He would put them on their guard. He might even lose their unintentional guidance. So he began to feel the wall with his hands. Soon he found that some of the stones were loose enough to pull out with little noise.

He took hold of a large one with both hands. He pulled it out gently and set it down softly.

'What was that noise?' said the goblin father.

Curdie blew out his light so it would not shine through.

'It must be that one miner who stayed behind the rest,' said the mother.

'No, he has been gone a good while. I have not heard a blow for an hour. Besides, it was not like that.'

'Then I suppose it must have been a stone carried down the stream inside.'

'Perhaps. It will have more room by and by.'

Curdie kept quite still. After a little while, he heard nothing but the sounds of their preparations to leave. Now and then a word of direction mixed in. He wanted to know whether removing the stone had made an opening into the goblins' house. So he put in his hand to feel. It went in a good way. Then it touched something soft. He had only a moment to feel it, because it was pulled back so quickly. It was one of the toeless goblin feet. Its owner gave a cry of fright.

'What is the matter, Helfer?' asked his mother.

'A beast came out of the wall and licked my foot.'

'Nonsense! There are no wild beasts in our country,' said his father.

'But it was, father. I felt it.'

'Nonsense, I say. Will you speak evil of your native land and make it the same as the country upstairs? That place is full of wild beasts of every kind.'

'But I did feel it, father.'

'I tell you to hold your tongue. You are no patriot.'

Curdie held back his laughter and lay still as a mouse. But he was no stiller than that, because every moment he kept nibbling away with his fingers at the edges of the hole. He was slowly making it bigger, because here the rock had been very much broken by the blasting.

There seemed to be a good many in the family, judging from the mass of confused talk that now and then came through the hole. But when they all spoke together, it was just as if they had bottle-brushes in their throats--each at least one. It was not easy to make out much that was said. At last he heard the father goblin speaking again.

'Now, then,' he said, 'get your bundles on your backs. Here, Helfer, I will help you up with your chest.'

'I wish it was my chest, father.'

'Your turn will come in good time enough! Make haste. I must go to the meeting at the palace tonight. When that is over, we can come back and clear out the last of the things before our enemies return in the morning. Now light your torches and come along. What a distinction it is to make our own light, instead of depending on a thing hung up in the air! A most disagreeable invention! No doubt it is meant to blind us when we go out under its harmful influence! Quite glaring and vulgar, I call it. No doubt it is useful to poor creatures who do not have the wit to make light for themselves.'

Curdie could hardly keep himself from calling through to ask whether they made fire to light their torches. But a moment's thought showed him that they would have said they did, since they struck two stones together and the fire came.

CHAPTER 9. The Hall of the Goblin Palace

The Hall of the Goblin Palace

A sound of many soft feet followed, but soon stopped. Then Curdie jumped at the hole like a tiger. He tore and pulled at it. The sides gave way. Soon the hole was large enough for him to crawl through. He did not want to give himself away by lighting his lamp again. But the torches of the leaving group threw back enough light. They were going in a straight line up a long road from the door of their cave. The light let him look around the empty home of the goblins. To his surprise, he could find nothing to make it different from an ordinary natural cave in the rock. He had come upon many such caves with the rest of the miners during their digging. The goblins had talked of coming back for the rest of their household goods. He saw nothing that would have made him think a family had sheltered there for a single night. The floor was rough and stony. The walls were full of projecting corners. The roof was twenty feet high in one place, and in another it endangered his forehead. On one side a stream, no thicker than a needle, it is true, but still enough to spread a wide dampness over the wall, flowed down the face of the rock. But the group in front of him was toiling under heavy loads. He could see Helfer now and then, in the flickering light and shade, with his heavy chest on his bending shoulders. The second brother was almost buried in what looked like a great feather bed. 'Where do they get the feathers?' thought Curdie. But in a moment the group disappeared at a turn of the way. It was now both safe and necessary for Curdie to follow them. If he did not, they might be round the next turning before he saw them again. Then he might lose them altogether. He darted after them like a greyhound. When he reached the corner and looked cautiously round, he saw them again at some distance down another long passage. None of the galleries he saw that night bore signs of the work of man--or of goblin either. Stalactites, far older than the mines, hung from their roofs. Their floors were rough with boulders and large round stones. This showed that water must have once run there. He waited again at this corner till they had disappeared round the next. And so he followed them a long way through one passage after another. The passages grew more and more lofty. They were more and more covered in the roof with shining stalactites.

It was a strange enough procession which he followed. But the strangest part of it was the household animals which crowded amongst the feet of the goblins. It was true they had no wild animals down there--at least they did not know of any. But they had a wonderful number of tame ones. I must, however, save any contributions towards the natural history of these for a later position in my story.

At length he turned a corner too abruptly. He almost rushed into the middle of the goblin family. They had already set down all their loads on the floor of a cave considerably larger than that which they had left. They were as yet too breathless to speak. If they had spoken, he would have had warning of their stop. He started back, however, before anyone saw him. He retreated a good way and stood watching till the father should come out to go to the palace.

Before very long, both he and his son Helfer appeared. They kept on in the same direction as before. Curdie followed them again with renewed precaution. For a long time he heard no sound except something like the rush of a river inside the rock. But at length what seemed the far-off noise of a great shouting reached his ears. It presently stopped. After advancing a good way farther, he thought he heard a single voice. It sounded clearer and clearer as he went on. At last he could almost distinguish the words. In a moment or two, keeping after the goblins round another corner, he once more started back--this time in amazement.

He was at the entrance of a magnificent cavern. It was of an oval shape. It was once probably a huge natural reservoir of water. Now it was the great palace hall of the goblins. It rose to a tremendous height. But the roof was composed of such shining materials. And the multitude of torches carried by the goblins who crowded the floor lighted up the place so brilliantly that Curdie could see to the top quite well. But he had no idea how immense the place was until his eyes had got accustomed to it. That was not for a good many minutes. The rough projections on the walls, and the shadows thrown upwards from them by the torches, made the sides of the chamber look as if they were crowded with statues upon brackets and pedestals. These reached in irregular tiers from floor to roof. The walls themselves were, in many parts, of gloriously shining substances. Some of them were gorgeously coloured besides. They powerfully contrasted with the shadows. Curdie could not help wondering whether his rhymes would be of any use against such a multitude of goblins as filled the floor of the hall. Indeed he felt considerably tempted to begin his shout of 'One, two, three!' But there was no reason for routing them. There was much reason for endeavouring to discover their designs. So he kept himself perfectly quiet. He peered round the edge of the doorway and listened with both his sharp ears.

At the other end of the hall, high above the heads of the multitude, was a terrace-like ledge of considerable height. It was caused by the receding of the upper part of the cavern-wall. Upon this sat the king and his court. The king sat on a throne hollowed out of a huge block of green copper ore. His court sat upon lower seats around it. The king had been making them a speech. The applause which followed it was what Curdie had heard. One of the court was now addressing the multitude. What he heard him say was to the following effect: 'Hence it appears that two plans have been for some time together working in the strong head of His Majesty for the deliverance of his people. Regardless of the fact that we were the first possessors of the regions they now inhabit; regardless equally of the fact that we abandoned that region from the loftiest motives; regardless also of the self-evident fact that we excel them so far in mental ability as they excel us in stature, they look upon us as a degraded race and make a mockery of all our finer feelings. But, the time has almost arrived when--thanks to His Majesty's inventive genius--it will be in our power to take a thorough revenge upon them once for all, in respect of their unfriendly behaviour.'

'May it please Your Majesty--' cried a voice close by the door. Curdie knew it was the goblin he had followed.

'Who is he that interrupts the Chancellor?' cried another from near the throne.

'Glump,' answered several voices.

'He is our loyal subject,' said the king himself, in a slow and formal voice. 'Let him come forward and speak.'

A path was opened through the crowd. Glump went up onto the platform and bowed to the king. Then he spoke as follows:

'Sire, I would have kept quiet, but I knew how close the moment was. The Chancellor had just spoken of it.

In all likelihood, before another day is past, the enemy will have broken through into my house. The wall between is even now not more than a foot thick.'

'Not quite so much,' thought Curdie to himself.

'This very evening I have had to move my household goods. So the sooner we are ready to carry out the plan, the better. His Majesty has been making such wonderful preparations for it. I may just add that within the last few days I have seen a small sudden attack in my dining-room. I have also seen the river escaping where the evil men enter. This has convinced me that close to the spot there must be a deep gulf in its channel. This discovery will, I hope, add a lot to the otherwise great forces at His Majesty's command.'

He stopped. The king kindly acknowledged his speech with a bend of his head. Then Glump bowed to His Majesty and slid down among the rest of the ordinary crowd. Then the Chancellor rose and continued.

'The information which the good Glump has given us,' he said, 'might have been of great importance at the present moment. But there is that other plan already mentioned, which naturally comes first. His Majesty is not willing to go to extremes. He is well aware that such measures sooner or later lead to violent reactions. So he has thought out a more basic and complete measure. I need say no more about it. Should His Majesty succeed--as who dares to doubt?--then a peace will be established for a generation at least. It will be all to the advantage of the goblin kingdom. It will be made completely safe by the promise which His Royal Highness the prince will have and hold for the good behaviour of her relatives. Should His Majesty fail--which who shall dare even to imagine in his most secret thoughts?--then will be the time for carrying out strictly the plan to which Glump referred. Our preparations for it are even now all but complete. The failure of the former will make the latter necessary.'

Curdie saw that the meeting was coming to a close. There was little chance of either plan being more fully discovered. So he now thought it wise to make his escape before the goblins began to scatter. He slipped quietly away.

There was not much danger of meeting any goblins. All the men at least were left behind him in the palace. But there was considerable danger of his taking a wrong turn. He had now no light, so he had to depend upon his memory and his hands. He left behind him the glow that came from the door of Glump's new home. After that he was completely without guide, as far as his eyes were concerned.

He was most anxious to get back through the hole before the goblins should return to fetch the rest of their furniture. It was not that he was in the least afraid of them. But it was of the greatest importance that he should fully discover what the plans they were keeping were. So he must not cause the slightest suspicion that they were watched by a miner.

He hurried on, feeling his way along the walls of rock. Had he not been very brave, he must have been very anxious. He could not but know that if he lost his way it would be the most difficult thing in the world to find it again. Morning would bring no light into these regions. And goblins could least of all be expected to show courtesy towards him, who was known as a special rhymester and persecutor. Well might he wish that he had brought his lamp and tinder-box with him! He had not thought of them when he crept so eagerly after the goblins. He wished it all the more when, after a while, he found his way blocked up and could get no farther. It was of no use to turn back, for he had not the least idea where he had begun to go wrong. However, he kept feeling about the walls that hemmed him in, without thinking. His hand came upon a place where a tiny stream of water was running down the face of the rock. 'What a stupid I am!' he said to himself. 'I am actually at the end of my journey! And there are the goblins coming back to fetch their things!' he added. The red glimmer of their torches appeared at the end of the long avenue that led up to the cave. In a moment he had thrown himself on the floor and wriggled backwards through the hole. The floor on the other side was several feet lower, which made it easier to get back. It was all he could do to lift the largest stone he had taken out of the hole, but he did manage to push it in again. He sat down on the ore-heap and thought.

He was quite sure that the second plan of the goblins was to flood the mine. They would break openings for the water that had gathered in the mountain's natural pools. They would also let it run through parts of the mine. The part dug out by the miners was still closed off from the part where the goblins lived. So the goblins had no chance to hurt them in this way before. But now a passage was broken through. And the goblins' part was higher in the mountain. So Curdie saw clearly that the mine could be destroyed in an hour. Water was always the main danger the miners faced. They sometimes met a little bad air, but never the dangerous gas so common in coal mines. So they were careful as soon as they saw any sign of water. The goblins were busy in their old home, and Curdie thought about it. It seemed best to him to fill up this whole tunnel with stone and clay. Then there would be no small channel for the water to get in. There was no immediate danger, though. The goblins' plan depended on another secret plan failing first. That other plan would come before it. And Curdie was very eager to keep the door of communication open. He wanted to find out what the first plan was if he could. At the same time, the goblins could not start their stopped work on the flood without him finding out. Then all hands could work at once. The one existing outlet could be made strong against any weight of water in a single night. For by filling the tunnel completely, the mountain's own sides would support their wall.

As soon as he found that the goblins had gone away again, he lit his lamp. Then he began to fill the hole he had made with stones he could pull out when he liked. Then he thought it better to go home and get some sleep. He might need to be up many nights after this.

How pleasant the night air felt outside the mountain! He had been through so much inside it. He hurried up the hill and met no goblin on the way. He called and tapped at the window until he woke his father. His father soon rose and let him in. He told him the whole story. Just as he had expected, his father thought it best to work that vein no farther. But at the same time, he should sometimes pretend to be working there still. Then the goblins would have no suspicions. Both father and son then went to bed and slept well until the morning.

CHAPTER 10. The Princess's King-Papa

The Princess's King-Papa

The weather stayed fine for weeks, and the little princess went out every day. So long a period of fine weather had really never been known upon that mountain. The only uncomfortable thing was that her nurse was so nervous and careful about being in before the sun was down. Often she would start running when nothing worse than a soft white cloud crossing the sun threw a shadow on the hill. And many an evening they were home a full hour before the sunlight had left the weather-cock on the stables. If it had not been for such strange behaviour Irene would by this time have almost forgotten the goblins. She never forgot Curdie, but him she remembered for his own sake. Indeed she would have remembered him if only because a princess never forgets her debts until they are paid.

One fine sunny day, about an hour after noon, Irene, who was playing on a lawn in the garden, heard the distant sound of a horn. She jumped up with a cry of joy, for she knew by that special sound that her father was on his way to see her. This part of the garden lay on the slope of the hill and allowed a full view of the country below. So she shaded her eyes with her hand and looked far away to catch the first sight of shining metal. In a few moments a little troop came shining round the shoulder of a hill. Spears and helmets were bright and flashing, flags were flying, horses were jumping, and again came the horn sound which was to her like the voice of her father calling across the distance: 'Irene, I'm coming.'

On and on they came until she could clearly see the king. He rode a white horse and was taller than any of the men with him. He wore a narrow circle of gold set with stones around his helmet, and as he came still nearer Irene could see the flashing of the stones in the sun. It was a long time since he had been to see her, and her little heart beat faster and faster as the shining troop came near. For she loved her king-father very much and was nowhere so happy as in his arms. When they reached a certain point, after which she could see them no more from the garden, she ran to the gate. There she stood until up they came, clanging and stamping, with one more bright horn sound which said: 'Irene, I am come.'

By this time the people of the house were all gathered at the gate, but Irene stood alone in front of them. When the riders stopped she ran to the side of the white horse and held up her arms. The king stopped and took her hands. In an instant she was on the seat and held in his great strong arms.

I wish I could describe the king so that you could see him in your mind. He had gentle, blue eyes, but a nose that made him look like an eagle. A long dark beard, with lines of silver in it, flowed from his mouth almost to his waist. As Irene sat on the seat and hid her glad face upon his chest it mixed with the golden hair which her mother had given her. The two together were like a cloud with lines of the sun woven through it. After he had held her to his heart for a minute he spoke to his white horse. The great beautiful creature, which had been jumping so proudly a little while before, walked as gently as a lady--for he knew he had a little lady on his back--through the gate and up to the door of the house. Then the king set her on the ground and, getting down, took her hand and walked with her into the great hall. This hall was hardly ever entered except when he came to see his little princess. There he sat down, with two of his advisers who had come with him, to have some food and drink. Irene sat on his right hand and drank her milk out of a wooden bowl strangely carved.

After the king had eaten and drunk he turned to the princess and said, stroking her hair:

'Now, my child, what shall we do next?'

This was the question he almost always put to her first after their meal together. Irene had been waiting for it with some impatience, for now, she thought, she should be able to settle a question which constantly puzzled her.

'I should like you to take me to see my great old grandmother.'

The king looked serious and said:

'What does my little daughter mean?'

'I mean the Queen Irene that lives up in the tower--the very old lady, you know, with the long hair of silver.'

The king only looked at his little princess with a look which she could not understand.

'She has got her crown in her bedroom,' she went on; 'but I have not been in there yet. You know she is there, do you not?'

'No,' said the king, very quietly.

'Then it must all be a dream,' said Irene. 'I half thought it was; but I could not be sure. Now I am sure of it. Besides, I could not find her the next time I went up.'

At that moment a snow-white bird flew in at an open window and settled upon Irene's head. She broke into a merry laugh, moved down a little, and put up her hands to her head, saying:

'Dear bird, do not peck me. You will pull out my hair with your long claws if you do not mind.'

The king stretched out his hand to take the bird, but it spread its wings and flew again through the open window. Its whiteness made one flash in the sun and vanished. The king laid his hand on his princess's head, held it back a little, looked in her face, smiled half a smile, and sighed half a sigh.

'Come, my child; we will have a walk in the garden together,' he said.

'You will not come up and see my huge, great, beautiful grandmother, then, king-father?' said the princess.

'Not this time,' said the king very gently. 'She has not invited me, you know, and great old ladies like her do not choose to be visited without leave asked and given.'

The garden was a very lovely place. Being upon a Mountainside there were parts in it where the rocks came through in great masses, and all immediately about them remained quite wild. Tufts of heather grew upon them, and other tough mountain plants and flowers, while near them would be lovely roses and lilies and all pleasant garden flowers. This mixing of the wild mountain with the civilized garden was very strange, and it was impossible for any number of gardeners to make such a garden look formal and stiff.

Against one of these rocks was a garden seat. The rock itself kept the afternoon sun off it. A little winding path went up to the top of the rock. There was another seat on top. But they sat on the seat at the bottom because the sun was hot. And there they talked together about many things. At last the king said:

'You were out late one evening, Irene.'

'Yes, father. It was my fault. And Lootie was very sorry.'

'I must talk to Lootie about it,' said the king.

'Please don't speak loud to her, father,' said Irene. 'She has been so afraid of being late ever since! Indeed she has not been bad. It was only a mistake for once.'

'Once might be too often,' the king said quietly to himself. He stroked his child's head.

I cannot tell you how he had come to know. I am sure Curdie had not told him. Someone about the palace must have seen them, after all.

He sat for a good while thinking. There was no sound except that of a little stream. It ran happily out of an opening in the rock by where they sat. It hurried away down the hill through the garden. Then he rose. He left Irene where she was and went into the house. He sent for Lootie. He had a talk with her that made her cry.

In the evening he rode away on his great white horse. He left six of his servants behind him. He ordered that three of them should watch outside the house every night. They should walk round and round it from sunset to sunrise. It was clear he was not quite comfortable about the princess.

CHAPTER 11. The Old Lady's Bedroom

The Old Lady's Bedroom

Nothing more happened worth telling for some time. The autumn came and went by. There were no more flowers in the garden. The wind blew strong, and made a loud sound among the rocks. The rain fell, and made the few yellow and red leaves very wet. These leaves could not get off the bare branches. Again and again there would be a beautiful morning followed by a pouring afternoon. Sometimes, for a week together, there would be rain, nothing but rain, all day. Then there would be the most lovely night with no clouds, with the sky all out in full stars--not one missing. But the princess could not see much of them, for she went to bed early. The winter drew on, and she found things growing sad. When it was too stormy to go out, and she had got tired of her toys, Lootie would take her about the house. Sometimes they went to the room of the woman who kept the house. This woman was a good, kind old woman, and she made much of her. Sometimes they went to the servants' hall or the kitchen. There she was not princess merely, but complete queen, and ran a great risk of being spoiled. Sometimes she would run off herself to the room where the men-at-arms whom the king had left sat. They showed her their arms and equipment and did what they could to make her happy. Still at times she found it very sad, and often and often wished that her huge great grandmother had not been a dream.

One morning the nurse left her with the woman who kept the house for a while. To make her happy she turned out the contents of an old cabinet upon the table. The little princess found her treasures, strange old ornaments, and many things the use of which she could not imagine. These were far more interesting than her own toys, and she sat playing with them for two hours or more. But, at length, in handling a curious old-fashioned brooch, she ran the pin of it into her thumb. She gave a little scream with the sharpness of the pain. But she would have thought little more of it had not the pain increased and her thumb begun to swell. This alarmed the woman who kept the house greatly. The nurse was fetched; the doctor was sent for; her hand was poulticed, and long before her usual time she was put to bed. The pain still continued. Although she fell asleep and dreamed a good many dreams, there was the pain always in every dream. At last it woke her up.

The moon was shining brightly into the room. The poultice had fallen off her hand and it was burning hot. She fancied if she could hold it into the moonlight that would cool it. So she got out of bed, without waking the nurse who lay at the other end of the room, and went to the window. When she looked out she saw one of the men-at-arms walking in the garden with the moonlight glancing on his armour. She was just going to tap on the window and call him, for she wanted to tell him all about it. Then she remembered that that might wake Lootie, and she would put her into her bed again. So she resolved to go to the window of another room, and call him from there. It was so much nicer to have somebody to talk to than to lie awake in bed with the burning pain in her hand. She opened the door very gently and went through the nursery, which did not look into the garden, to go to the other window. But when she came to the foot of the old staircase there was the moon shining down from some window high up. It made the worm-eaten oak look very strange and delicate and lovely. In a moment she was putting her little feet one after the other in the silvery path up the stair. She looked behind as she went, to see the shadow they made in the middle of the silver. Some little girls would have been afraid to find themselves thus alone in the middle of the night, but Irene was a princess.

As she went slowly up the stair, not quite sure that she was not dreaming, suddenly a great longing woke up in her heart. She wanted to try once more whether she could not find the old lady with the silvery hair. 'If she is a dream,' she said to herself, 'then I am the likelier to find her, if I am dreaming.'

So up and up she went, stair after stair, until she came to the many rooms--all just as she had seen them before. Through passage after passage she softly sped. She comforted herself that if she should lose her way it would not matter much. When she woke she would find herself in her own bed with Lootie not far off. But, as if she had known every step of the way, she walked straight to the door at the foot of the narrow stair that led to the tower.

'What if I should really find my beautiful old grandmother up there!' she said to herself as she crept up the steep steps.

When she reached the top she stood a moment listening in the dark, for there was no moon there. Yes! it was! it was the hum of the spinning-wheel! What a diligent grandmother to work both day and night! She tapped gently at the door.

'Come in, Irene,' said the sweet voice.

The princess opened the door and entered. There was the moonlight streaming in at the window. In the middle of the moonlight sat the old lady in her black dress with the white lace. Her silvery hair mingled with the moonlight, so that you could not have told which was which. 'Come in, Irene,' she said again. 'Can you tell me what I am spinning?'

'She speaks,' thought Irene, 'just as if she had seen me five minutes ago, or yesterday at the farthest. --No,' she answered; 'I don't know what you are spinning. Please, I thought you were a dream. Why couldn't I find you before, great-great-grandmother?'

'That you are hardly old enough to understand. But you would have found me sooner if you hadn't come to think I was a dream. I will give you one reason though why you couldn't find me. I didn't want you to find me.'

'Why, please?'

'Because I did not want Lootie to know I was here.'

'But you told me to tell Lootie.'

'Yes. But I knew Lootie would not believe you. If she were to see me sitting spinning here, she wouldn't believe me, either.'

'Why?'

'Because she couldn't. She would rub her eyes, and go away and say she felt strange, and forget half of it and more, and then say it had been all a dream.'

'Just like me,' said Irene, feeling very much ashamed of herself.

'Yes, a good deal like you, but not just like you; for you've come again; and Lootie wouldn't have come again. She would have said, No, no--she had had enough of such nonsense.'

'Is it naughty of Lootie, then?'

'It would be bad of you. I have never done anything for Lootie.'

'And you did wash my face and hands for me,' said Irene, starting to cry.

The old lady smiled a sweet smile and said:

'I am not angry with you, my child—nor with Lootie either. But I do not want you to say anything more to Lootie about me. If she asks you, you must just be silent. But I do not think she will ask you.'

All the time they talked the old lady kept on spinning.

'You have not told me yet what I am spinning,' she said.

'Because I do not know. It is very pretty stuff.'

It was indeed very pretty stuff. There was a good bunch of it on the stick attached to the spinning-wheel. In the moonlight it shone like—what shall I say it was like? It was not white enough for silver—yes, it was like silver, but shone grey rather than white, and glittered only a little. The thread the old lady pulled out from it was so fine that Irene could hardly see it. 'I am spinning this for you, my child.'

'For me! What am I to do with it, please?'

'I will tell you later. But first I will tell you what it is. It is spider-web—of a special kind. My pigeons bring it to me from over the great sea. There is only one forest where the spiders live who make this special kind—the finest and strongest of any. I have almost finished my present job. What is on the rock now will be enough. I still have a week of work there, though,' she added, looking at the bunch.

'Do you work all day and all night, too, great-great-great-great-grandmother?' said the princess, trying to be very polite with so many greats.

'I am not quite so great as all that,' she answered, smiling almost happily. 'If you call me grandmother, that will do. No, I do not work every night—only nights with moonlight, and then no longer than the moon shines upon my wheel. I will not work much longer tonight.'

'And what will you do next, grandmother?'

'Go to bed. Would you like to see my bedroom?'

'Yes, that I would.'

'Then I think I will not work any longer tonight. I will be in good time.'

The old lady rose, and left her wheel standing just as it was. You see there was no good in putting it away. Where there was no furniture there was no danger of being untidy.

Then she took Irene by the hand, but it was her bad hand and Irene gave a little cry of pain. 'My child!' said her grandmother, 'what is the matter?'

Irene held her hand into the moonlight, so the old lady might see it, and told her all about it. At this she looked serious. But she only said: 'Give me your other hand.' She led her out onto the little dark landing and opened the door on the opposite side. What was Irene's surprise to see the loveliest room she had ever seen in her life! It was large and high, and shaped like a dome. From the middle hung a lamp as round as a ball. It shone as if with the brightest moonlight, which made everything visible in the room. But it was not so clear that the princess could tell what many of the things were. A large oval bed stood in the middle, with a cover of rose colour, and velvet curtains all round it of a lovely pale blue. The walls were also blue—spangled all over with what looked like stars of silver.

The old lady left her and went to a strange-looking cabinet. She opened it and took out a curious silver box. Then she sat down on a low chair and called Irene. She made her kneel before her while she looked at her hand. Having examined it, she opened the box and took from it a little ointment. The sweetest smell filled the room—like that of roses and lilies—as she rubbed the ointment gently all over the hot swollen hand. Her touch was so pleasant and cool that it seemed to drive away the pain and heat wherever it came.

'Oh, grandmother! it is so nice!' said Irene. 'Thank you; thank you.'

Then the old lady went to a chest of drawers and took out a large handkerchief of fine thin cloth. She tied it round her hand.

'I do not think I can let you go away tonight,' she said. 'Would you like to sleep with me?'

'Oh, yes, yes, dear grandmother,' said Irene, and would have clapped her hands, forgetting that she could not.

'You will not be afraid, then, to go to bed with such an old woman?'

'No. You are so beautiful, grandmother.'

'But I am very old.'

'And I suppose I am very young. You will not mind sleeping with such a very young woman, grandmother?'

'You sweet little bold girl!' said the old lady, and drew her towards her. She kissed her on the forehead and the cheek and the mouth. Then she got a large silver basin, and having poured some water into it made Irene sit on the chair, and washed her feet. This done, she was ready for bed. And oh, what a delicious bed it was into which her grandmother laid her! She hardly could have told she was lying upon anything: she felt nothing but the softness.

The old lady having undressed herself lay down beside her.

'Why do you not put out your moon?' asked the princess.

'That never goes out, night or day,' she answered. 'In the darkest night, if any of my pigeons are out on a message, they always see my moon and know where to fly to.'

'But if somebody besides the pigeons were to see it—somebody about the house, I mean—they would come to look what it was and find you.'

'The better for them, then,' said the old lady. 'But it does not happen above five times in a hundred years that anyone does see it. Most of those who do take it for a meteor, close their eyes, and forget it again. Besides, nobody could find the room except I pleased. Besides, again—I will tell you a secret—if that light were to go out you would think yourself lying in a bare attic, on a heap of old straw. You would not see one of the pleasant things round about you all the time.'

'I hope it will never go out,' said the princess.

'I hope not. But it is time we both went to sleep. Shall I take you in my arms?'

The little princess nestled close up to the old lady. She took her in both her arms and held her close to her chest.

'Oh, dear! this is so nice!' said the princess. 'I did not know anything in the world could be so comfortable. I should like to lie here for ever.'

'You may if you want to,' said the old lady. 'But I must give you one test. I hope it is not a very hard one. This night next week you must come back to me. If you do not, I do not know when you may find me again. And you will soon want me very much.'

'Oh! please, do not let me forget.'

'You shall not forget. The only question is whether you will believe I am somewhere. Will you believe I am anything but a dream? You may be sure I will do all I can to help you to come. But it will rest with yourself, after all. On the night of next Friday, you must come to me. Remember now.'

'I will try,' said the princess.

'Then good night,' said the old lady. She kissed the forehead that lay against her chest.

In a moment more the little princess was dreaming. She was in the middle of the loveliest dreams. She dreamed of summer seas and soft light and wet springs and great whispering trees. She dreamed of beds of wild flowers with such smells as she had never smelled before. But, after all, no dream could be more lovely than what she had left behind when she fell asleep.

In the morning she found herself in her own bed. There was no cloth or anything else on her hand. Only a sweet smell stayed about it. The swelling had all gone down. The mark of the pin had vanished. In fact, her hand was perfectly well.

CHAPTER 12. A Short Chapter About Curdie

A Short Chapter About Curdie

Curdie spent many nights in the mine. His father and he had told Mrs. Peterson the secret. They knew mother could keep quiet about it. The same could not be said of all the miners' wives.

But Curdie did not tell her this: every night he spent in the mine, part of the time went to earning a new red petticoat for her.

Mrs. Peterson was such a nice good mother! All mothers are nice and good more or less. But Mrs. Peterson was nice and good all more and no less. She made and kept a little heaven in that poor small house on the high hillside. Her husband and son came home to it out of the low and rather sad earth in which they worked. I doubt if the princess was much happier even in the arms of her huge great-grandmother than Peter and Curdie were in the arms of Mrs. Peterson. True, her hands were hard and rough and large. But it was from work for them. So, in the sight of the angels, her hands were much more beautiful. And if Curdie worked hard to get her a petticoat, she worked hard every day to get him comforts. He would have missed those comforts much more than she would a new petticoat even in winter. Not that she and Curdie ever thought of how much they worked for each other. That would have spoiled everything.

When left alone in the mine, Curdie always worked on for an hour or two at first. He followed the lode which, according to Glump, would lead at last into the deserted home. After that, he would set out to look around. To manage this better than the first time, or rather the return from it, he had bought a huge ball of fine string. He had learned the trick from Hop-o'-my-Thumb, whose history his mother had often told him. Not that Hop-o'-my-Thumb had ever used a ball of string--I should be sorry to be thought so far out in my classics--but the principle was the same as that of the pebbles. The end of this string he tied to his pickaxe. The pickaxe made a good enough anchor. Then, with the ball in his hand, he let it out as he went. He set out in the dark through the natural gangs of the goblins' territory. The first night or two he came upon nothing worth remembering. He saw only a little of the home life of the cobs in the various caves they called houses. He failed to come upon anything to cast light upon the earlier plan. That plan kept the flood for the present in the background. But at length, I think on the third or fourth night, he found a company of workers. He was partly guided by the noise of their tools. They were clearly the best sappers and miners amongst them, hard at work. What were they about? It could not well be the flood, since that had in the meantime been put off for something else. Then what was it? He hid and watched. Every now and then he was in the greatest risk of being detected, but without success. He had again and again to go back in haste. This was made more difficult because he had to gather up his string as he returned upon its course. It was not that he was afraid of the goblins. He was afraid of their finding out that they were watched. That might have prevented the discovery at which he aimed. Sometimes his haste had to be such that, when he reached home towards morning, his string would be in what seemed most hopeless entanglement. This happened because he lacked time to wind it up as he dodged the cobs. But after a good sleep, though a short one, he always found his mother had got it right again. There it was, wound in a most respectable ball, ready for use the moment he should want it!

'I can't think how you do it, mother,' he would say.

'I follow the thread,' she would answer--'just as you do in the mine.' She never had more to say about it. But the less clever she was with her words, the more clever she was with her hands. And the less his mother said, the more Curdie believed she had to say. But still he had made no discovery as to what the goblin miners were about.

CHAPTER 13. The Cobs' Creatures

The Cobs' Creatures

About this time the gentlemen whom the king had left behind him to watch over the princess each had reason to doubt what their own eyes told them. For the things they saw were more than strange. They were all of one kind—creatures—but so strange and badly formed that they looked more like a child's drawings on a slate than anything natural. They saw them only at night, while on guard around the house. The man who first reported seeing one of them said this: he was walking slowly round the house, still in the shadow, when he caught sight of a creature standing on its back legs in the moonlight. Its front feet were on a window ledge, and it was staring in at the window. Its body might have been that of a dog or wolf, he thought. But he declared on his honour that its head was twice the size it should have been for the size of its body, and as round as a ball. The face, which it turned on him as it fled, was more like one carved by a boy on the turnip inside which he is going to put a candle than anything else he could think of. It rushed into the garden. He sent an arrow after it and thought he must have hit it. For it gave a strange, unearthly howl, and he could not find his arrow any more than the beast, though he searched all about the place where it vanished. They laughed at him until he was forced to hold his tongue. They said he must have taken too long a pull at the ale jug.

But before two nights were over he had one man on his side. For this man too had seen something strange, only quite different from what the other had reported. The second man's description of the creature he had seen was even more strange and unlikely. The rest laughed at both of them. But night after night another man came over to their side, until at last there was only one left to laugh at all his companions. Two more nights passed, and he saw nothing. But on the third he came rushing from the garden to the other two in front of the house. He was in such a state that they declared—for it was their turn now—that the band of his helmet was cracking under his chin as his hair rose inside it. They ran with him into that part of the garden I have already described. There they saw a score of creatures, not one of which they could give a name to, and not one of which was like another. They were hideous and funny at the same time, jumping and playing on the lawn in the moonlight. The strange ugliness of their faces, the length of legs and necks in some, the apparent absence of both or either in others, made the watchers doubtful of what their own eyes told them—and their ears as well. For though the noises they made were not loud, they were as rough and varied as their forms. They could be described neither as grunts nor squeaks nor roars nor howls nor barks nor yells nor screams nor croaks nor hisses nor mews nor shrieks. They were only something like all of them mixed in one horrible sound. Keeping in the shade, the watchers had a few moments to recover themselves before the hideous group suspected they were there. But all at once, as if by common consent, they ran off toward a great rock and vanished before the men had come to themselves enough to think of following them.

My readers will guess what these were. But I will now give them full information about them. They were, of course, household animals belonging to the goblins. Their ancestors had taken their ancestors many centuries before from the upper regions of light into the lower regions of darkness. The original stocks of these horrible creatures were very much the same as the animals now seen about farms and homes in the country. A few of them were exceptions, which had been wild creatures, such as foxes, and indeed wolves and small bears. The goblins, from their liking for animal creatures, had caught these when they were cubs and tamed them. But in the course of time all had undergone even greater changes than had passed upon their owners. They had altered—that is, their descendants had altered—into such creatures as I have not attempted to describe except in the vaguest manner. The various parts of their bodies took on, in an apparently arbitrary and self-willed manner, the most abnormal developments. Indeed, so little did any distinct type predominate in some of the bewildering results that you could only have guessed at any known animal as the original. And even then, what likeness remained would be more one of general expression than of definable shape. But what increased the gruesomeness tenfold was that, from constant domestic, or indeed rather family association with the goblins, their faces had grown in strange resemblance to the human.

No one understands animals who does not see that every one of them, even among the fishes, it may be with a dimness and vagueness infinitely remote, yet shadows the human. In the case of these the human resemblance had greatly increased. While their owners had sunk towards them, they had risen towards their owners. But the conditions of underground life being equally unnatural for both, while the goblins were worse, the creatures had not improved by the approximation. Its result would have appeared far more funny than comforting to the warmest lover of animal nature. I shall now explain how it was that just then these animals began to show themselves about the king's country house.

The goblins, as Curdie had discovered, were mining on—at work both day and night, in divisions, urging the scheme after which he lay in wait. In the course of their tunnelling they had broken into the channel of a small stream. But the break being in the top of it, no water had escaped to interfere with their work. Some of the creatures, hovering as they often did about their masters, had found the hole. With the curiosity which had grown to a passion from the restraints of their unnatural circumstances, they proceeded to explore the channel. The stream was the same which ran out by the seat on which Irene and her king-papa had sat as I have told. The goblin creatures found it jolly fun to get out for a romp on a smooth lawn such as they had never seen in all their poor miserable lives. But although they had partaken enough of the nature of their owners to delight in annoying and alarming any of the people whom they met on the mountain, they were, of course, incapable of designs of their own, or of intentionally furthering those of their masters.

For several nights, the men-at-arms finally agreed that some horrible creatures had visited the garden. They still could not say if the creatures had bodies or were like ghosts. They watched with special attention the part of the garden where they had last seen them. Perhaps because of this, they paid too little attention to the house. But the creatures were too clever to be caught easily. The watchers were not quick-eyed enough to see the head, or the sharp eyes in it. From the opening where the stream came out, the head would watch them in turn. It was ready to report the place clear the moment they left the lawn.

CHAPTER 14. That Night Week

That Night Week

During the whole week Irene had been thinking every other moment of her promise to the old lady. Even now she could not feel quite sure that she had not been dreaming. Could it really be that an old lady lived up in the top of the house? Could she really have pigeons and a spinning-wheel and a lamp that never went out? She was, however, just as determined on the coming Friday to go up the three stairs. She would walk through the passages with the many doors. She would try to find the tower in which she had either seen or dreamed her grandmother.

Her nurse could not help wondering what had come to the child. She would sit so quietly silent, and even in the middle of a game with her she would so suddenly fall into a far-away mood. But Irene took care to show nothing, whatever efforts Lootie might make to get at her thoughts. And Lootie had to say to herself, "What an odd child she is!" and give it up.

At last the long-wished-for Friday arrived. Irene tried to keep herself as quiet as possible, in case Lootie should decide to watch her. In the afternoon she asked for her doll house. She went on placing and replacing the different rooms and their people for a whole hour. Then she gave a sigh and threw herself back in her chair. One of the dolls would not sit, and another would not stand, and they were all very annoying. Indeed, there was one that would not even lie down, which was too bad. But it was now getting dark. The darker it got, the more excited Irene became, and the more she felt it necessary to be calm.

"I see you want your tea, princess," said the nurse. "I will go and get it. The room feels close. I will open the window a little. The evening is mild. It will not hurt you."

"There's no fear of that, Lootie," said Irene. She wished she had put off going for the tea until it was darker, when she might have made her attempt with every advantage.

I think Lootie was longer in returning than she had intended. When Irene, who had been lost in thought, looked up, she saw it was nearly dark. At the same moment she caught sight of a pair of eyes, bright with a green light, looking angrily at her through the open window. The next instant something jumped into the room. It was like a cat, with legs as long as a horse's, Irene said, but its body was no bigger and its legs no thicker than those of a cat. She was too afraid to cry out, but not too afraid to jump from her chair and run from the room.

It is plain enough to every one of my readers what she ought to have done. Indeed, Irene thought of it herself. But when she came to the foot of the old stair, just outside the nursery door, she imagined the creature running up those long climbs after her. She imagined it pursuing her through the dark passages, which, after all, might lead to no tower. That thought was too much. Her heart failed her. She turned from the stair and rushed along to the hall. There she found the front door open, and she ran out into the court, pursued—at least she thought so—by the creature. No one happened to see her. On she ran, unable to think for fear, and ready to run anywhere to escape the awful creature with the long legs. Not daring to look behind her, she rushed straight out of the gate and up the mountain. It was indeed foolish to run farther and farther from all who could help her. It was as if she had been seeking a fit spot for the goblin creature to eat her in his free time. But that is the way fear serves us: it always sides with the thing we are afraid of.

The princess was soon out of breath from running uphill. But she kept running. She felt the horrible creature was just behind her. She forgot that such long legs would have caught her long ago if it had been following her. At last she could run no more. She fell by the side of the road. She could not even scream. She lay there for some time, half dead with fear. But nothing grabbed her. Her breath began to come back. At last she dared to get half up and look around her with fear. It was now so dark she could see nothing. Not a single star was out. She could not even tell which way the house lay. She felt the terrible creature was lying between her and home, ready to jump on her. She saw now that she should have run up the steps at once. It was good that she did not scream. Very few of the goblins had come out for weeks. But one or two lazy ones might have heard her. She sat down on a stone. Nobody but someone who had done something wrong could have been more unhappy. She had quite forgotten her promise to visit her grandmother. A drop of rain fell on her face. She looked up. For a moment her fear was lost in wonder. At first she thought the rising moon had left her place. She thought it had come near to see what was wrong with the little girl. The girl sat alone, without hat or coat, on the dark bare mountain. But she soon saw she was wrong. There was no light on the ground at her feet. There was no shadow anywhere. But a great silver ball was hanging in the air. As she looked at the lovely thing, her courage came back. If she were inside again, she would fear nothing. She would not even fear the terrible creature with the long legs! But how was she to find her way back? What could that light be? Could it be--? No, it could not. But what if it should be--yes--it must be--her great-great-grandmother's lamp! It guided her pigeons home through the darkest night! She jumped up. She only had to keep that light in view and she must find the house. Her heart grew strong. She walked down the hill quickly but softly. She hoped to pass the watching creature without being seen. It was dark, but there was little danger now of choosing the wrong road. And--which was most strange--the light from the lamp filled her eyes. Instead of blinding her for a moment to the next thing she looked at, it let her see it for a moment, even in the darkness. She looked at the lamp and then dropped her eyes. Then she could see the road for a yard or two in front of her. This saved her from several falls, for the road was very rough. But all at once, to her fear, it vanished. The fear of the beast had left her when she began to return. Now it took hold of her heart again. The same instant, however, she caught the light of the windows. She knew exactly where she was. It was too dark to run, but she went as fast as she could. She reached the gate in safety. She found the house door still open. She ran through the hall. She did not even look into the children's room. She ran straight up the steps, and the next, and the next. Then she turned to the right. She ran through the long row of silent rooms. She found her way at once to the door at the foot of the tower steps.

When the nurse first missed her, she thought the princess was playing a trick on her. For some time she did not worry about her. But at last she got frightened and began to search. When the princess entered, the whole household was running here and there over the house, hunting for her. A few seconds after she reached the steps of the tower, they had even begun to search the forgotten rooms. They would never have thought of looking there if they had not already searched every other place they could think of, with no result. But by this time she was knocking at the old lady's door.

CHAPTER 15. Woven and Then Spun

Woven and Then Spun

'Come in, Irene,' said the silvery voice of her grandmother.

The princess opened the door and looked in. But the room was quite dark and there was no sound of the spinning-wheel. She grew afraid once more. She thought that, although the room was there, the old lady might be a dream after all. Every little girl knows how terrible it is to find a room empty where she thought somebody was. But Irene had to think for a moment that the person she came to find was nowhere at all. She remembered, however, that at night she spun only in the moonlight. She decided that must be why there was no sweet, bee-like humming. The old lady might be somewhere in the darkness. Before she had time to think another thought, she heard her voice again, saying as before: 'Come in, Irene.' From the sound, she understood at once that she was not in the room beside her. Perhaps she was in her bedroom. She turned across the passage, feeling her way to the other door. When her hand fell on the lock, again the old lady spoke:

'Shut the other door behind you, Irene. I always close the door of my workroom when I go to my room.'

Irene wondered to hear her voice so clearly through the door. Having shut the other, she opened it and went in. Oh, what a lovely safe place to reach from the darkness and fear through which she had come! The soft light made her feel as if she were going into the heart of the whitest pearl. The blue walls and their silver stars for a moment puzzled her. She thought for a moment that they were really the sky which she had left outside a minute ago covered with rainclouds.

'I've lighted a fire for you, Irene: you are cold and wet,' said her grandmother.

Then Irene looked again. She saw that what she had taken for a huge bunch of red roses on a low stand against the wall was in fact a fire. It burned in the shapes of the loveliest and reddest roses. It shone brightly between the heads and wings of two cherubs of shining silver. And when she came nearer, she found that the smell of roses with which the room was filled came from the fire-roses on the hearth. Her grandmother was dressed in the loveliest pale blue velvet. Over it her hair streamed like a waterfall. It was no longer white, but of a rich golden colour. Here it fell in dull gathered heaps, there it rushed away in smooth shining falls. And ever as she looked, the hair seemed pouring down from her head. It seemed to vanish in a golden mist before it reached the floor. It flowed from under the edge of a circle of shining silver, set with pearls and opals in turn. On her dress was no ornament whatever. Neither was there a ring on her hand, or a necklace or carcanet about her neck. But her slippers glimmered with the light of the Milky Way, for they were covered with seed-pearls and opals in one mass. Her face was that of a woman of three-and-twenty.

The princess was so confused with astonishment and admiration that she could hardly thank her. She drew near with shyness, feeling dirty and uncomfortable. The lady was seated on a low chair by the side of the fire, with hands outstretched to take her. But the princess hung back with a troubled smile.

'Why, what is the matter?' asked her grandmother. 'You have not been doing anything wrong--I know that by your face, though it is rather sad. What is the matter, my dear?'

And she still held out her arms.

'Dear grandmother,' said Irene, 'I am not so sure that I have not done something wrong. I ought to have run up to you at once when the long-legged cat came in at the window. Instead I ran out on the mountain and made myself such a fright.'

'You were taken by surprise, my child, and you are not so likely to do it again. It is when people do wrong things on purpose that they are the more likely to do them again. Come.'

And still she held out her arms.

'But, grandmother, you are so beautiful and grand with your crown on; and I am so dirty with mud and rain! I should quite spoil your beautiful blue dress.'

With a merry little laugh the lady sprung from her chair, more lightly far than Irene herself could. She caught the child to her bosom, and, kissing the tear-stained face over and over, sat down with her in her lap.

'Oh, grandmother! You will make yourself such a mess!' cried Irene, clinging to her.

'You darling! do you think I care more for my dress than for my little girl? Besides--look here.'

As she spoke she set her down. Irene saw to her dismay that the lovely dress was covered with the mud of her fall on the mountain road. But the lady stooped to the fire. She took from it, by the stalk in her fingers, one of the burning roses. She passed it once and again and a third time over the front of her dress. And when Irene looked, not a single stain was to be discovered.

'There!' said her grandmother, 'you will not mind coming to me now?'

But Irene again hung back, eying the flaming rose which the lady held in her hand.

'You are not afraid of the rose--are you?' she said, about to throw it on the hearth again.

'Oh! do not, please!' cried Irene. 'Will you not hold it to my frock and my hands and my face? And I am afraid my feet and my knees want it too.'

'No,' answered her grandmother, smiling a little sadly, as she threw the rose from her. 'It is too hot for you yet. It would set your frock in a flame. Besides, I do not want to make you clean tonight.

I want your nurse and the rest of the people to see you as you are. You will have to tell them how you ran away for fear of the long-legged cat. I should like to wash you, but they would not believe you then. Do you see that bath behind you?'

The princess looked, and saw a large oval tub of silver, shining brilliantly in the light of the wonderful lamp.

'Go and look into it,' said the lady.

Irene went, and came back very silent with her eyes shining.

'What did you see?' asked her grandmother.

'The sky, and the moon and the stars,' she answered. 'It looked as if there was no bottom to it.'

The lady smiled a pleased satisfied smile, and was silent also for a few moments. Then she said:

'Any time you want a bath, come to me. I know you have a bath every morning, but sometimes you want one at night, too.'

'Thank you, grandmother; I will—I will indeed,' answered Irene, and was silent again for some moments, thinking. Then she said: 'How was it, grandmother, that I saw your beautiful lamp—not only the light of it—but the great round silver lamp itself, hanging alone in the great open air, high up? It was your lamp I saw—was it not?'

'Yes, my child—it was my lamp.'

'Then how was it? I do not see a window anywhere around.'

'When I please, I can make the lamp shine through the walls. It shines so strong that it melts them away from before the sight, and shows itself as you saw it. But, as I told you, not everybody can see it.'

'How is it that I can, then? I am sure I do not know.'

'It is a gift born with you. And one day I hope everybody will have it.'

'But how do you make it shine through the walls?'

'Ah! you would not understand that if I tried ever so much to make you—not yet—not yet. But,' added the lady, rising, 'you must sit in my chair while I get you the present I have been preparing for you. I told you my spinning was for you. It is finished now, and I am going to get it. I have been keeping it warm under one of my sitting pigeons.'

Irene sat down in the low chair, and her grandmother left her, shutting the door behind her. The child sat looking, now at the rose fire, now at the walls full of stars, now at the silver light. And a great quiet grew in her heart. If all the long-legged cats in the world had come rushing at her then, she would not have been afraid of them for a moment. How this was she could not tell. She only knew there was no fear in her, and everything was so right and safe that it could not get in.

She had been looking at the lovely lamp for some minutes without moving her eyes. Then she turned her eyes, and found the wall had gone. She was looking out on the dark cloudy night. But though she heard the wind blowing, none of it blew upon her. In a moment more the clouds themselves parted, or rather vanished like the wall, and she looked straight into the herds of stars, shining gloriously in the dark blue. It was but for a moment. The clouds gathered again and shut out the stars. The wall gathered again and shut out the clouds. And there stood the lady beside her with the loveliest smile on her face, and a shining ball in her hand, about the size of a pigeon's egg.

'There, Irene; there is my work for you!' she said, holding out the ball to the princess.

She took it in her hand, and looked at it all over. It sparkled a little, and shone here and there, but not much. It was of a sort of grey-white, something like spun glass.

'Is this all your spinning, grandmother?' she asked.

'All since you came to the house. There is more there than you think.'

'How pretty it is! What am I to do with it, please?'

'That I will now explain to you,' answered the lady, turning from her and going to her cabinet. She came back with a small ring in her hand. Then she took the ball from Irene's hand, and did something with the ring—Irene could not tell what.

'Give me your hand,' she said. Irene held up her right hand.

'Yes, that is the hand I want,' said the lady, and put the ring on the forefinger of it.

'What a beautiful ring!' said Irene. 'What is the stone called?'

'It is a fire-opal.'

'Please, am I to keep it?'

'Always.'

'Oh, thank you, grandmother! It is prettier than anything I ever saw, except those—of all colours—in your—Please, is that your crown?'

'Yes, it is my crown. The stone in your ring is of the same sort—only not so good. It has only red, but mine have all colours, you see.'

'Yes, grandmother. I will take such care of it! But—' she added, hesitating.

'But what?' asked her grandmother.

'What am I to say when Lootie asks me where I got it?'

'You will ask her where you got it,' answered the lady smiling.

'I do not see how I can do that.'

'You will, though.'

'Of course I will, if you say so. But, you know, I cannot pretend not to know.'

'Of course not. But do not trouble yourself about it. You will see when the time comes.'

So saying, the lady turned, and threw the little ball into the rose fire.

'Oh, grandmother!' exclaimed Irene; 'I thought you had spun it for me.'

'So I did, my child. And you have got it.'

'No; it is burnt in the fire!'

The lady put her hand in the fire, brought out the ball, shining as before, and held it towards her. Irene stretched out her hand to take it, but the lady turned and, going to her cabinet, opened a drawer, and laid the ball in it.

'Have I done anything to vex you, grandmother?' said Irene pitifully.

'No, my darling. But you must understand that no one ever gives anything to another properly and really without keeping it. That ball is yours.'

'Oh! I am not to take it with me! You are going to keep it for me!'

'You are to take it with you. I have fastened the end of it to the ring on your finger.'

Irene looked at the ring.

'I cannot see it there, grandmother,' she said.

'Feel—a little way from the ring—towards the cabinet,' said the lady.

'Oh! I do feel it!' exclaimed the princess. 'But I cannot see it,' she added, looking close to her outstretched hand.

'No. The thread is too fine for you to see it. You can only feel it. Now you can fancy how much spinning that took, although it does seem such a little ball.'

'But what use can I make of it, if it lies in your cabinet?'

'That is what I will explain to you. It would be of no use to you—it would not be yours at all if it did not lie in my cabinet. Now listen. If ever you find yourself in any danger—such, for example, as you were in this same evening—you must take off your ring and put it under the pillow of your bed. Then you must lay your finger, the same that wore the ring, upon the thread, and follow the thread wherever it leads you.'

'Oh, how delightful! It will lead me to you, grandmother, I know!'

'Yes. But, remember, it may seem to you a very roundabout way indeed, and you must not doubt the thread. Of one thing you may be sure, that while you hold it, I hold it too.'

'It is very wonderful!' said Irene thoughtfully. Then suddenly becoming aware, she jumped up, crying:

'Oh, grandmother! here have I been sitting all this time in your chair, and you standing! I beg your pardon.'

The lady laid her hand on her shoulder, and said:

'Sit down again, Irene. Nothing makes me happier than to see someone sit in my chair. I am very happy to stand as long as someone will sit in it.'

'How kind of you!' said the princess, and sat down again.

'It makes me happy,' said the lady.

'But,' said Irene, still confused, 'will the thread get in someone's way and break? One end is tied to my ring, and the other is in your cabinet.'

'You will find that it all works out by itself. I am afraid it is time for you to go.'

'May I stay and sleep with you tonight, grandmother?'

'No, not tonight. If I had wanted you to stay tonight, I would have given you a bath. But you know everyone in the house is unhappy about you. It would be unkind to keep them so all night. You must go downstairs.'

'I am so glad, grandmother, you did not say "Go home," for this is my home. May I call this my home?'

'You may, my child. And I hope you will always think it your home. Now come. I must take you back without anyone seeing you.'

'Please, I want to ask you one more question,' said Irene. 'Is it because you have your crown on that you look so young?'

'No, child,' answered her grandmother. 'It is because I felt so young this evening that I put my crown on. And I thought you would like to see your old grandmother in her best.'

'Why do you call yourself old? You are not old, grandmother.'

'I am very old indeed. It is so silly of people—I do not mean you, for you are so tiny, and could not know better—but it is so silly of people to think that old age means a bent body and dry skin and weakness and sticks and glasses and sore joints and bad memory! It is so silly! Old age has nothing to do with all that. The right old age means strength and beauty and laughter and courage and clear eyes and strong limbs without pain. I am older than you can imagine, and—'

'And look at you, grandmother!' cried Irene, jumping up and throwing her arms around her neck. 'I will not be so silly again, I promise you. At least—I am rather afraid to promise—but if I am, I promise to be sorry for it—I do. I wish I were as old as you, grandmother. I do not think you are ever afraid of anything.'

'Not for long, at least, my child. Perhaps by the time I am two thousand years of age, I shall truly never be afraid of anything. But I confess I have sometimes been afraid about my children—sometimes about you, Irene.'

'Oh, I am so sorry, grandmother! Tonight, I suppose, you mean.'

'Yes—a little tonight. But a good deal when you had almost decided that I was a dream, and not a real great-great-grandmother. You must not think I am blaming you for that. I dare say you could not help it.'

'I do not know, grandmother,' said the princess, beginning to cry. 'I cannot always do as I would like. And I do not always try. I am very sorry anyway.'

The lady bent down, lifted her in her arms, and sat down with her in her chair, holding her close to her chest. In a few minutes the princess had cried herself to sleep. How long she slept I do not know. When she came to herself she was sitting in her own high chair at the nursery table, with her doll's house before her.

CHAPTER 16. The Ring

The same moment her nurse came into the room, crying. When she saw her sitting there she stepped back with a loud cry of surprise and joy. Then she ran to her and caught her in her arms. She covered her with kisses.

'My precious dear princess! where have you been? What has happened to you? We have all been crying our eyes out. We have been searching the house from top to bottom for you.'

'Not quite from the top,' thought Irene to herself. And she might have added, 'not quite to the bottom', perhaps, if she had known all. But the one she would not say, and the other she could not say. 'Oh, Lootie! I have had such a terrible adventure!' she replied. She told her all about the cat with the long legs, and how she ran out upon the mountain, and came back again. But she said nothing of her grandmother or her lamp.

'And we have been searching for you all over the house for more than an hour and a half!' cried the nurse. 'But that is no matter, now we have got you! Only, princess, I must say,' she added, her mood changing, 'what you should have done was to call for your own Lootie to come and help you. You should not have run out of the house and up the mountain in that wild and, I must say, silly way.'

'Well, Lootie,' said Irene quietly, 'perhaps if you had a big cat, all legs, running at you, you might not exactly know what was the wisest thing to do at the moment.'

'I would not run up the mountain, anyway,' returned Lootie.

'Not if you had time to think about it. But when those creatures came at you that night on the mountain, you were so frightened yourself that you lost your way home.'

This put a stop to Lootie's blame. She had been about to say that the long-legged cat must have been a half-light fancy of the princess. But the memory of the horrors of that night stopped her. So did the memory of the talk the king had given her because of it. So she did not say what after all she did not half believe. She had a strong suspicion that the cat was a goblin. For she knew nothing of the difference between the goblins and their creatures. She counted them all just goblins.

Without another word she went and got some fresh tea and bread and butter for the princess. Before she returned, the whole household came into the nursery. The housekeeper led them, and they all rejoiced over their dear one. The gentlemen-at-arms followed. They were ready enough to believe all she told them about the long-legged cat. Indeed, they were wise enough to say nothing about it. But they remembered, with no little horror, just such a creature among those they had surprised at their play upon the princess's lawn.

In their own hearts they blamed themselves for not having kept better watch. And their captain gave orders. From this night the front door and all the windows on the ground floor should be locked as soon as the sun set. They should be opened after that on no excuse whatever. The men-at-arms doubled their watch, and for some time there was no further cause of alarm.

When the princess woke the next morning, her nurse was bending over her. 'How your ring does shine this morning, princess! Just like a rose of fire!' she said.

'Does it, Lootie?' returned Irene. 'Who gave me the ring, Lootie? I know I have had it a long time, but where did I get it? I do not remember.'

'I think it must have been your mother gave it to you, princess. But really, for as long as you have worn it, I do not remember that I ever heard,' answered her nurse.

'I will ask my king-papa the next time he comes,' said Irene.

CHAPTER 17. Springtime

Springtime

The spring that all creatures love, young and old, came at last. Before its first few days had passed, the king rode through its budding valleys to see his little daughter. He had been in a distant part of his lands all winter. He did not usually stay in one great city, or visit only his best-loved country houses. He moved from place to place so that all his people might know him. Wherever he traveled, he always looked for the most able and best men to give office to. Wherever he found he had been wrong, and those he had appointed could not do the job or were unfair, he removed them at once. So you see it was his care for the people that kept him from seeing his princess as often as he would have liked. You may wonder why he did not take her about with him. But there were several reasons against doing so, and I suspect her great-great-grandmother had a main hand in preventing it. Once more Irene heard the horn call, and once more she was at the gate to meet her father as he rode up on his great white horse.

After they had been alone for a little while, she thought of what she had decided to ask him.

'Please, king-papa,' she said, 'Will you tell me where I got this pretty ring? I can't remember.'

The king looked at it. A strange beautiful smile spread like sunshine over his face. A smile came back on Irene's face too, but at the same time it was a questioning one, like moonlight. 'It was your queen-mamma's once,' he said.

'And why isn't it hers now?' asked Irene.

'She does not want it now,' said the king, looking grave.

'Why doesn't she want it now?'

'Because she's gone where all those rings are made.'

'And when shall I see her?' asked the princess.

'Not for some time yet,' answered the king, and tears came into his eyes.

Irene did not remember her mother. She did not know why her father looked so, and why the tears came into his eyes. But she put her arms round his neck and kissed him, and asked no more questions.

The king was much troubled when he heard the report of the gentlemen-at-arms about the creatures they had seen. I think he would have taken Irene with him that very day, but for what the ring on her finger told him. About an hour before he left, Irene saw him go up the old stair. He did not come down again until they were just ready to start. She thought to herself that he had been up to see the old lady. When he went away he left six other gentlemen behind him, so that there might always be six of them on guard.

And now, in the lovely spring weather, Irene was out on the mountain for most of the day. In the warmer hollows there were lovely primroses, and not so many that she ever got tired of them. Whenever she saw a new one opening an eye of light in the blind earth, she would clap her hands with joy. Unlike some children I know, she did not pull it. She would touch it as gently as if it had been a new baby. Having made its acquaintance, she would leave it as happy as she found it. She treated the plants they grew on like birds' nests. Every fresh flower was like a new little bird to her. She would visit all the flower-nests she knew, remembering each one by itself. She would go down on her hands and knees beside one and say: 'Good morning! Are you all smelling very sweet this morning? Good-bye!' Then she would go to another nest and say the same. It was a best-loved amusement with her. There were many flowers up and down, and she loved them all, but the primroses were her best-loved.

'They're not too shy, and they're not a bit forward,' she would say to Lootie.

There were goats too about, over the mountain, and when the little kids came she was as pleased with them as with the flowers. The goats belonged to the miners mostly—a few of them to Curdie's mother. But there were a good many wild ones that seemed to belong to nobody. The goblins counted these as theirs, and it was partly on them that they lived. They set traps and dug pits for them. They did not hesitate to take any tame ones that happened to be caught. But they did not try to steal them in any other way, because they were afraid of the dogs the hill-people kept to watch them. The knowing dogs always tried to bite their feet. But the goblins had a kind of sheep of their own—very strange creatures, which they drove out to feed at night. The other goblin creatures were wise enough to keep good watch over them, for they knew they should have their bones by and by.

CHAPTER 18. Curdie's Clue

Curdie's Clue

Curdie was as watchful as ever. But he was almost getting tired of his bad luck. Every other night or so, he followed the goblins about. They went on digging and boring. He got as near them as he could. He watched them from behind stones and rocks. But so far he seemed no nearer finding out what they planned. As at first, he always kept hold of the end of his string. His pickaxe was left just outside the hole. He entered the goblins' country from the mine by that hole. The pickaxe still served as an anchor and held fast the other end. The goblins heard no more noise in that quarter. They had stopped fearing an immediate invasion. They kept no watch.

One night he moved about quietly and listened until he was nearly falling asleep with tiredness. Then he began to roll up his ball. He had decided to go home to bed. It was not long, however, before he began to feel lost. One after another he passed goblin houses. These were caves where goblin families lived. At last he was sure there were many more than he had passed as he came. He had to use great care to pass unseen. They lay so close together. Could his string have led him wrong? He still followed it, winding it as he went. And still it led him into more crowded quarters. At last he became quite uneasy, and indeed fearful. He was not afraid of the cobs. But he was afraid of not finding his way out. But what could he do? It was no use to sit down and wait for the morning. The morning made no difference here. It was dark, and always dark. And if his string failed him, he was helpless. He might even arrive within a yard of the mine and never know it. He saw he could do nothing better. So he would at least find where the end of his string was. And if possible, he would find how it had come to play him such a trick. He knew by the size of the ball that he was getting pretty near the last of it. Then he began to feel a tugging and pulling at it. What could it mean? Turning a sharp corner, he thought he heard strange sounds. As he went on, these grew to a scuffling and growling and squeaking. The noise increased. Then he turned a second sharp corner and found himself in the midst of it. At the same moment he fell over a rolling mass. He knew it must be a group of the cobs' creatures. Before he could get back on his feet, he had caught some great scratches on his face. He also had several bad bites on his legs and arms. But as he struggled to get up, his hand fell upon his pickaxe. Before the awful beasts could do him any serious harm, he was striking about with it right and left in the dark. The terrible cries that followed gave him satisfaction. He knew he had punished some of them pretty hard for their rudeness. By their running away and their retreating howls, he saw that he had driven them off. He stood for a little, weighing his battle-axe in his hand. It felt like the most precious lump of metal. But indeed no lump of gold itself could have been so precious at the time as that common tool. Then he untied the end of the string from it. He put the ball in his pocket, and still stood thinking. It was clear that the cobs' creatures had found his axe. They had carried it off between them. They had so led him he knew not where. But for all his thinking he could not tell what he ought to do. Then suddenly he became aware of a glimmer of light in the distance. Without a moment's hesitation he set out for it. He went as fast as the unknown and rough way would permit. Yet again turning a corner, led by the dim light, he spied something quite new in his experience of the underground regions. It was a small irregular shape of something shining. Going up to it, he found it was a piece of mica. It is also called Muscovy glass, and sheep-silver in Scotland. The light flickered as if from a fire behind it. He tried for some time to discover an entrance to the place where it was burning, but in vain. At last he came to a small chamber. In it, an opening high in the wall showed a glow beyond. He managed to scramble up to this opening. Then he saw a strange sight.

Below sat a little group of goblins around a fire. The smoke of it vanished in the darkness far above. The sides of the cave were full of shining minerals like those of the palace hall. The company was clearly of a higher order. Every one wore stones about head, or arms, or waist. They shone dull gorgeous colours in the light of the fire. Curdie had not looked long before he recognized the king himself. He found that he had made his way into the inner apartment of the royal family. He had never had such a good chance of hearing something. He crept through the hole as softly as he could. He scrambled a good way down the wall towards them without attracting attention. Then he sat down and listened. The king, clearly the queen, and probably the crown prince and the Prime Minister were talking together. He was sure of the queen by her shoes. As she warmed her feet at the fire, he saw them quite plainly.

'That will be fun!' said the one he took for the crown prince. It was the first whole sentence he heard.

'I don't see why you should think it such a grand affair!' said his stepmother, tossing her head backward.

'You must remember, my spouse,' interposed His Majesty, as if making excuse for his son, 'he has got the same blood in him. His mother—'

'Don't talk to me of his mother! You positively encourage his unnatural fancies. Whatever belongs to that mother ought to be cut out of him.'

'You forget yourself, my dear!' said the king.

'I don't,' said the queen, 'nor you either. If you expect me to approve of such coarse tastes, you will find yourself mistaken. I don't wear shoes for nothing.'

'You must acknowledge, however,' the king said, with a little groan, 'that this at least is no whim of Harelip's, but a matter of State policy. You are well aware that his satisfaction comes purely from the pleasure of sacrificing himself to the public good. Does it not, Harelip?'

'Yes, father; of course it does. Only it will be nice to make her cry. I'll have the skin taken off between her toes, and tie them up till they grow together. Then her feet will be like other people's, and there will be no occasion for her to wear shoes.'

'Do you mean to say I have toes, you strange and bad creature?' cried the queen. She moved toward Harelip in anger. The councillor, however, was between them. He leaned forward to stop her touching him. But he did it only as if to speak to the prince.

'Your Royal Highness,' he said, 'perhaps I should remind you that you have three toes yourself. One on one foot, two on the other.'

'Ha! ha! ha!' shouted the queen in triumph.

The councillor was encouraged by this sign of favour. He went on.

'It seems to me, Your Royal Highness, it would make your future people love you greatly. It would prove to them that you are not less one of them just because you had the bad luck to be born of a sun-mother. This would happen if you were to order upon yourself the fairly small operation. In a larger form, you so wisely plan it for your future princess.'

'Ha! ha! ha!' laughed the queen louder than before. The king and the minister joined in the laugh. Harelip gave a low angry sound. For a few moments the others continued to show their pleasure at his discomfort.

The queen was the only one Curdie could see clearly. She sat with her side to him. The light of the fire shone full upon her face. He could not think her handsome. Her nose was certainly wider at the end than its great length. Her eyes were not level. They were set up like two upright eggs, one on the wide end, the other on the small end. Her mouth was no bigger than a small buttonhole until she laughed. Then it stretched from ear to ear. Only, to be sure, her ears were very nearly in the middle of her cheeks.

Curdie wanted to hear everything they might say. So he dared to slide down a smooth part of the rock just under him. He slid to a shelf below, where he thought to rest. But perhaps he was not careful enough, or the shelf gave way. Down he came with a rush onto the floor of the cave. He brought with him a great rumbling shower of stones.

The goblins jumped from their seats in more anger than fear. They had never yet seen anything to be afraid of in the palace. But when they saw Curdie with his pick in his hand, their anger was mixed with fear. They took him for the first of an invasion of miners. The king nevertheless drew himself up to his full height of four feet. He spread himself to his full width of three and a half. For he was the most handsome and squarest of all the goblins. He walked proudly up to Curdie. He planted himself with feet spread wide before him. Then he said with dignity:

'Please tell me what right you have in my palace?'

'The right of necessity, Your Majesty,' answered Curdie. 'I lost my way and did not know where I was wandering to.'

'How did you get in?'

'By a hole in the mountain.'

'But you are a miner! Look at your pickaxe!'

Curdie did look at it. He answered:

'I came upon it lying on the ground a little way from here. I fell over some wild beasts who were playing with it. Look, Your Majesty.' And Curdie showed him how he was scratched and bitten.

The king was pleased to find him behave more politely than he had expected. He had expected less from what his people had told him about the miners. He credited it to the power of his own presence. But he did not therefore feel friendly to the intruder.

'You will oblige me by walking out of my lands at once,' he said. He well knew what a joke lay in the words.

'With pleasure, if Your Majesty will give me a guide,' said Curdie.

'I will give you a thousand,' said the king with a mocking air of great generosity.

'One will be quite sufficient,' said Curdie.

But the king gave a strange shout, half call, half roar. In rushed goblins until the cave was full. He said something to the first of them which Curdie could not hear. It was passed from one to another. In a moment the farthest in the crowd had clearly heard and understood it. They began to gather about him in a way he did not like. He moved back toward the wall. They pressed upon him.

'Stand back,' said Curdie. He held his pickaxe tighter by his knee.

They only smiled and pressed closer. Curdie thought for a moment and began to rhyme.

'Ten, twenty, thirty-- You're all so very dirty! Twenty, thirty, forty-- You're all so thick and snorty! 'Thirty, forty, fifty-- You're all so puff-and-snifty! Forty, fifty, sixty-- Beast and man so mixty!

'Fifty, sixty, seventy-- Mixty, maxty, leaventy! Sixty, seventy, eighty-- All your cheeks so slaty!

'Seventy, eighty, ninety, All your hands so flinty! Eighty, ninety, hundred, Altogether dundred!'

The goblins moved back a little when he began. They made awful faces all through the poem. It was as if they were eating something so bad that it hurt their teeth and made them feel afraid. But I cannot tell whether it was because most of the rhyming words were not real words at all—Curdie had made the poem on the moment, because a new poem was thought to work better—or whether it was because the king and queen were there and gave them courage. But the moment the poem was over, they crowded around him again. A hundred long arms shot out, with many thick fingers without nails at the ends, to catch him. Then Curdie lifted up his axe. But he was as gentle as he was brave, and he did not wish to kill any of them. So he turned the end that was square and blunt like a hammer. With that he brought down a great blow on the head of the goblin nearest him. The heads of all goblins are hard, but he thought the goblin must feel that. And so he did, no doubt. But he only gave an awful cry and jumped at Curdie's throat. Curdie, however, pulled back in time. Just at that critical moment he remembered the weak part of the goblin body. He made a sudden rush at the king and stamped with all his might on His Majesty's feet. The king gave a most unkingly cry and almost fell into the fire. Curdie then rushed into the crowd, stamping right and left. The goblins moved back, crying out on every side as he came near. But they were so crowded that few of those he attacked could escape his steps. The screaming and roaring that filled the cave would have frightened Curdie, but it gave him good hope. They were falling over each other in heaps in their eagerness to rush from the cave. Then a new attacker suddenly faced him—the queen, with burning eyes and wide nostrils, her hair standing half up from her head, rushed at him. She trusted in her shoes: they were of granite—hollowed like French sabots. Curdie would have suffered much rather than hurt a woman, even if she was a goblin. But here was a matter of life and death. Forgetting her shoes, he made a great stamp on one of her feet. But she instantly returned it with very different effect, causing him terrible pain and almost disabling him. His only chance with her would have been to attack the granite shoes with his pickaxe. But before he could think of that, she had caught him up in her arms and was rushing with him across the cave. She threw him into a hole in the wall with a force that almost stunned him. But although he could not move, he was not too far gone to hear her great cry. He heard the rush of many soft feet, followed by the sounds of something lifted up against the rock. After that came a many-footed patter of stones falling near him. The last had not stopped when he grew very faint, for his head had been badly cut, and at last he became insensible.

When he came to himself there was perfect silence about him, and complete darkness, but for the merest glimmer in one tiny spot. He crawled to it and found that they had lifted a slab against the mouth of the hole. Past the edge of it a poor little gleam found its way from the fire. He could not move it a hairbreadth, for they had piled a great heap of stones against it. He crawled back to where he had been lying, in the faint hope of finding his pickaxe. But after a vain search he was at last forced to admit that he was in an evil plight. He sat down and tried to think, but soon fell fast asleep.

CHAPTER 19. Goblin Counsels

Goblin Counsels

He must have slept a long time. When he woke up, he felt very strong again. In fact, he felt almost well, and very hungry. There were voices in the outer cave.

So it was night again. The goblins slept during the day and did their business at night.

In their home it was always dark. So they had no reason to like one time better than the other. But they hated the sun-people. So they chose to be busy when there was the least chance of meeting them. They did not want to meet the miners below when they were digging. They did not want to meet the people of the mountain above when they were feeding their sheep or catching their goats. And in fact, only when the sun was gone did the outside of the mountain look enough like their own sad dark places. Only then could their mole eyes bear it. They were so unused to any light except their own fires and torches.

Curdie listened. Soon he found that they were talking about him.

'How long will it take?' asked Harelip.

'Not many days, I should think,' answered the king. 'They are poor weak creatures, those sun-people. They want to eat all the time. We can go a week without food and be all the better for it. But I have been told they eat two or three times every day! Can you believe it? They must be quite empty inside. Not at all like us. Nine parts of ten of our body is solid flesh and bone. Yes, I think a week without food will be enough for him.'

'If I may say a word,' said the queen, 'and I think I should have some say in this—'

'The wretch is all yours, my wife,' the king interrupted. 'He is your property. You caught him yourself. We would never have done it.'

The queen laughed. She seemed in a much better mood than the night before.

'I was going to say,' she went on, 'that it does seem a shame to waste so much fresh meat.'

'What are you thinking of, my love?' said the king. 'The very idea of starving him means we are not going to give him any meat, either salted or fresh.'

'I am not that stupid,' said Her Majesty. 'What I mean is that by the time he is starved, there will hardly be anything left on his bones.'

The king gave a great laugh.

'Well, my wife, you may have him when you like,' he said. 'I do not like him for myself. I am quite sure he is tough to eat.'

'That would be to honour him instead of punish his rudeness,' said the queen. 'But why should our poor creatures lose so much food? Our little dogs and cats and pigs and small bears would enjoy him very much.'

'You are the best of housekeepers, my lovely queen!' said her husband. 'Let it be so by all means. Let us bring our people in. Let us get him out and kill him at once. He deserves it. Think of the harm he might have brought upon us. He had got so far as our most secret fort. The harm is too great to count. Or rather, let us tie his hands and feet. Then we can have the pleasure of seeing him torn to pieces by full torchlight in the great hall.'

'Better and better!' cried the queen and the prince together. Both of them clapped their hands. And the prince made an ugly noise with his hare-lip. It was just as if he meant to be one at the feast.

'But,' added the queen, thinking again, 'he is so troublesome. Poor as they are, there is something about those sun-people that is very troublesome. I cannot imagine how it is. We have greater strength and skill and understanding than they do. Yet we let them live at all. Why do we not destroy them completely? Then we could use their cattle and their grass lands as we please. Of course we do not want to live in their awful country! It is far too bright for our quieter and finer tastes. But we might use it as a kind of outer building, you know. Even our creatures' eyes might get used to it. And if they did grow blind, that would not matter, as long as they grew fat too. But we might even keep their great cows and other creatures. Then we should have a few more fine things, like cream and cheese. At present we only taste them sometimes, when our brave men have managed to carry some off from their farms.'

'It is worth thinking about,' said the king. 'And I do not know why you should be the first to suggest it. Except that you have a true gift for conquest. But still, as you say, there is something very troublesome about them. And it would be better, as I understand you to suggest, that we should starve him for a day or two first. Then he may be a little less lively when we take him out.'

'Once there was a goblin Living in a hole; Busy he was cobblin' A shoe without a sole.

'By came a birdie: "Goblin, what do you do?" "Cobble at a sturdie Upper leather shoe."

'"What's the good o' that, Sir?" Said the little bird. "Why it's very Pat, Sir-- Plain without a word.

'"Where 'tis all a hole, Sir, Never can be holes: Why should their shoes have soles, Sir, When they've got no souls?"'

'What is that awful noise?' cried the queen. She shook from her pot-metal head to her granite shoes.

'I say,' said the king with solemn anger, 'it is the sun-creature in the hole!'

'Stop that disgusting noise!' cried the crown prince bravely. He got up and stood in front of the heap of stones, with his face towards Curdie's prison. 'Do it now, or I will break your head.'

'Break away,' shouted Curdie, and began singing again:

'Once there was a goblin, Living in a hole--'

'I really cannot bear it,' said the queen. 'If I could only get at his awful toes with my slippers again!'

'I think we had better go to bed,' said the king.

'It is not time to go to bed,' said the queen.

'I would if I was you,' said Curdie.

'Rude wretch!' said the queen, with the greatest scorn in her voice.

'An impossible if,' said His Majesty with dignity.

'Quite,' said Curdie, and began singing again:

'Go to bed, Goblin, do. Help the queen Take off her shoe.

'If you do, It will disclose A horrid set Of sprouting toes.'

'What a lie!' roared the queen in a rage.

'By the way, that reminds me,' said the king, 'that as long as we have been married, I have never seen your feet, queen. I think you might take off your shoes when you go to bed! They really hurt me sometimes.'

'I will do as I like,' said the queen in a bad mood.

'You ought to do as your own hubby wishes,' said the king.

'I will not,' said the queen.

'Then I insist,' said the king.

His Majesty went to the queen to follow Curdie's advice. Curdie heard a short fight, and then a loud cry from the king.

'Will you be quiet, then?' said the queen in a mean way.

'Yes, yes, queen. I only meant to persuade you.'

'Hands off!' cried the queen with joy. 'I'm going to bed. You may come when you like. But as long as I am queen I will sleep in my shoes. It is my royal right. Harelip, go to bed.'

'I'm going,' said Harelip in a sleepy voice.

'So am I,' said the king.

'Come along, then,' said the queen; 'and be good, or I'll--'

'Oh, no, no, no!' screamed the king in the most begging of voices.

Curdie heard only a low reply far away. Then the cave was quite still.

They had left the fire burning, and the light came through brighter than before. Curdie thought it was time to try again if anything could be done. But he found he could not get even a finger through the narrow opening between the flat stone and the rock. He pushed hard with his shoulder against the flat stone. But it moved no more than if it had been part of the rock. All he could do was to sit down and think again.

After a while he decided to pretend to be dying. He hoped they might take him out before his strength was too far gone to give him a chance. Then, as for the creatures, if he could only find his axe again, he would have no fear of them. And if it were not for the queen's awful shoes, he would have no fear at all.

Meanwhile, until they should come again at night, there was nothing for him to do but make new rhymes. They were now his only weapons. He had no plan to use them at present, of course. But it was good to have a store, for he might live to need them. And making them would help to pass the time.

CHAPTER 20. Irene's Clue

Irene's Clue

That same morning, early, the princess woke in a terrible fear. There was a very ugly noise in her room. Creatures were making angry sounds and rushing about as if they were fighting. The moment she came to herself, she remembered something she had never thought of again. It was what her grandmother told her to do when she was afraid. She immediately took off her ring and put it under her pillow. As she did so, she felt a finger and thumb take it gently from under her palm. 'It must be my grandmother!' she said to herself. The thought gave her such courage that she stopped to put on her small pretty shoes before running from the room. While doing this, she caught sight of a long coat of sky-blue. It was thrown over the back of a chair by the bed. She had never seen it before, but it was clearly waiting for her. She put it on. Then she felt with the index finger of her right hand and soon found her grandmother's thread. She began to follow it at once. She expected it would lead her straight up the old steps. When she reached the door, she found it went down and ran along the floor. She had almost to crawl to keep hold of it. Then, to her surprise and somewhat to her worry, she found it did not lead her towards the steps. It turned in quite the opposite direction. It led her through certain narrow passages towards the kitchen. It turned aside before she reached the kitchen and guided her to a door that opened to a small back yard. Some of the servant women were already up, and this door was standing open. Across the yard the thread still ran along the ground. It brought her to a door in the wall that opened upon the mountainside. When she had passed through, the thread rose to about half her height. She could hold it with ease as she walked. It led her straight up the mountain.

The cause of her alarm was less terrible than she supposed. The cook's great black cat was chased by the housekeeper's small dog. It had bounced against her bedroom door, which had not been properly closed. The two had burst into the room together and begun a great battle. How the nurse came to sleep through it was a mystery. But I suspect the old lady had something to do with it.

It was a clear warm morning. The wind blew pleasantly over the Mountainside. Here and there she saw a late primrose, but she did not stop to call upon them. The sky was marked with small clouds.

The sun was not yet up, but some of their soft edges had caught his light. They hung out orange and gold-coloured edges upon the air. The dew lay in round drops upon the leaves. It hung like tiny diamond ear-rings from the blades of grass about her path.

'How lovely that bit of thin shining web is!' thought the princess. She was looking at a long waving line that shone at some distance from her up the hill. It was not the time for such webs though. Irene soon discovered that it was her own thread she saw shining on before her in the light of the morning. It was leading her she knew not where. But she had never in her life been out before sunrise. Everything was so fresh and cool and full of life and full of something coming. She felt too happy to be afraid of anything.

After leading her up a good distance, the thread turned to the left. It went down the path upon which she and Lootie had met Curdie. But she never thought of that. Now, in the morning light, with its far view over the country, no path could have been more open and bright and cheerful. She could see the road almost to the horizon. Along that road she had so often watched her king-papa and his troop come shining. The bugle-blast cut the air before them. It was like a companion to her. Down and down the path went, then up, and then down and then up again. It got rough and more rough as it went. Still along the path went the silvery thread. Still along the thread went Irene's little rosy-tipped index finger. By and by she came to a little stream that talked and ran down the hill. Up the side of the stream went both path and thread. And still the path grew rougher and steeper. The mountain grew wilder, till Irene began to think she was going a very long way from home. When she turned to look back, she saw that the level country had vanished. The rough bare mountain had closed in about her. But still on went the thread, and on went the princess. Everything around her was getting brighter and brighter as the sun came nearer. At length his first rays all at once landed on the top of a rock before her, like some golden creature fresh from the sky. Then she saw that the little stream ran out of a hole in that rock. She saw that the path did not go past the rock. She saw that the thread was leading her straight up to it. A shake ran through her from head to foot when she found that the thread was actually taking her into the hole out of which the stream ran. It ran out talking joyously, but she had to go in.

She did not wait. She went right into the hole. The hole was high enough for her to walk without bending down. For a short way there was a soft brown light. But at the first turn the light almost stopped. Before she had gone many steps she was in complete darkness. Then she became truly afraid. Every moment she kept feeling the thread back and forth. As she went farther and farther into the darkness of the great empty mountain, she thought more and more about her grandmother. She thought about all that her grandmother had said to her. She thought about how kind she had been, and how beautiful she was. She thought about her lovely room, and the fire of roses, and the great lamp that sent its light through stone walls. And she became more and more sure that the thread could not have gone there by itself. Her grandmother must have sent it. But it was very hard for her when the path went down very steep. It was especially hard when she came to places where she had to go down rough stairs, and sometimes even a ladder. The thread guided her through one narrow passage after another, over lumps of rock and sand and clay. At last she came to a small hole. She had to go through it on her hands and knees. She found no change on the other side. "Shall I ever get back?" she thought, again and again. She wondered at herself that she was not ten times more afraid. She often felt as if she were only walking in the story of a dream. Sometimes she heard the noise of water, a low sound inside the rock. By and by she heard the sounds of blows. They came nearer and nearer. But again they grew softer, and almost died away. She turned in a hundred directions, following the guiding thread.

At last she saw a soft red shine. She came up to the mica window. Then she went away and around, and right, into a cave. There the red coals of a fire were burning. Here the thread began to rise. It rose as high as her head and higher still. What should she do if she lost her hold? She was pulling it down. She might break it! She could see it far up, shining as red as her fire-opal in the light of the coals.

But then she came to a huge pile of stones. The stones were piled in a slope against the wall of the cave. She climbed on them. Soon she found the level of the thread again. But the next moment she saw that it went through the pile of stones. It left her standing on the pile, with her face to the solid rock. For one terrible moment she felt as if her grandmother had left her. The thread which the spiders had spun far over the seas, which her grandmother had sat in the moonlight and spun again for her, which she had tempered in the rose-fire and tied to her opal ring, had left her. It had gone where she could no longer follow it. It had brought her into a horrible cave, and there left her! She was truly left alone!

"When shall I wake?" she said to herself in great pain. But the same moment she knew that it was no dream. She threw herself upon the pile, and began to cry. It was well she did not know what creatures were lying in the next cave. One of them had stone shoes on her feet. But she also did not know who was on the other side of the slab.

At last the thought struck her that at least she could follow the thread back. In that way she could get out of the mountain, and home. She rose at once, and found the thread. But the instant she tried to feel it back, it vanished from her touch. Forward, it led her hand up to the pile of stones. Back, it seemed to go nowhere. She could not see it as before in the light of the fire. She burst into a crying sound, and again threw herself down on the stones.

CHAPTER 21. The Escape

The Escape

As the princess lay and cried, she kept feeling the thread without thinking. She followed it with her finger many times up to the stones where it disappeared. After a while she began, still without thinking, to push her finger in after it between the stones as far as she could. All at once it came into her head that she might remove some of the stones and see where the thread went next. She almost laughed at herself for never having thought of this before, and jumped to her feet. Her fear went away. Once more she was sure her grandmother's thread could not have brought her there just to leave her there. She began to throw away the stones from the top as fast as she could. Sometimes she took two or three at a handful. Sometimes she used both hands to lift one. After clearing them away a little, she found that the thread turned and went straight down. The pile of stones sloped a good deal and grew wider towards its base. So she had to throw away a great many stones to follow the thread. But this was not all. She soon found that the thread went straight down for a little way, then turned first one way, then another way, and then went at different angles here and there inside the pile. She began to be afraid that to clear the thread she must remove the whole huge pile. She was upset at the very idea. But she lost no time and set to work with energy. Her back hurt and her fingers and hands bled. But she worked on, kept going by the pleasure of seeing the pile slowly get smaller and begin to show itself on the opposite side of the fire. Another thing that helped keep up her courage was this: as often as she uncovered a turn of the thread, it did not lie loose upon the stone but got tight. This made her sure that her grandmother was at the end of it somewhere.

She had got about half-way down when she started and nearly fell with fear. Close to her ears, it seemed, a voice broke out singing:

'Jabber, bother, smash! You'll have it all in a crash. Jabber, smash, bother! You'll have the worst of the pother. Smash, bother, jabber!--'

Here Curdie stopped. Maybe he could not find a rhyme to 'jabber'. Or maybe he remembered what he had forgotten when he woke up at the sound of Irene's work. His plan was to make the goblins think he was getting weak. But he had said enough to let Irene know who he was.

'It is Curdie!' she cried with joy.

'Hush! hush!' came Curdie's voice again from somewhere. 'Speak softly.'

'Why, you were singing loud!' said Irene.

'Yes. But they know I am here, and they do not know you are. Who are you?'

'I am Irene,' answered the princess. 'I know who you are quite well. You are Curdie.'

'Why, how ever did you come here, Irene?'

'My great-great-grandmother sent me. And I think I have found out why. You cannot get out, I suppose?'

'No, I cannot. What are you doing?'

'Clearing away a huge pile of stones.'

'There is a princess!' cried Curdie, in a tone of great pleasure, but still speaking in little more than a whisper. 'I cannot think how you got here, though.'

'My grandmother sent me after her thread.'

'I do not know what you mean,' said Curdie. 'But since you are there, it does not much matter.'

'Oh, yes, it does!' answered Irene. 'I should never have been here but for her.'

'You can tell me all about it when we get out, then. There is no time to lose now,' said Curdie.

And Irene went to work, as fresh as when she began.

'There are such a lot of stones!' she said. 'It will take me a long time to get them all away.'

'How far on have you got?' asked Curdie.

'I have got about the half away, but the other half is much bigger.'

'I do not think you will have to move the lower half. Do you see a flat stone laid up against the wall?'

Irene looked, and felt about with her hands, and soon saw the outline of the flat stone.

'Yes,' she answered, 'I do.'

'Then, I think,' answered Curdie, 'when you have cleared the flat stone about half-way down, or a bit more, I shall be able to push it over.'

'I must follow my thread,' answered Irene, 'whatever I do.'

'What do you mean?' cried Curdie.

'You will see when you get out,' answered the princess, and went on harder than ever.

But she was soon sure that what Curdie wanted done and what the thread wanted done were one and the same thing. She saw that by following the turns of the thread she had been clearing the face of the flat stone. She also saw that, a little more than half-way down, the thread went through the narrow opening between the flat stone and the wall into the place where Curdie was kept. So she could not follow it any farther until the flat stone was out of her way. As soon as she found this, she said in a very happy whisper:

'Now, Curdie, I think if you were to give a great push, the flat stone would fall over.'

'Stand quite clear of it, then,' said Curdie, 'and let me know when you are ready.'

Irene got off the pile and stood on one side of it. 'Now, Curdie!' she cried.

Curdie gave a great rush with his shoulder against it. Out fell the flat stone on the pile, and out crept Curdie over the top of it.

'You have saved my life, Irene!' he whispered.

'Oh, Curdie! I am so glad! Let us get out of this horrible place as fast as we can.'

'That is easier said than done,' answered he.

'Oh, no, it is quite easy,' said Irene. 'We have only to follow my thread. I am sure that it is going to take us out now.'

She had already begun to follow it over the fallen flat stone into the hole. Curdie was searching the floor of the cave for his pickaxe.

'Here it is!' he cried. 'No, it is not,' he added, in a disappointed tone. 'What can it be, then? I declare it is a torch. That is great! It is better almost than my pickaxe. Much better if it were not for those stone shoes!' he went on, as he lit the torch by blowing the last burning pieces of the dying fire.

When he looked up, with the lit torch throwing a bright light into the great darkness of the huge cave, he caught sight of Irene disappearing in the hole out of which he had himself just come.

'Where are you going there?' he cried. 'That is not the way out. That is where I could not get out.'

'I know that,' whispered Irene. 'But this is the way my thread goes, and I must follow it.'

'What nonsense the child talks!' said Curdie to himself. 'I must follow her, though, and see that she comes to no harm. She will soon find she can't get out that way, and then she will come with me.'

So he crept over the slab once more into the hole with his torch in his hand. But when he looked about in it, he could see her nowhere. And now he found that although the hole was narrow, it was much longer than he had thought. For in one direction the roof came down very low, and the hole went off in a narrow passage. He could not see the end of it. The princess must have crept in there. He got on his knees and one hand, holding the torch with the other, and crept after her. The hole turned this way and that. In some parts it was so low that he could hardly get through. In others it was so high that he could not see the roof. But everywhere it was narrow—far too narrow for a goblin to get through. So I presume they never thought that Curdie might. He was beginning to feel very uncomfortable lest something should have happened to the princess. Then he heard her voice almost close to his ear, whispering:

'Aren't you coming, Curdie?'

And when he turned the next corner there she stood waiting for him.

'I knew you couldn't go wrong in that narrow hole. But now you must keep by me, for here is a great wide place,' she said.

'I can't understand it,' said Curdie, half to himself, half to Irene.

'Never mind,' she returned. 'Wait till we get out.'

Curdie was utterly astonished that she had already got so far, and by a path he had known nothing of. He thought it better to let her do as she pleased. 'At all events,' he said again to himself, 'I know nothing about the way, miner as I am. And she seems to think she does know something about it, though how she should passes my comprehension. So she's just as likely to find her way as I am. And as she insists on taking the lead, I must follow. We can't be much worse off than we are, anyhow.' Reasoning thus, he followed her a few steps. Then he came out in another great cavern. Across it Irene walked in a straight line, as confidently as if she knew every step of the way. Curdie went on after her, flashing his torch about, and trying to see something of what lay around them. Suddenly he started back a pace as the light fell upon something close by which Irene was passing. It was a platform of rock raised a few feet from the floor and covered with sheepskins. On it lay two horrible figures asleep. Curdie recognized them at once as the king and queen of the goblins. He lowered his torch instantly lest the light should awake them. As he did so it flashed upon his pickaxe, lying by the side of the queen. Her hand lay close by the handle of it.

'Stop one moment,' he whispered. 'Hold my torch, and don't let the light on their faces.'

Irene shuddered when she saw the frightful creatures. She had passed them without observing them. But she did as he requested. She turned her back and held the torch low in front of her. Curdie drew his pickaxe carefully away. As he did so he spied one of her feet, projecting from under the skins. The great clumsy granite shoe was exposed to his hand. It was a temptation not to be resisted. He laid hold of it, and, with cautious efforts, drew it off. The moment he succeeded, he saw to his astonishment that what he had sung in ignorance, to annoy the queen, was actually true: she had six horrible toes. He was overjoyed at his success. He saw by the huge bump in the sheepskins where the other foot was. He proceeded to lift them gently. If he could only succeed in carrying away the other shoe as well, he would be no more afraid of the goblins than of so many flies. But as he pulled at the second shoe the queen gave a growl and sat up in bed. The same instant the king awoke also and sat up beside her.

'Run, Irene!' cried Curdie. He was not now in the least afraid for himself, but he was for the princess.

Irene looked once round and saw the fearful creatures awake. Like the wise princess she was, she dashed the torch on the ground and extinguished it, crying out:

'Here, Curdie, take my hand.'

He darted to her side, forgetting neither the queen's shoe nor his pickaxe. He caught hold of her hand, as she sped fearlessly where her thread guided her. They heard the queen give a great bellow. But they had a good start, for it would be some time before they could get torches lighted to pursue them. Just as they thought they saw a gleam behind them, the thread brought them to a very narrow opening. Through it Irene crept easily, and Curdie with difficulty.

'Now,' said Curdie; 'I think we shall be safe.'

'Of course we shall,' returned Irene.

'Why do you think so?' asked Curdie.

'Because my grandmother is taking care of us.'

'That's all nonsense,' said Curdie. 'I don't know what you mean.'

'Then if you don't know what I mean, what right have you to call it nonsense?' asked the princess, a little offended.

'I beg your pardon, Irene,' said Curdie; 'I did not mean to vex you.'

'Of course not,' returned the princess. 'But why do you think we shall be safe?'

'Because the king and queen are far too stout to get through that hole.'

'There might be ways round,' said the princess.

'To be sure there might: we are not out of it yet,' acknowledged Curdie.

'But what do you mean by the king and queen?' asked the princess. 'I should never call such creatures as those a king and a queen.'

'Their own people do, though,' answered Curdie.

The princess asked more questions. Curdie walked slowly beside her and gave her a full account. He told her about the character and habits of the goblins, as far as he knew them. He also told her about his own adventures with them. He began from the very night after the one in which he had met her and Lootie on the mountain. When he had finished, he asked Irene to tell him how she had come to save him. So Irene too had to tell a long story. She told it in a rather indirect way. Many questions about things she had not explained broke into it. But he did not believe more than half of her tale. So everything stayed as strange to him as before. He was almost as confused about what he must think of the princess. He could not believe that she was telling lies on purpose. The only conclusion he could reach was this: Lootie had been playing tricks on the child. She had invented many lies to make her afraid, for her own purposes.

'But how did Lootie ever let you go into the mountains alone?' he asked.

'Lootie knows nothing about it. I left her fast asleep--at least I think so. I hope my grandmother will not let her get into trouble. It was not her fault at all, as my grandmother very well knows.'

'But how did you find your way to me?' Curdie asked again.

'I told you already,' answered Irene. 'By keeping my finger on my grandmother's thread, as I am doing now.'

'You do not mean you have the thread there?'

'Of course I do. I have told you so ten times already. I have hardly taken my finger off it--except when I was moving the stones. There!' she added. She guided Curdie's hand to the thread. 'You feel it yourself--do you not?'

'I feel nothing at all,' replied Curdie.

'Then what can be the matter with your finger? I feel it perfectly. To be sure it is very thin. In the sunlight it looks just like the thread of a spider. Many of them are twisted together to make it. But still I cannot think why you should not feel it as well as I do.'

Curdie was too polite to say he did not believe there was any thread there at all. What he did say was:

'Well, I can make nothing of it.'

'I can, though. And you must be glad of that, for it will do for both of us.'

'We are not out yet,' said Curdie.

'We soon shall be,' returned Irene confidently. And now the thread went downwards. It led Irene's hand to a hole in the floor of the cavern. From the hole came a sound of running water. They had been hearing it for some time.

'It goes into the ground now, Curdie,' she said, and stopped.

He had been listening to another sound. His trained ear had caught it long ago, and it also had been growing louder. It was the noise the goblin miners made at their work. They seemed to be at no great distance now. Irene heard it the moment she stopped.

'What is that noise?' she asked. 'Do you know, Curdie?'

'Yes. It is the goblins digging and burrowing,' he answered.

'And you do not know what they do it for?'

'No; I have not the least idea. Would you like to see them?' he asked. He wished to have another try at their secret.

'If my thread took me there, I should not much mind. But I do not want to see them, and I cannot leave my thread. It leads me down into the hole, and we had better go at once.'

'Very well. Shall I go in first?' said Curdie.

'No; better not. You cannot feel the thread,' she answered. She stepped down through a narrow break in the floor of the cavern. 'Oh!' she cried. 'I am in the water. It is running strong--but it is not deep, and there is just room to walk. Make haste, Curdie.'

He tried, but the hole was too small for him to get in.

'Go on a little bit,' he said, and put his pickaxe on his shoulder. In a few moments he had cleared a larger opening and followed her. They went on, down and down with the running water. Curdie became more and more afraid. He feared it was leading them to some terrible gulf in the heart of the mountain. In one or two places he had to break away the rock to make room. Even Irene could not get through without hurting herself. But at length they saw a glimmer of light. In a minute more they were almost blinded by the full sunlight. They came out into it. It was some little time before the princess could see well enough to discover that they stood in her own garden. They were close by the seat on which she and her king-papa had sat that afternoon. They had come out by the channel of the little stream. She danced and clapped her hands with delight.

'Now, Curdie!' she cried. 'Will you not believe what I told you about my grandmother and her thread?'

For she had felt all the time that Curdie was not believing what she told him.

'There! Do you not see it shining on before us?' she added.

'I do not see anything,' persisted Curdie.

'Then you must believe without seeing,' said the princess. 'For you cannot deny it has brought us out of the mountain.'

'I cannot deny we are out of the mountain. And I should be very ungrateful indeed to deny that you had brought me out of it.'

'I could not have done it but for the thread,' persisted Irene.

'That is the part I do not understand.'

'Well, come along, and Lootie will get you something to eat. I am sure you must want it very much.'

'Indeed I do. But my father and mother will be so anxious about me. I must make haste--first up the mountain to tell my mother, and then down into the mine again to let my father know.'

'Very well, Curdie. But you cannot get out without coming this way. And I will take you through the house, for that is nearest.'

They met no one by the way. Indeed, as before, the people were here and there and everywhere searching for the princess. When they got in, Irene found that the thread went up the old staircase, as she had half expected. A new thought struck her. She turned to Curdie and said:

'My grandmother wants me. Do come up with me and see her. Then you will know that I have been telling you the truth. Do come--to please me, Curdie. I cannot bear you should think what I say is not true.'

'I never doubted you believed what you said,' returned Curdie. 'I only thought you had some fancy in your head that was not correct.'

'But do come, dear Curdie.'

The little miner could not withstand this appeal. He felt shy in what seemed to him a huge grand house. But he yielded, and followed her up the stair.

CHAPTER 22. The Old Lady and Curdie

Up the stairs they went, and the next and the next. They went through the long rows of empty rooms, and up the little tower stairs. Irene grew happier and happier as she went up. She knocked at last at the door of the workroom. There was no answer. She could not hear any sound of the spinning-wheel. Once more her heart sank within her. But it was only for one moment. She turned and knocked at the other door.

'Come in,' answered the sweet voice of her grandmother. Irene opened the door and entered. Curdie followed her.

'You dear!' cried the lady. She was sitting by a fire of red roses mixed with white. 'I've been waiting for you. I was getting a little worried about you. I was beginning to think whether I had not better go and get you myself.'

As she spoke she took the little princess in her arms and placed her upon her lap. She was dressed in white now. She looked if possible more lovely than ever.

'I've brought Curdie, grandmother. He would not believe what I told him. So I've brought him.'

'Yes--I see him. He is a good boy, Curdie, and a bold boy. Are you not glad you've got him out?'

'Yes, grandmother. But it was not very good of him not to believe me when I was telling him the truth.'

'People must believe what they can. Those who believe more must not be hard upon those who believe less. I doubt if you would have believed it all yourself if you had not seen some of it.'

'Ah! yes, grandmother, I dare say. I'm sure you are right. But he'll believe now.'

'I do not know that,' replied her grandmother.

'Will you not, Curdie?' said Irene. She looked round at him as she asked the question. He was standing in the middle of the floor. He was staring and looking strangely lost. She thought this came from his surprise at the beauty of the lady.

'Make a bow to my grandmother, Curdie,' she said.

'I do not see any grandmother,' answered Curdie rather roughly.

'Do not see my grandmother, when I'm sitting in her lap?' cried the princess.

'No, I do not,' said Curdie again, in an angry tone.

'Do you not see the lovely fire of roses--white ones amongst them this time?' asked Irene. She was almost as lost as he was.

'No, I do not,' answered Curdie, almost in a bad temper.

'Nor the blue bed? Nor the rose-coloured cover? Nor the beautiful light, like the moon, hanging from the roof?'

'You're making fun of me, Your Royal Highness. After what we have come through together this day, I do not think it is kind of you,' said Curdie. He felt very much hurt.

'Then what do you see?' asked Irene. She saw at once that for her not to believe him was at least as bad as for him not to believe her.

'I see a big, bare, empty room at the top of the house. It is like the one in my mother's small house. But it is big enough to take the house itself in, and leave a good space all round,' answered Curdie.

'And what more do you see?'

'I see a large tub, and a pile of old straw, and a dried apple. I see a ray of sunlight coming through a hole in the middle of the roof. It is shining on your head and making all the place look a curious dark brown. I think you had better drop it, princess, and go down to the children's room, like a good girl.'

'But do you not hear my grandmother talking to me?' asked Irene. She was almost crying.

'No. I hear the soft sound of a lot of pigeons. If you will not come down, I will go without you. I think that will be better anyhow. I'm sure nobody who met us would believe a word we said to them. They would think we made it all up. I do not expect anybody but my own father and mother to believe me. They know I would not tell a lie.'

'And yet you will not believe me, Curdie?' The princess was now really crying. She was full of upset and sorrow at the great distance between her and Curdie.

'No. I cannot, and I cannot help it,' said Curdie. He turned to leave the room.

'What SHALL I do, grandmother?' cried the princess with tears. She turned her face round upon the lady's chest and shook with held-back crying.

'You must give him time,' said her grandmother. 'And you must be content not to be believed for a while. It is very hard to bear. But I have had to bear it, and shall have to bear it many a time yet. I will take care of what Curdie thinks of you in the end. You must let him go now.'

'You're not coming, are you?' asked Curdie.

'No, Curdie. My grandmother says I must let you go. Turn to the right when you get to the bottom of all the stairs. That will take you to the hall where the great door is.'

'Oh! I do not doubt I can find my way--without you, princess, or your old grannie's thread either,' said Curdie quite rudely.

'Oh, Curdie! Curdie!'

'I wish I had gone home at once. I'm very much thankful to you, Irene, for getting me out of that hole. But I wish you had not made a fool of me afterwards.'

He said this as he opened the door. He left it open, and without another word went down the stairs. Irene listened with fear to his leaving footsteps. Then she turned again to the lady:

'What does it all mean, grandmother?' she cried, and burst into fresh tears.

'It means, my love, that I did not mean to show myself. Curdie is not yet able to believe some things. Seeing is not believing--it is only seeing. You remember I told you that if Lootie were to see me, she would rub her eyes. She would forget the half she saw, and call the other half nonsense.'

'Yes; but I should have thought Curdie--'

'You are right. Curdie is much farther on than Lootie, and you will see what will come of it. But in the meantime you must be content, I say, to be misunderstood for a while. We are all very eager to be understood, and it is very hard not to be. But there is one thing much more necessary.'

'What is that, grandmother?'

'To understand other people.'

'Yes, grandmother. I must be fair. If I'm not fair to other people, I'm not worth being understood myself. I see. So as Curdie cannot help it, I will not be angry with him, but just wait.'

'There's my own dear child,' said her grandmother, and pressed her close to her chest.

'Why were you not in your workroom when we came up, grandmother?' asked Irene, after a few moments' silence.

'If I had been there, Curdie would have seen me well enough. But why should I be there rather than in this beautiful room?'

'I thought you would be spinning.'

'I've nobody to spin for just at present. I never spin without knowing for whom I am spinning.'

'That reminds me—there is one thing that puzzles me,' said the princess. 'How are you going to get the thread out of the mountain again? Surely you will not have to make another for me? That would be such a problem!'

The lady set her down and stood up and went to the fire. She put in her hand and drew it out again. She held up the shining ball between her finger and thumb.

'I have it now, you see,' she said, coming back to the princess. 'All ready for you when you want it.'

She went to her cabinet and laid it in the same drawer as before.

'And here is your ring,' she added. She took it from the little finger of her left hand and put it on the first finger of Irene's right hand.

'Oh, thank you, grandmother! I feel so safe now!'

'You are very tired, my child,' the lady went on. 'Your hands are hurt with the stones, and I have counted nine dark marks on you. Just look at yourself.'

And she held up to her a little mirror which she had brought from the cabinet. The princess burst into a happy laugh at the sight. She was so wet and dirty from the stream and from moving through narrow places. If she had seen the reflection without knowing it was a reflection, she would have taken herself for some gipsy child whose face was washed and hair combed about once in a month. The lady laughed too. She lifted her again onto her knee and took off her cloak and night-gown. Then she carried her to the side of the room. Irene wondered what she was going to do with her, but asked no questions. She only started a little when she found that she was going to lay her in the large silver bath. For as she looked into it, again she saw no bottom. But the stars were shining miles away, as it seemed, in a great blue gulf. Her hands closed by themselves on the beautiful arms that held her, and that was all.

The lady pressed her once more to her chest, saying:

'Do not be afraid, my child.'

'No, grandmother,' answered the princess, with a little quick breath. And the next instant she sank in the clear cool water.

When she opened her eyes, she saw nothing but a strange lovely blue over and beneath and all about her. The lady and the beautiful room had vanished from her sight, and she seemed completely alone. But instead of being afraid, she felt more than happy—perfectly full of joy. And from somewhere came the voice of the lady, singing a strange sweet song. She could hear every word, but of the meaning she had only a feeling—no understanding. Nor could she remember a single line after it was gone. It vanished, like the poetry in a dream, as fast as it came. In later years, however, she would sometimes think that small pieces of melody suddenly rising in her brain must be little phrases and parts of the air of that song. And the very thought would make her happier, and better able to do her duty.

How long she lay in the water she did not know. It seemed a long time—not from tiredness but from pleasure. But at last she felt the beautiful hands take hold of her, and through the rushing water she was lifted out into the lovely room. The lady carried her to the fire, and sat down with her in her lap, and dried her gently with the softest towel. It was so different from Lootie's drying. When the lady had done, she bent to the fire, and drew from it her night-gown, as white as snow.

'How wonderful!' cried the princess. 'It smells of all the roses in the world, I think.'

When she stood up on the floor she felt as if she had been made over again. Every dark mark and all tiredness were gone, and her hands were soft and whole as ever.

'Now I am going to put you to bed for a good sleep,' said her grandmother.

'But what will Lootie be thinking? And what am I to say to her when she asks me where I have been?'

'Do not trouble yourself about it. You will find it all come right,' said her grandmother, and laid her into the blue bed, under the pink cover.

'There is just one thing more,' said Irene. 'I am a little anxious about Curdie. As I brought him into the house, I ought to have seen him safe on his way home.'

'I took care of all that,' answered the lady. 'I told you to let him go, and therefore I was bound to look after him. Nobody saw him, and he is now eating a good dinner in his mother's cottage far up in the mountain.'

'Then I will go to sleep,' said Irene, and in a few minutes she was fast asleep.

CHAPTER 23. Curdie and His Mother

Curdie and His Mother

Curdie went up the mountain neither whistling nor singing. He was angry with Irene for tricking him, as he called it. He was also angry with himself for having spoken to her so angrily. His mother gave a cry of joy when she saw him. She at once began to get him something to eat. She asked him questions all the time. He did not answer so happily as usual. When his meal was ready, she left him to eat it. She hurried to the mine to let his father know he was safe. When she came back, she found him fast asleep on her bed. He did not wake until his father came home in the evening.

'Now, Curdie,' his mother said, as they sat at the evening meal, 'tell us the whole story from beginning to end, just as it all happened.'

Curdie did as she asked. He told everything up to the point where they came out on the lawn in the garden of the king's house.

'And what happened after that?' asked his mother. 'You have not told us all. You should be very happy to have got away from those evil creatures. Instead, I never saw you so sad. There must be something more. Besides, you do not speak of that lovely child as I would like to hear you. She saved your life at the risk of her own. Yet somehow you do not seem to think much of it.'

'She talked such foolish things,' answered Curdie. 'She told me a lot of things that were not a bit true. And I cannot get over it.'

'What were they?' asked his father. 'Your mother may be able to throw some light on them.'

Then Curdie told them everything honestly, and told them all of it.

They all sat silent for some time, thinking about the strange tale. At last Curdie's mother spoke.

'You admit, my boy,' she said, 'there is something about the whole affair you do not understand?'

'Yes, of course, mother,' he answered. 'I cannot understand how a child who knew nothing about the mountain, or even that I was shut up in it, could come all that way alone, straight to where I was. And then, after getting me out of the hole, she led me out of the mountain too. I would not have known a step of the way if it had been as light as in the open air.'

'Then you have no right to say what she told you was not true. She did take you out, and she must have had something to guide her. Why not a thread as well as a rope, or anything else? There is something you cannot explain, and her explanation may be the right one.'

'It is no explanation at all, mother. And I cannot believe it.'

'That may be only because you do not understand it. If you did, you would probably find it was an explanation, and believe it fully. I do not blame you for not being able to believe it. But I do blame you for thinking such a child would try to deceive you. Why should she? Be sure she told you all she knew. Until you had found a better way of explaining it all, you might at least have been more careful in your judgement.'

'That is what something inside me has been saying all the time,' said Curdie, hanging down his head. 'But what do you make of the grandmother? That is what I cannot get over. To take me up to an old attic, and try to persuade me against the sight of my own eyes that it was a beautiful room, with blue walls and silver stars, and no end of things in it, when there was nothing there but an old tub and a dried apple and a pile of straw and a sunbeam! It was too bad! She might have had some old woman there at least to pass for her precious grandmother!'

'Did she not speak as if she saw those other things herself, Curdie?'

'Yes. That is what bothers me. You would have thought she really meant and believed that she saw every one of the things she talked about. And not one of them there! It was too bad, I say.'

'Perhaps some people can see things other people cannot see, Curdie,' said his mother very seriously. 'I think I will tell you something I saw myself once--only perhaps you will not believe me either!'

'Oh, mother, mother!' cried Curdie, bursting into tears. 'I do not deserve that, surely!'

'But what I am going to tell you is very strange,' his mother went on. 'And if after hearing it you were to say I must have been dreaming, I do not know that I should have any right to be angry with you. Though I know at least that I was not asleep.'

'Do tell me, mother. Perhaps it will help me to think better of the princess.'

'That is why I am tempted to tell you,' replied his mother. 'But first, I may as well mention that, according to old whispers, there is something more than common about the king's family. And the queen was of the same blood, for they were cousins of some degree. There were strange stories told concerning them--all good stories--but strange, very strange. What they were I cannot tell, for I only remember the faces of my grandmother and my mother as they talked together about them. There was wonder and awe--not fear--in their eyes. And they whispered, and never spoke aloud. But what I saw myself was this: Your father was going to work in the mine one night, and I had been down with his evening meal. It was soon after we were married, and not very long before you were born. He came with me to the mouth of the mine, and left me to go home alone, for I knew the way almost as well as the floor of our own small house. It was pretty dark, and in some parts of the road where the rocks hung over it was nearly quite dark. But I got along perfectly well, never thinking of being afraid, until I reached a spot you know well enough, Curdie, where the path has to make a sharp turn out of the way of a great rock on the left-hand side. When I got there, I was suddenly surrounded by about half a dozen of the cobs, the first I had ever seen, although I had heard tell of them often enough. One of them blocked up the path, and they all began tormenting and teasing me in a way it makes me shudder to think of even now.'

'If I had only been with you!' cried father and son in a breath.

The mother gave a funny little smile, and went on.

'They had some of their horrible creatures with them too. I must say I was very frightened. They had torn my clothes very badly. I was afraid they were going to tear me to pieces. Then suddenly a great white soft light shone on me. I looked up. A wide ray, like a shining road, came down from a large ball of silver light. It was not very high up. It was not quite as high as the horizon. So it could not have been a new star or another moon or anything like that. The cobs stopped hurting me. They looked confused. I thought they were going to run away. But soon they began again. At the same moment, a bird came down the path from the ball of light. It shone like silver in the sun. First it gave a few quick beats of its wings. Then, with its wings straight out, it shot down, sliding along the slope of the light. It looked to me just like a white pigeon. But whatever it was, when the cobs saw it coming straight down at them, they ran away fast across the mountain. They left me safe, only very frightened. As soon as it had sent them off, the bird went sliding up the light again. The moment it reached the ball, the light disappeared. It was just as if a cover had been closed over a window. I saw it no more. But I had no more trouble with the cobs that night or ever after.'

'How strange!' cried Curdie.

'Yes, it was strange. But I cannot help believing it, whether you do or not,' said his mother.

'It is exactly as your mother told it to me the very next morning,' said his father.

'You do not think I am doubting my own mother?' cried Curdie.

'There are other people in the world just as worth believing as your own mother,' said his mother. 'I do not know that she is any more fit to be believed just because she happens to be your mother, Mr. Curdie. There are mothers far more likely to tell lies than the little girl I saw talking to the primroses a few weeks ago. If she were to lie, I should begin to doubt my own word.'

'But princesses have told lies as well as other people,' said Curdie.

'Yes, but not princesses like that child. She is a good girl, I am certain. And that is more than being a princess. Be sure of it, you will be sorry for behaving so to her, Curdie. You should at least have kept quiet.'

'I am sorry now,' answered Curdie.

'You should go and tell her so, then.'

'I do not see how I could manage that. They would not let a miner boy like me have a word with her alone. And I could not tell her in front of that nurse of hers. She would ask ever so many questions. And I do not know how many the little princess would like me to answer. She told me that Lootie did not know anything about her coming to get me out of the mountain. I am certain she would have stopped her somehow if she had known it. But I may have a chance before long. And in the meantime I must try to do something for her. I think, father, I have got on the track at last.'

'Have you, indeed, my boy?' said Peter. 'I am sure you deserve some success. You have worked very hard for it. What have you found out?'

'It is difficult, you know, father, inside the mountain. Especially in the dark, and not knowing what turns you have taken, it is hard to tell the lie of things outside.'

'Impossible, my boy, without a chart, or at least a compass,' returned his father.

'Well, I think I have nearly discovered in what direction the cobs are mining. If I am right, I know something else that I can put to it. And then one and one will make three.'

'They very often do, Curdie. We miners ought to be very well aware of that. Now tell us, my boy, what the two things are. And see whether we can guess at the same third as you.'

'I do not see what that has to do with the princess,' interposed his mother.

'I will soon let you see that, mother. Perhaps you may think me foolish. But until I am sure there is nothing in my present fancy, I am more determined than ever to go on with my observations. Just as we came to the channel by which we got out, I heard the miners at work somewhere near. I think down below us. Now since I began to watch them, they have mined a good half-mile, in a straight line. And so far as I am aware, they are working in no other part of the mountain. But I never could tell in what direction they were going. When we came out in the king's garden, however, I thought at once whether it was possible they were working towards the king's house. And what I want to do tonight is to make sure whether they are or not. I will take a light with me--'

'Oh, Curdie,' cried his mother, 'then they will see you.'

'I am no more afraid of them now than I was before,' rejoined Curdie. 'Now that I have got this precious shoe, they cannot make another such in a hurry. And one bare foot will do for my purpose. Woman as she may be, I will not spare her next time. But I shall be careful with my light, for I do not want them to see me. I will not stick it in my hat.'

'Go on, then, and tell us what you mean to do.'

'I mean to take a bit of paper with me and a pencil. And I will go in at the mouth of the stream by which we came out. I shall mark on the paper as near as I can the angle of every turning I take until I find the cobs at work. And so I will get a good idea in what direction they are going. If it should prove to be nearly parallel with the stream, I shall know it is towards the king's house they are working.'

'And what if you should? How much wiser will you be then?'

'Wait a minute, mother dear. I told you that when I came upon the royal family in the cave, they were talking of their prince--Harelip, they called him--marrying a sun-woman. That means one of us--one with toes on her feet. Now one of them made a speech that night at their great gathering. I heard only a part of it. He said that peace would be secured for a generation at least by the promise the prince would hold for the good behaviour of her relatives. That is what he said. He must have meant the sun-woman the prince was to marry. I am quite sure the king is much too proud to wish his son to marry any but a princess. He is also much too clever to think that his having a peasant woman for a wife would be of any great advantage to them.'

'I see what you are driving at now,' said his mother.

'But,' said his father, 'our king would dig the mountain down to the plain before he would have his princess the wife of a cob, even if he were ten times a prince.'

'Yes; but they think so much of themselves!' said his mother. 'Small creatures always do. The bantam is the proudest cock in my little yard.'

'And I think,' said Curdie, 'if they once got her, they would tell the king they would kill her unless he agreed to the marriage.'

'They might say so,' said his father, 'but they would not kill her. They would keep her alive because it gave them power over our king. Whatever he did to them, they would threaten to do the same to the princess.'

'And they are bad enough to hurt her just for their own fun--I know that,' said his mother.

'Anyhow, I will keep a watch on them, and see what they are up to,' said Curdie. 'It is too terrible to think of. I dare not let myself do it. But they shall not have her--at least if I can help it. So, mother dear--my clue is all right--will you get me a bit of paper and a pencil and a lump of pease pudding, and I will set out at once. I saw a place where I can climb over the wall of the garden quite easily.'

'You must mind and keep out of the way of the men on the watch,' said his mother.

'That I will. I do not want them to know anything about it. They would spoil it all. The cobs would only try some other plan--they are such stubborn creatures! I shall take good care, mother. They will not kill and eat me either, if they should come upon me. So you need not mind them.'

His mother got him what he had asked for, and Curdie set out. Close beside the door by which the princess left the garden for the mountain stood a great rock. By climbing it Curdie got over the wall. He tied his clue to a stone just inside the channel of the stream, and took his pickaxe with him. He had not gone far before he met a terrible creature coming towards the mouth. The spot was too narrow for two of almost any size or shape. Besides, Curdie had no wish to let the creature pass. He could not use his pickaxe, however, so he had a hard struggle with him. It was only after receiving many bites, some of them bad, that he succeeded in killing him with his pocket-knife. Having dragged him out, he hurried to get in again before another should block the way.

I need not follow him farther in this night's adventures. He returned to his breakfast, satisfied that the goblins were mining in the direction of the palace. They were on so low a level that their intention must, he thought, be to dig under the walls of the king's house, and rise up inside it. In order, he fully believed, to lay hands on the little princess, and carry her off for a wife to their terrible Harelip.

CHAPTER 24. Irene Behaves Like a Princess

Irene Behaves Like a Princess

When the princess woke from the sweetest of sleeps, she found her nurse bending over her. The housekeeper was looking over the nurse's shoulder. The laundry girl was looking over the housekeeper's. The room was full of women servants. The gentlemen-at-arms, with a long line of servants behind them, were looking in, or trying to look in, at the door of the child's room.

'Are those terrible creatures gone?' asked the princess. She remembered first what had frightened her in the morning.

'You bad, bad little princess!' cried Lootie.

Her face was very pale, with red lines in it. She looked as if she were going to shake her. But Irene said nothing. She only waited to hear what should come next.

'How could you get under the clothes like that, and make us all think you were lost! And keep it up all day too! You are the most stubborn child! It's anything but fun for us, I can tell you!'

It was the only way the nurse could explain her disappearance.

'I did not do that, Lootie,' said Irene, very quietly.

'Do not tell stories!' cried her nurse quite rudely.

'I shall tell you nothing at all,' said Irene.

'That's just as bad,' said the nurse.

'Just as bad to say nothing at all as to tell stories?' exclaimed the princess. 'I will ask my papa about that. He will not say so. And I do not think he will like you to say so.'

'Tell me directly what you mean by it!' screamed the nurse. She was half wild with anger at the princess and fear at the possible results to herself.

'When I tell you the truth, Lootie,' said the princess, who somehow did not feel at all angry, 'you say to me "Do not tell stories". It seems I must tell stories before you will believe me.'

'You are very rude, princess,' said the nurse.

'You are so rude, Lootie, that I will not speak to you again until you are sorry. Why should I, when I know you will not believe me?' returned the princess. For she did know perfectly well that if she were to tell Lootie what she had been about, the more she went on to tell her, the less would she believe her.

'You are the most annoying child!' cried her nurse. 'You deserve to be well punished for your bad behaviour.'

'Please, Mrs Housekeeper,' said the princess, 'will you take me to your room, and keep me until my king-papa comes? I will ask him to come as soon as he can.'

Every one stared at these words. Up to this moment they had all regarded her as little more than a baby.

But the housekeeper was afraid of the nurse, and tried to smooth things over, saying:

'I am sure, princess, nursie did not mean to be rude to you.'

'I do not think my papa would wish me to have a nurse who spoke to me as Lootie does. If she thinks I tell lies, she had better either say so to my papa, or go away. Sir Walter, will you take charge of me?'

'With the greatest of pleasure, princess,' answered the captain of the gentlemen-at-arms, walking with his great stride into the room.

The crowd of servants made eager way for him. He bowed low before the little princess's bed. 'I shall send my servant at once, on the fastest horse in the stable, to tell your king-papa that Your Royal Highness desires his presence. When you have chosen one of these under-servants to wait upon you, I shall order the room to be cleared.'

'Thank you very much, Sir Walter,' said the princess. Her eye glanced towards a girl with rosy cheeks who had lately come to the house as a kitchen maid.

But when Lootie saw the eyes of her dear princess going in search of another instead of her, she fell upon her knees by the bedside. She burst into a great cry of distress.

'I think, Sir Walter,' said the princess, 'I will keep Lootie. But I put myself under your care. And you need not trouble my king-papa until I speak to you again. Will you all please to go away? I am quite safe and well. I did not hide myself either to amuse myself, or to trouble my people. Lootie, will you please to dress me.'

CHAPTER 25. Curdie Comes to Grief

Curdie Comes to Grief

Everything was quiet above ground for some time. The king was still away in a distant part of his lands. The men-at-arms kept watching around the house. They were very surprised to find the ugly body of the goblin creature killed by Curdie at the foot of the rock in the garden. But they decided it had been killed in the mines and had come out there to die. Except for an occasional quick look at a live one, they saw nothing to cause alarm. Curdie kept watching in the mountain, and the goblins kept digging deeper into the earth. As long as they went deeper, Curdie judged there was no immediate danger.

To Irene the summer was as full of pleasure as ever. For a long time she did not see her grandmother, although she often thought of her during the day and often dreamed about her at night. The young goats and the flowers were as much her joy as ever. She made as much friendship with the miners' children she met on the mountain as Lootie would permit. But Lootie had very silly ideas about the dignity of a princess. She did not understand that the truest princess is just the one who loves all her brothers and sisters best. She is also the one most able to do them good by being humble towards them. At the same time Lootie was much changed for the better in her behaviour to the princess. She could not help seeing that the princess was no longer a mere child, but wiser than her age would explain. She kept foolishly whispering to the servants, however. Sometimes she said the princess was not right in her mind. Sometimes she said she was too good to live. And she said other nonsense of the same sort.

All this time Curdie had to be sorry, without a chance of confessing, that he had behaved so unkindly to the princess. This perhaps made him the more hard-working in his efforts to serve her. His mother and he often talked on the subject. She comforted him and told him she was sure he would some day have the opportunity he so much desired.

Here I should like to remark, for the sake of princes and princesses in general, that it is a low and shameful thing to refuse to confess a fault, or even an error. If a true princess has done wrong, she is always uneasy until she has had an opportunity of throwing the wrong away from her by saying: 'I did it; and I wish I had not; and I am sorry for having done it.' So you see there is some reason for supposing that Curdie was not a miner only, but a prince as well. Many such instances have been known in the world's history.

At length, however, he began to see signs of a change in the movements of the goblin diggers. They were going no deeper, but had started running on a level. And he watched them, therefore, more closely than ever. All at once, one night, they came to a slope of very hard rock. They began to go up along the sloping surface. Having reached its top, they went again on a level for a night or two. After that they began to go up once more, and kept on at a pretty steep angle. At length Curdie judged it was time to move his watching to another quarter. The next night he did not go to the mine at all. He left his pickaxe and clue at home, and took only his usual lumps of bread and pease pudding. Then he went down the mountain to the king's house. He climbed over the wall and remained in the garden the whole night. He went on hands and knees from one spot to the other, and lay at full length with his ear to the ground, listening. But he heard nothing except the step of the men-at-arms as they marched about. As the night was cloudy and there was no moon, he had little difficulty in avoiding their observation. For several following nights he continued to visit the garden and listen, but with no success.

At length, early one evening, his watching came to a sudden end. Perhaps he had become careless of his own safety, or perhaps the growing moon had become strong enough to show him. He was moving from behind the rock where the stream ran out. He had been listening all round it in the hope it might carry to his ear some sign of where the goblin miners were. Just as he came into the moonlight on the lawn, a whizz in his ear and a blow upon his leg startled him. He instantly squatted in the hope of avoiding further notice. But when he heard the sound of running feet, he jumped up to take the chance of escape by flight. He fell, however, with a sharp shoot of pain. The bolt of a crossbow had wounded his leg, and the blood was now streaming from it. He was instantly laid hold of by two or three of the men-at-arms. It was useless to struggle, and he submitted in silence.

'It's a boy!' cried several of them together, in a tone of amazement. 'I thought it was one of those demons. What are you doing here?'

'Going to have a little rough usage, apparently,' said Curdie, laughing, as the men shook him.

'Impertinence will do you no good. You have no business here in the king's grounds. And if you don't give a true account of yourself, you shall be treated as a thief.'

'Why, what else could he be?' said one.

'He might have been after a lost young goat, you know,' suggested another.

'I see no good in trying to excuse him. He has no business here, anyhow.'

'Let me go away, then, if you please,' said Curdie.

'But we don't please--not except you give a good account of yourself.'

'I don't feel quite sure whether I can trust you,' said Curdie.

'We are the king's own men-at-arms,' said the captain politely, for he was taken with Curdie's appearance and courage.

'Well, I will tell you all about it--if you will promise to listen to me and not do anything rash.'

'I call that cool!' said one of the party, laughing. 'He will tell us what mischief he was about, if we promise to do as pleases him.'

'I was about no mischief,' said Curdie.

But before he could say more he turned faint, and fell senseless on the grass. Then first they discovered that the bolt they had shot, taking him for one of the goblin creatures, had wounded him.

They carried him into the house and laid him down in the hall. The report spread that they had caught a robber, and the servants crowded in to see the villain. Amongst the rest came the nurse. The moment she saw him she exclaimed with anger:

'I say it is the same young bad man, a miner, who was not kind to me and the princess on the mountain. He actually wanted to kiss the princess. I took good care of that--the bad man! And he was moving about quietly, was he? Just like his daring!' The princess was fast asleep, so she could say things that were not true as she liked.

When he heard this, the captain, although he had a lot of doubt about its truth, decided to keep Curdie a prisoner until they could look into the matter. So, after they had brought him round a little, and cared for his wound, which was rather a bad one, they laid him, still very tired from the loss of blood, upon a mattress in a room not being used--one of those already so often mentioned--and locked the door, and left him. He passed a troubled night, and in the morning they found him talking in a mad way. In the evening he came to himself, but felt very weak, and his leg was very painful. Wondering where he was, and seeing one of the men-at-arms in the room, he began to question him and soon remembered the events of the night before. As he was himself unable to watch any more, he told the soldier all he knew about the goblins, and begged him to tell his companions, and stir them up to watch with ten times more care. But whether it was that he did not talk quite clearly, or that the whole thing appeared impossible to believe, certainly the man concluded that Curdie was only talking in a mad way still, and tried to persuade him to hold his tongue. This, of course, made Curdie very angry, who now felt in his turn what it was not to be believed, and the result was that his fever returned. By the time when, at his repeated requests, the captain was called, there could be no doubt that he was talking in a mad way. They did for him what they could, and promised everything he wanted, but with no intention of doing it. At last he went to sleep, and when at length his sleep grew deep and peaceful, they left him, locked the door again, and went away, intending to come back to him early in the morning.

CHAPTER 26. The Goblin-Miners

The Goblin-Miners

That same night several of the servants were talking together before going to bed.

'What can that noise be?' said one of the house servants, who had been listening for a moment or two.

'I've heard it the last two nights,' said the cook. 'If there were any about the place, I should have taken it for rats, but my Tom keeps them far enough.'

'I've heard, though,' said the kitchen servant, 'that rats move about in great groups sometimes. There may be an army of them coming in on us. I've heard the noises yesterday and today too.'

'It'll be grand fun, then, for my Tom and Mrs Housekeeper's Bob,' said the cook. 'They'll be friends for once in their lives, and fight on the same side. I'll promise Tom and Bob together will chase away any number of rats.'

'It seems to me,' said the nurse, 'that the noises are much too loud for that. I have heard them all day, and my princess has asked me several times what they could be. Sometimes they sound like distant thunder, and sometimes like the noises you hear in the mountain from those awful miners below.'

'I wouldn't be surprised,' said the cook, 'if it was the miners after all. They may have come on some hole in the mountain through which the noises reach to us. They are always digging and blowing up rock and breaking, you know.'

As he spoke, there came a great rolling rumble below them, and the house shook. They all jumped up in fear, and rushed to the hall. They found the gentlemen-at-arms in great fear also. They had sent to wake their captain. He said from their description that it must have been an earthquake. This was very rare in that country, but had happened almost within the century. Then, strange to say, he went to bed again and fell fast asleep. He did not once think of Curdie, or connect the noises they had heard with what he had told them. He had not believed Curdie. If he had, he would at once have thought of what he had said, and would have taken care. As they heard nothing more, they decided that Sir Walter was right, and that the danger was over for perhaps another hundred years. The fact, as found out later, was that the goblins had been working up a second sloping face of stone. They had reached a huge block which lay under the cellars of the house, within the line of the foundations.

It was so round that when they succeeded, after hard work, in moving it without blowing it up, it rolled thundering down the slope with a bouncing, shaking roll, which shook the foundations of the house. The goblins were themselves afraid at the noise. They knew, by careful watching and measuring, that they must now be very near, if not under the king's house. They feared making an alarm. They therefore remained quiet for a while. When they began to work again, they no doubt thought themselves very lucky in coming upon a vein of sand which filled a winding crack in the rock on which the house was built. By digging this away they came out in the king's wine cellar.

As soon as they found where they were, they ran back again, like rats into their holes. They ran at full speed to the goblin palace, and announced their success to the king and queen with shouts of victory.

In a moment the goblin royal family and the whole goblin people were on their way in great hurry to the king's house. Each was eager to have a share in the glory of carrying off that same night the Princess Irene.

The queen went walking along in one shoe of stone and one of skin.

This could not have been pleasant, and my readers may wonder that, with such skilled workmen about her, she had not yet replaced the shoe carried off by Curdie. As the king, however, had more than one reason to object to her stone shoes, he no doubt took advantage of the discovery of her toes. He threatened to show her deformity if she had another made. I presume he insisted on her being content with skin shoes. He allowed her to wear the remaining granite one on the present occasion only because she was going out to war.

They soon arrived in the king's wine cellar. They paid no attention to its huge vessels, of which they did not know the use. They went on at once, but as quietly as they could, to force the door that led upwards.

CHAPTER 27. The Goblins in the King's House

The Goblins in the King's House

When Curdie fell asleep he began to dream at once. He thought he was going up the Mountainside from the mouth of the mine. He was singing and making music: 'Ring, dod, bang!' Then he met a woman and child who had lost their way. From that point he went on dreaming everything that had happened to him since he met the princess and Lootie. He dreamed how he had watched the goblins, how they had taken him, and how the princess had saved him. He dreamed everything, until he was wounded, captured, and locked up by the men-at-arms. And now he thought he was lying wide awake where they had laid him. Suddenly he heard a great sound like thunder.

'The cobs are coming!' he said. 'They did not believe a word I told them! The cobs will be carrying off the princess from under their stupid noses! But they will not! that they will not!'

He jumped up, as he thought, and began to dress. But to his dismay, he found that he was still lying in bed.

'Now then, I will!' he said. 'Here goes! I am up now!'

But yet again he found himself comfortable in bed. Twenty times he tried, and twenty times he failed. In fact he was not awake, only dreaming that he was. At last, in great pain and despair, he gave a great cry. He thought he heard the goblins all over the house. Then he thought a hand came upon the lock of his door. It opened, and he looked up and saw a lady with white hair. She was carrying a silver box in her hand and came into the room. She came to his bed, he thought, and touched his head and face with cool, soft hands. She took the dressing from his leg and rubbed it with something that smelt like roses. Then she waved her hands over him three times. At the last wave of her hands everything vanished. He felt himself sinking into the deepest sleep. He remembered nothing more until he woke for real.

The setting moon was throwing a weak light through the window, and the house was full of noise. There was soft, heavy, many-footed stamping. There was a clashing and clanging of weapons, the voices of men and the cries of women. Mixed with this was a terrible bellowing that sounded victorious. The cobs were in the house! He sprang from his bed and hurried on some of his clothes. He did not forget his shoes, which had nails in them. Then he saw an old hunting-knife, or short sword, hanging on the wall. He caught it and rushed down the stairs. The sounds of fighting guided him, and they grew louder and louder.

When he reached the ground floor he found the whole place full of them.

All the goblins of the mountain seemed gathered there. He rushed among them, shouting:

'One, two, Hit and hew! Three, four, Blast and bore!'

and with every rhyme he came down with a great stamp upon a foot. At the same time he cut at their faces. Indeed, he was doing a sword dance of the wildest kind. Away scattered the goblins in every direction—into closets, up stairs, into chimneys, up on rafters, and down to the cellars. Curdie went on stamping and slashing and singing. But he saw nothing of the people of the house until he came to the great hall. The moment he entered it, a great goblin shout arose. The last of the men-at-arms, the captain himself, was on the floor. He was buried beneath a rolling crowd of goblins. Each knight was busy defending himself as well as he could by stabbing the thick bodies of the goblins. He had soon found their heads were almost impossible to hurt. But the queen had attacked his legs and feet with her horrible granite shoe, and he was soon down. The captain had got his back to the wall and stood out longer. The goblins would have torn them all to pieces, but the king had given orders to carry them away alive. Over each of them, in twelve groups, stood a knot of goblins. As many as could find room were sitting upon their fallen bodies.

Curdie burst in dancing and turning and stamping and singing like a small whirlwind in human form.

'Where it is all a hole, sir, Never can be holes: Why should their shoes have soles, sir, When they have got no souls?

'But she upon her foot, sir, Has a granite shoe: The strongest leather boot, sir, Six would soon be through.'

The queen gave a howl of rage and dismay. Before she recovered her presence of mind, Curdie had begun with the group nearest him. He had eleven of the knights on their legs again.

'Stamp on their feet!' he cried as each man rose. In a few minutes the hall was nearly empty. The goblins were running from it as fast as they could, howling and shrieking and limping. Every now and then they crouched as they ran to hold their wounded feet in their hard hands. They also tried to protect them from the frightful stamp-stamp of the armed men.

And now Curdie approached the group that kept guard over the fallen captain. They trusted in the queen and her shoe. The king sat on the captain's head, but the queen stood in front like a very angry cat. Her upright eyes gleamed green, and her hair stood half up from her horrible head. Her heart was shaking, however, and she kept moving her skin-shod foot about with nervous fear. When Curdie was within a few paces, she rushed at him. She made one tremendous stamp at his opposing foot, which happily he pulled back in time. Then she caught him round the waist to dash him on the marble floor. But just as she caught him, he came down with all the weight of his iron-shod shoe upon her skin-shod foot. With a terrible howl she dropped him, squatted on the floor, and took her foot in both her hands. Meanwhile the rest rushed on the king and the bodyguard, sent them flying, and lifted the fallen captain. He was almost pressed to death. It was some moments before he recovered breath and consciousness.

'Where is the princess?' cried Curdie, again and again.

No one knew, and off they all rushed in search of her.

Through every room in the house they went, but nowhere was she to be found. Neither was one of the servants to be seen. But Curdie had kept to the lower part of the house, which was now quiet enough. He began to hear a confused sound like a distant noise, and set out to find where it came from. The noise grew as his sharp ears guided him to a stair and so to the wine cellar. It was full of goblins, and the butler was supplying them with wine as fast as he could draw it.

While the queen and her group had met the soldiers, Harelip with another group had gone off to search the house. They caught every one they met. When they could find no more, they hurried away to carry them safe to the caves below. But the butler was among them. He found that their path went through the wine room. He thought of persuading them to taste the wine. As he had hoped, they no sooner tasted than they wanted more. The beaten goblins, on their way below, joined them. When Curdie entered, they were all pressing around the butler with open hands. In their hands were containers of every kind, from a cooking pot to a silver cup. The butler sat at the tap of a huge barrel, filling and filling. Curdie looked once around the place before starting his attack. He saw in the farthest corner a frightened group of servants, not watched, but bending low with no courage to try to escape. Among them was the terrified face of Lootie. But nowhere could he see the princess. A horrible belief seized him: Harelip had already carried her off. He rushed among them, unable to sing any more for anger. He stamped and cut with greater anger than ever.

'Stamp on their feet! Stamp on their feet!' he shouted. In a moment the goblins were disappearing through the hole in the floor like rats and mice.

They could not vanish so fast, however. Many more goblin feet had to go limping back over the paths under the mountain that morning.

Soon, however, the king and his group came down from above to help them. The terrible queen was at their head. She found Curdie busy among her unlucky subjects again. She rushed at him once more with the anger of hopelessness. This time she gave him a bad bruise on the foot. Then a regular stamping fight began between them. Curdie kept her from closing her powerful arms around him with the point of his hunting knife. He watched for his chance to get one more good stamp at her foot, which was covered in skin. But the queen was more careful as well as more quick than before.

The rest, meanwhile, found their enemy thus matched for the moment. They paused in their wild hurry and turned to the shaking group of women in the corner. Harelip seemed determined to copy his father and have a sun-woman of some sort to share his future throne. He rushed at them, caught up Lootie, and ran with her to the hole. She gave a great cry. Curdie heard her and saw the trouble she was in. He gathered all his strength and gave the queen a sudden cut across the face with his weapon. As she started back, he came down with all his weight on the proper foot and jumped to Lootie's rescue. The prince had two feet with no protection. Curdie stamped on both of them just as he reached the hole. He dropped his load and rolled away, crying out, into the earth. Curdie made one stab at him as he disappeared. He caught hold of the senseless Lootie and dragged her back to the corner. There he stood guard over her, preparing once more to meet the queen.

Her face was streaming with blood, and her eyes flashed green lightning through it. She came on with her mouth open and her teeth grinning like a tiger's. The king and her guard of the thickest goblins followed her. But at the same moment the captain and his men rushed in and ran at them, stamping furiously. They dared not meet such an attack. Away they ran, the queen first. Of course, the right thing would have been to take the king and queen prisoners and hold them as hostages for the princess. But they were so anxious to find her that no one thought of holding them back until it was too late.

Having this way rescued the servants, they set about searching the house once more. None of them could give the least information about the princess. Lootie was almost foolish with terror. Although she could hardly walk, she would not leave Curdie's side for a single moment. Again he let the others search the rest of the house. There, except a dismayed goblin hiding here and there, they found no one. He asked Lootie to take him to the princess's room. She was as obedient as if he had been the king.

He found the bedclothes thrown about, and most of them on the floor. The princess's clothes were scattered all over the room, which was in the greatest confusion. It was only too clear that the goblins had been there. Curdie no longer had any doubt that she had been carried off at the very first of the attack. With a sharp pain of hopelessness he saw how wrong they had been not to secure the king and queen and prince. But he determined to find and rescue the princess as she had found and rescued him, or meet the worst fate to which the goblins could doom him.

CHAPTER 28. Curdie's Guide

Curdie's Guide

Just as the comfort of this decision came to his mind and he was turning away for the cellar to follow the goblins into their hole, something touched his hand. It was the lightest touch, and when he looked he could see nothing. Feeling and looking about in the grey light of the dawn, his fingers found a tight thread. He looked again, and closely, but still could see nothing. It came to him in a flash that this must be the princess's thread. Without saying a word, for he knew no one would believe him any more than he had believed the princess, he followed the thread with his finger. He managed to get away from Lootie without her seeing, and was soon out of the house and on the mountainside. He was surprised that, if the thread were indeed the grandmother's messenger, it should have led the princess, as he supposed it must, into the mountain. There she would be sure to meet the goblins rushing back enraged from their defeat. But he hurried on in the hope of catching her first. When he arrived, however, at the place where the path turned off for the mine, he found that the thread did not turn with it, but went straight up the mountain. Could it be that the thread was leading him home to his mother's cottage? Could the princess be there? He jumped up the mountain like one of its own goats, and before the sun was up the thread had brought him indeed to his mother's door. There it vanished from his fingers, and he could not find it, search as he might.

The door was on the latch, and he entered. There sat his mother by the fire, and in her arms lay the princess, fast asleep.

'Hush, Curdie!' said his mother. 'Do not wake her. I'm so glad you're come! I thought the cobs must have got you again!'

With a heart full of delight, Curdie sat down at a corner of the hearth, on a stool opposite his mother's chair, and gazed at the princess, who slept as peacefully as if she had been in her own bed. All at once she opened her eyes and fixed them on him.

'Oh, Curdie! you're come!' she said quietly. 'I thought you would!'

Curdie rose and stood before her with downcast eyes.

'Irene,' he said, 'I am very sorry I did not believe you.'

'Oh, never mind, Curdie!' answered the princess. 'You couldn't, you know. You do believe me now, don't you?'

'I can't help it now. I ought to have helped it before.'

'Why can't you help it now?'

'Because, just as I was going into the mountain to look for you, I got hold of your thread, and it brought me here.'

'Then you've come from my house, have you?'

'Yes, I have.'

'I didn't know you were there.'

'I've been there two or three days, I believe.'

'And I never knew it! Then perhaps you can tell me why my grandmother has brought me here? I can't think. Something woke me--I didn't know what, but I was frightened, and I felt for the thread, and there it was! I was more frightened still when it brought me out on the mountain, for I thought it was going to take me into it again, and I like the outside of it best. I supposed you were in trouble again, and I had to get you out. But it brought me here instead; and, oh, Curdie! your mother has been so kind to me--just like my own grandmother!'

Here Curdie's mother gave the princess a hug, and the princess turned and gave her a sweet smile, and held up her mouth to kiss her.

'Then you didn't see the cobs?' asked Curdie.

'No; I haven't been into the mountain, I told you, Curdie.'

'But the cobs have been into your house--all over it--and into your bedroom, making such a row!'

'What did they want there? It was very rude of them.'

'They wanted you--to carry you off into the mountain with them, for a wife to their prince Harelip.'

'Oh, how dreadful!' cried the princess, shuddering.

'But you needn't be afraid, you know. Your grandmother takes care of you.'

'Ah! you do believe in my grandmother, then? I'm so glad! She made me think you would some day.'

All at once Curdie remembered his dream, and was silent, thinking.

'But how did you come to be in my house, and me not know it?' asked the princess.

Then Curdie had to explain everything--how he had watched for her sake, how he had been wounded and shut up by the soldiers, how he heard the noises and could not rise, and how the beautiful old lady had come to him, and all that followed.

'Poor Curdie! to lie there hurt and ill, and me never to know it!' exclaimed the princess, stroking his rough hand. 'I would have come and nursed you, if they had told me.'

'I didn't see you were lame,' said his mother.

'Am I, mother? Oh--yes--I suppose I ought to be! I declare I've never thought of it since I got up to go down amongst the cobs!'

'Let me see the wound,' said his mother.

He pulled down his stocking--when behold, except a great scar, his leg was perfectly sound!

Curdie and his mother gazed in each other's eyes, full of wonder, but Irene called out:

'I thought so, Curdie! I was sure it wasn't a dream. I was sure my grandmother had been to see you. Don't you smell the roses? It was my grandmother healed your leg, and sent you to help me.'

'No, Princess Irene,' said Curdie; 'I wasn't good enough to be allowed to help you: I didn't believe you. Your grandmother took care of you without me.'

'She sent you to help my people, anyhow. I wish my king-papa would come. I do want so to tell him how good you have been!'

'But,' said the mother, 'we are forgetting how frightened your people must be. You must take the princess home at once, Curdie--or at least go and tell them where she is.'

'Yes, mother. Only I'm dreadfully hungry. Do let me have some breakfast first. They ought to have listened to me, and then they wouldn't have been taken by surprise as they were.'

'That is true, Curdie; but it is not for you to blame them much. You remember?'

'Yes, mother, I do. Only I must really have something to eat.'

'You shall, my boy--as fast as I can get it,' said his mother, rising and setting the princess on her chair.

But before his breakfast was ready, Curdie jumped up so suddenly as to startle both his companions.

'Mother, mother!' he cried, 'I was forgetting. You must take the princess home yourself. I must go and wake my father.'

Without a word of explanation, he rushed to the place where his father was sleeping. Having thoroughly roused him with what he told him he darted out of the cottage.

CHAPTER 29. Masonwork

Masonwork

He suddenly remembered the goblins' plan to try their second idea after the first one failed. They were surely already working, so the mine was in great danger of being flooded and made useless—not to mention the lives of the miners.

He woke all the miners he could reach. When he got to the mouth of the mine, he found his father and many others just going in. They all hurried to the passage by which he had found a way into the goblin country. Peter had already prepared many blocks of stone and cement there, ready to build up the weak place—which the goblins knew well. Only two could build at once, but they set all the others to prepare the cement and pass the stones. So they finished a huge support wall in one day. It filled the whole passage and was held everywhere by the solid rock. Before the usual stopping time, they were sure the mine was safe.

All the time they had heard goblin hammers and picks busy. At last they thought they heard sounds of water they had never heard before. But they found another reason for that when they left the mine. They stepped out into a great storm that was raging all over the mountain. The thunder was rolling, and the lightning was flashing out of a huge black cloud above. The cloud hung down edges of thick mist over its sides. The lightning was also breaking out of the mountain and flashing up into the cloud. The streams had grown into raging floods, so it was clear the storm had been raging all day.

The wind was blowing as if it would blow him off the mountain. But Curdie was worried about his mother and the princess, so he rushed up through the thick storm. Even if they had not set out before the storm came, he did not think they were safe. In such a storm even their poor little house was in danger. Soon he found that a huge rock, against which the house was built, had protected it from both the wind and the water. Without that rock, the house would have been swept away or blown away. The rock split the rush of water behind it into two floods. These two dangerous, roaring streams met again in front of the house. His mother and the princess could not possibly have crossed them. It was very hard for him to force his way through one of them and up to the door.

The moment his hand touched the door latch, through all the noise of wind and water came the happy cry of the princess:

'There's Curdie! Curdie! Curdie!'

She was sitting wrapped in blankets on the bed. His mother was trying for the hundredth time to light the fire, which the rain coming down the chimney had put out. The floor of clay was all mud, and the whole place looked miserable. But the faces of the mother and the princess shone as if their troubles only made them happier. Curdie burst out laughing at the sight of them.

'I never had such fun!' said the princess, her eyes twinkling and her pretty teeth shining. 'How nice it must be to live in a house on the mountain!'

'It all depends on what kind your inside house is,' said the mother.

'I know what you mean,' said Irene. 'That's the kind of thing my grandmother says.'

By the time Peter returned, the storm was almost over. But the streams were so fierce and so swollen that the princess could not go down the mountain. It was also very dangerous for Peter or Curdie to try in the growing darkness.

'They will be very frightened about you,' said Peter to the princess, 'but we cannot help it. We must wait until morning.'

With Curdie's help, the fire was lit at last. The mother set about making their supper. After supper they all told the princess stories until she grew sleepy. Then Curdie's mother laid her in Curdie's bed, which was in a tiny little room in the roof. As soon as she was in bed, through a little window low down in the roof she saw her grandmother's lamp shining far away below. She gazed at the beautiful silvery globe until she fell asleep.

CHAPTER 30. The King and the Kiss

The next morning the sun rose so bright that Irene said the rain had washed his face and let the light out clean. The rivers were still running down the side of the mountain. But they were so much smaller that they were not dangerous in the day. After an early breakfast, Peter went to his work. Curdie and his mother set out to take the princess home. They had difficulty getting her dry across the streams. Curdie had to carry her again and again. But at last they got safe on the wider part of the road. They walked slowly down towards the house of the king. And what should they see as they turned the last corner but the last of the troop of the king riding through the gate!

'Oh, Curdie!' cried Irene, hitting her hands together with joy, 'my king-papa is come.'

The moment Curdie heard that, he caught her up in his arms. He set off at full speed, crying:

'Come on, mother dear! The king may break his heart before he knows that she is safe.'

Irene held round his neck and he ran with her like a deer. When he entered the gate into the court, there sat the king on his horse. All the people of the house were about him, crying and hanging their heads. The king was not crying, but his face was white as a dead man's. He looked as if the life had gone out of him. The men-at-arms he had brought with him sat with faces full of horror. But their eyes flashed with anger. They waited only for the word of the king to do something. They did not know what, and nobody knew what.

The day before, the men-at-arms belonging to the house were sure the princess had been carried away. They rushed after the goblins into the hole. But they found that the goblins had already so skillfully blocked the narrowest part, not many feet below the cellar. Without miners and their tools the men could do nothing. Not one of them knew where the mouth of the mine lay. Some of those who had set out to find it had been caught by the storm. They had not even yet returned. Poor Sir Walter was especially filled with shame. He almost hoped the king would order his head to be cut off. To think of that sweet little face down among the goblins was too terrible to bear.

When Curdie ran in at the gate with the princess in his arms, they were all so absorbed in their own suffering. They were also filled with fear and wonder by the king's presence and grief. So no one saw his arrival. He went straight up to the king, where he sat on his horse.

'Papa! papa!' the princess cried, stretching out her arms to him; 'here I am!'

The king started. The color rushed to his face. He gave a cry without words. Curdie held up the princess, and the king bent down and took her from his arms. As he held her to his chest, the big tears went dropping down his cheeks and his beard. And such a shout arose from all the people watching that the surprised horses jumped and danced. The armour rang and made loud noises, and the rocks of the mountain echoed back the noises. The princess greeted them all as she sat close in her father's chest. The king did not set her down until she had told them all the story. But she had more to tell about Curdie than about herself. And what she did tell about herself none of them could understand--except the king and Curdie. Curdie stood by the knee of the king stroking the neck of the great white horse. And still as she told what Curdie had done, Sir Walter and others added to what she told. Even Lootie joined in the praises of his courage and energy.

Curdie kept quiet, looking quietly up in the king's face. And his mother stood on the outside of the crowd listening with joy. The deeds of her son were pleasant in her ears. This went on until the princess caught sight of her.

'And there is his mother, king-papa!' she said. 'See--there. She is such a nice mother, and has been so kind to me!'

They all parted as the king made a sign to her to come forward. She obeyed, and he gave her his hand, but could not speak.

'And now, king-papa,' the princess went on, 'I must tell you another thing. One night long ago Curdie drove the goblins away and brought Lootie and me safe from the mountain. And I promised him a kiss when we got home, but Lootie would not let me give it him. I do not want you to scold Lootie, but I want you to tell her that a princess must do as she promises.'

'Indeed she must, my child--except it be wrong,' said the king. 'There, give Curdie a kiss.'

And as he spoke he held her towards him.

The princess reached down, threw her arms round Curdie's neck, and kissed him on the mouth. She said: 'There, Curdie! There's the kiss I promised you!'

Then they all went into the house. The cook rushed to the kitchen and the servants to their work. Lootie dressed Irene in her most shining clothes. The king put off his armour, and put on purple and gold. A messenger was sent for Peter and all the miners. There was a great and a grand feast, which continued long after the princess was put to bed.

CHAPTER 31. The Subterranean Waters

The Subterranean Waters

The king's musician was always with him as a guard. He was singing a song as he played his instrument. The song was about the princess and the goblins, and the brave acts of Curdie. All at once he stopped. His eyes were on one of the doors of the hall. Then the eyes of the king and his guests turned that way too. The next moment, through the open doorway came the princess Irene. She went straight up to her father. Her right hand was held out a little to the side. Her index finger, as her father and Curdie understood, was feeling its way along the invisible thread. The king took her on his knee, and she said in his ear:

'King-papa, do you hear that noise?'

'I hear nothing,' said the king.

'Listen,' she said, holding up her index finger.

The king listened, and a great silence fell upon the company. Each man saw that the king listened, and listened also. The musician sat with his instrument between his arms, and his finger silent upon the strings.

'I do hear a noise,' said the king at last--'a noise like distant thunder. It is coming nearer and nearer. What can it be?'

They all heard it now. Each seemed ready to jump to his feet as he listened. Yet all sat perfectly still. The noise came quickly nearer.

'What can it be?' said the king again.

'I think it must be another storm coming over the mountain,' said Sir Walter.

Then Curdie slipped from his seat at the first word of the king. He laid his ear to the ground. He rose up quickly and came near the king. He spoke very fast:

'Please, Your Majesty, I think I know what it is. I have no time to explain. That might make it too late for some of us. Will Your Majesty give orders that everybody leave the house as quickly as possible and get up the mountain?'

The king was the wisest man in the kingdom. He knew well there was a time when things must be done and questions left till later. He had faith in Curdie. He rose at once, with Irene in his arms. 'Every man and woman follow me,' he said, and walked out into the darkness.

Before he had reached the gate, the noise had grown to a great loud roar. The ground shook beneath their feet. Before the last of them had crossed the court, a huge rush of muddy water came out after them from the great hall door. It almost swept them away. But they got safe out of the gate and up the mountain. The rushing water went roaring down the road into the valley beneath.

Curdie had left the king and the princess to look after his mother. He and his father caught her up, one on each side, when the stream reached them. They carried her safe and dry.

The king got out of the way of the water, a little up the mountain. He stood with the princess in his arms. He looked back with wonder at the rushing water. It shone fierce and foamy through the night. There Curdie joined them again.

'Now, Curdie,' said the king, 'what does it mean? Is this what you expected?'

'It is, Your Majesty,' said Curdie. Then he told him about the second plan of the goblins. The goblins thought the miners were more important to the upper world than they were. They had decided that if they failed to carry off the king's daughter, they would flood the mine and drown the miners. Then he explained what the miners had done to prevent it. The goblins had let loose all the underground reservoirs and streams, as they had planned. They expected the water to run down into the mine. The mine was lower than their part of the mountain. They had broken a passage through into it, as they supposed. But they did not know about the solid wall close behind. The easiest way out for the water turned out to be the tunnel they had made to the king's house. The young miner had not thought of this possible disaster until he had laid his ear to the floor of the hall.

What was to be done then? The house appeared in danger of falling. Every moment the water was increasing.

'We must set out at once,' said the king. 'But how to get at the horses!'

'Shall I see if we can manage that?' said Curdie.

'Do,' said the king.

Curdie gathered the men-at-arms. He took them over the garden wall and so to the stables. They found their horses in terror. The water was rising fast around them. It was quite time they were got out. But there was no way to get them out except by riding them through the stream. The water was now pouring from the lower windows as well as the door. One horse was quite enough for any man to manage through such a rushing stream. Curdie got on the king's white horse. He led the way and brought them all in safety to the rising ground.

'Look, look, Curdie!' cried Irene, the moment he had got off the horse and led it up to the king.

Curdie did look. He saw, high in the air, somewhere about the top of the king's house, a great ball of light shining like the purest silver.

'Oh!' he cried in some fear, 'that is your grandmother's lamp! We must get her out. I will go and find her. The house may fall, you know.'

'My grandmother is in no danger,' said Irene, smiling.

'Here, Curdie, take the princess while I get on my horse,' said the king.

Curdie took the princess again. Both turned their eyes to the ball of light. The same moment a white bird shot from it. It came down with wings held out. It made one circle round the king and Curdie and the princess, and then glided up again. The light and the pigeon vanished together.

'Now, Curdie!' said the princess, as he lifted her to her father's arms. 'You see my grandmother knows all about it, and isn't frightened. I believe she could walk through that water and it wouldn't wet her a bit.'

'But, my child,' said the king, 'you will be cold if you haven't something more on. Run, Curdie, my boy, and bring anything you can lay your hands on to keep the princess warm. We have a long ride before us.'

Curdie was gone in a moment. He soon returned with a great rich fur. He also brought the news that dead goblins were tossing about in the current through the house. They had been caught in their own trap. Instead of the mine, they had flooded their own country. They were now swept up drowned. Irene shook with fear, but the king held her close to his chest. Then he turned to Sir Walter and said:

'Bring Curdie's father and mother here.'

'I wish,' said the king, when they stood before him, 'to take your son with me. He shall enter my guards at once, and wait for further promotion.'

Peter and his wife, overcome, only said very quiet thanks. But Curdie spoke in a clear voice.

'Please, Your Majesty,' he said, 'I cannot leave my father and mother.'

'That's right, Curdie!' cried the princess. 'I would not if I was you.'

The king looked at the princess and then at Curdie with a warm smile of satisfaction on his face.

'I too think you are right, Curdie,' he said, 'and I will not ask you again. But I shall have a chance of doing something for you some time.'

'Your Majesty has already allowed me to serve you,' said Curdie.

'But, Curdie,' said his mother, 'why should you not go with the king? We can get on very well without you.'

'But I cannot get on very well without you,' said Curdie. 'The king is very kind, but I could not be half the use to him that I am to you. Please, Your Majesty, if you would not mind giving my mother a red skirt! I should have got her one long ago, but for the goblins.'

'As soon as we get home,' said the king, 'Irene and I will search out the warmest one to be found, and send it by one of the gentlemen.'

'Yes, that we will, Curdie!' said the princess. 'And next summer we will come back and see you wear it, Curdie's mother,' she added. 'Shall we not, king-papa?'

'Yes, my love; I hope so,' said the king.

Then turning to the miners, he said:

'Will you do the best you can for my servants tonight? I hope they will be able to return to the house tomorrow.'

The miners with one voice promised their welcome. Then the king commanded his servants to mind whatever Curdie should say to them, and after shaking hands with him and his father and mother, the king and the princess and all their company rode away down the side of the new stream, which had already swallowed half the road, into the night full of stars.

CHAPTER 32. The Last Chapter

The Last Chapter

All the rest went up the mountain. They split into groups and went to the homes of the miners. Curdie and his father and mother took Lootie with them. All the way, a light shone on their path. Everyone except Lootie knew where it came from. But when they looked around, they could see nothing of the silvery globe.

For days and days, the water kept rushing from the doors and windows of the king's house. A few dead goblins were swept out into the road.

Curdie saw that something must be done. He spoke to his father and the rest of the miners. They at once began to make another outlet for the water. Everyone worked hard. They dug tunnels here and built there. Soon they succeeded. They also made a little tunnel to drain the water away from under the king's house. Soon they were able to get into the wine cellar. There they found a multitude of dead goblins. Among them was the queen. Her skin-shoe was gone, and the stone one was stuck fast to her ankle. The water had swept away the barricade. That barricade had stopped the men-at-arms from following the goblins. The water had also made the passage much wider. They built it up securely and then went back to their work in the mine.

A good many of the goblins and their creatures escaped from the flood out onto the mountain. But most of them soon left that part of the country. Most of those who stayed became gentler in character. Indeed, they became very much like the Scotch brownies. Their heads became softer, and so did their hearts. Their feet grew harder. Little by little, they became friendly with the people of the mountain and even with the miners. But the miners were merciless to any of the cobs' creatures that came their way. In the end, these creatures all but disappeared.

The rest of the story of The Princess and Curdie must be kept for another book.
