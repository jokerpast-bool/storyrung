CHAPTER 1. Mr. Sherlock Holmes

Mr. Sherlock Holmes

Mr. Sherlock Holmes was usually very late in the mornings. He was only up early on the many nights when he stayed up all night. He was sitting at the breakfast table. I stood on the hearth rug and picked up the stick our visitor had left behind the night before. It was a fine, thick piece of wood with a round, heavy head. It was the kind called a “Penang lawyer.” Just under the head was a wide silver band almost an inch across. “To James Mortimer, M.R.C.S., from his friends of the C.C.H.” was written on it, with the date “1884.” It was just the kind of stick an old-fashioned family doctor used to carry—dignified, solid, and comforting.

“Well, Watson, what do you make of it?”

Holmes was sitting with his back to me. I had given him no sign of what I was doing.

“How did you know what I was doing? I believe you have eyes in the back of your head.”

“I have, at least, a well-polished, silver-plated coffee pot in front of me,” said he. “But tell me, Watson, what do you make of our visitor’s stick? We were so unlucky as to miss him, and we have no idea what his errand was. So this stick left by accident becomes important. Let me hear you rebuild the man by examining it.”

“I think,” said I, following my companion’s methods as far as I could, “that Dr. Mortimer is a successful, older medical man. People think well of him, since those who know him gave him this sign of their appreciation.”

“Good!” said Holmes. “Excellent!”

“I think also that it is likely he is a country doctor who does a great deal of his visiting on foot.”

“Why so?”

“Because this stick was once very handsome, but it has been knocked about so much that I can hardly imagine a town doctor carrying it. The thick iron cap at the bottom is worn down. So it is clear that he has done a great amount of walking with it.”

“Perfectly sound!” said Holmes.

“And then again, there is the ‘friends of the C.C.H.’ I should guess that is the Something Hunt, the local hunt. He has possibly given some surgical help to its members, and it has made him a small gift in return.”

“Really, Watson, you do better than yourself,” said Holmes, pushing back his chair and lighting a cigarette. “I must say that in all the accounts you have been so good as to give of my own small achievements, you have always underrated your own abilities. It may be that you are not yourself bright, but you carry light. Some people without genius have a remarkable power of stirring it up. I confess, my dear fellow, that I owe you a great deal.”

He had never said as much before. I must admit that his words gave me sharp pleasure. I had often been annoyed by his lack of interest in my admiration and in my attempts to make his methods known to the public. I was proud, too, to think that I had learned his system well enough to use it in a way that earned his approval. He now took the stick from my hands and examined it for a few minutes with his bare eyes. Then with an expression of interest he laid down his cigarette. He carried the stick to the window and looked over it again with a curved lens.

“Interesting, though simple,” said he as he returned to his favorite corner of the sofa. “There are certainly one or two signs on the stick. It gives us the basis for several deductions.”

“Has anything escaped me?” I asked with some self-importance. “I trust that there is nothing important which I have overlooked?”

“I am afraid, my dear Watson, that most of your conclusions were wrong. When I said that you stirred me up, I meant, to be frank, that in noticing your mistakes I was sometimes guided toward the truth. Not that you are entirely wrong in this case. The man is certainly a country doctor. And he walks a good deal.”

“Then I was right.”

“To that extent.”

“But that was all.”

“No, no, my dear Watson, not all—by no means all. I would suggest, for example, that a gift to a doctor is more likely to come from a hospital than from a hunt. And when the letters ‘C.C.’ are placed before that hospital, the words ‘Charing Cross’ very naturally come to mind.”

“You may be right.”

“The likelihood lies in that direction. And if we take this as a working idea, we have a fresh basis from which to start our rebuilding of this unknown visitor.”

“Well, then, supposing that ‘C.C.H.’ does stand for ‘Charing Cross Hospital,’ what further inferences may we draw?”

“Do none come to mind? You know my methods. Apply them!”

“I can only think of the obvious conclusion that the man practiced in town before going to the country.”

“I think that we might go a little farther than this. Look at it in this light. On what occasion would such a gift most likely be made? When would his friends unite to give him a sign of their good will? Obviously at the moment when Dr. Mortimer left the service of the hospital in order to start a practice for himself. We know there has been a gift. We believe there has been a change from a town hospital to a country practice. Is it, then, stretching our inference too far to say that the gift was on the occasion of the change?”

“It certainly seems likely.”

“Now, you will observe that he could not have been on the _staff_ of the hospital. Only a man well-established in a London practice could hold such a position, and such a one would not drift into the country. What was he, then? If he was in the hospital and yet not on the staff, he could only have been a house-surgeon or a house-physician—little more than a senior student. And he left five years ago—the date is on the stick. So your serious, middle-aged family doctor vanishes into thin air, my dear Watson. And there emerges a young fellow under thirty, friendly, without ambition, absent-minded, and the owner of a favorite dog. I should describe it roughly as being larger than a terrier and smaller than a mastiff.”

I laughed in disbelief as Sherlock Holmes leaned back in his sofa and blew little wavering rings of smoke up to the ceiling.

“As to the latter part, I have no way of checking you,” said I, “but at least it is not difficult to find out a few details about the man’s age and professional career.” From my small medical shelf I took down the Medical Directory and looked up the name. There were several Mortimers, but only one who could be our visitor. I read his record aloud.

"Mortimer, James, M.R.C.S., 1882, Grimpen, Dartmoor, Devon. House-surgeon, from 1882 to 1884, at Charing Cross Hospital. Winner of the Jackson prize for Comparative Pathology, with essay titled 'Is Disease a Reversion?' Corresponding member of the Swedish Pathological Society. Author of 'Some Freaks of Atavism' (_Lancet_ 1882). 'Do We Progress?' (_Journal of Psychology_, March, 1883). Medical Officer for the areas of Grimpen, Thorsley, and High Barrow."

"No mention of that local hunt, Watson," said Holmes with a light smile. "But a country doctor, as you very smartly observed. I think that I am fairly right in my guesses. As to the describing words, I said, if I remember right, friendly, not ambitious, and forgetful. In my experience, only a friendly man in this world receives such letters of praise. Only a man who is not ambitious gives up a London career for the country. And only a forgetful one leaves his stick and not his visiting-card after waiting an hour in your room."

"And the dog?"

"He has been in the habit of carrying this stick behind his master. The stick is heavy, so the dog has held it tightly by the middle. The marks of his teeth are very clearly visible. The dog's jaw, as shown in the space between these marks, is too broad in my opinion for a terrier and not broad enough for a mastiff. It may have been—yes, by Jove, it _is_ a curly-haired spaniel."

He had risen and paced the room as he spoke. Now he stopped in the window alcove. There was such a ring of certainty in his voice that I glanced up in surprise.

"My dear fellow, how can you possibly be so sure of that?"

"For the very simple reason that I see the dog himself on our very door-step. And there is the ring of its owner. Don't move, I beg you, Watson. He is a professional brother of yours, and your presence may be of help to me. Now is the dramatic moment of fate, Watson. You hear a step upon the stair which is walking into your life, and you know not whether for good or ill. What does Dr. James Mortimer, the man of science, ask of Sherlock Holmes, the specialist in crime? Come in!"

The appearance of our visitor was a surprise to me, since I had expected a typical country doctor. He was a very tall, thin man, with a long nose like a bird's beak. It stuck out between two sharp, grey eyes, set closely together and shining brightly from behind a pair of gold-rimmed glasses. He was dressed in a professional but rather untidy fashion, for his frock-coat was dirty and his trousers worn. Though young, his long back was already bent. He walked with a forward push of his head and a general air of peering kindness. As he entered his eyes fell upon the stick in Holmes's hand, and he ran towards it with a cry of joy. "I am so very glad," said he. "I was not sure whether I had left it here or in the Shipping Office. I would not lose that stick for the world."

"A gift, I see," said Holmes.

"Yes, sir."

"From Charing Cross Hospital?"

"From one or two friends there on the occasion of my marriage."

"Dear, dear, that is bad!" said Holmes, shaking his head.

Dr. Mortimer blinked through his glasses in mild surprise. "Why was it bad?"

"Only that you have disordered our little deductions. Your marriage, you say?"

"Yes, sir. I married, and so left the hospital, and with it all hopes of a consulting practice. It was necessary to make a home of my own."

"Come, come, we are not so far wrong, after all," said Holmes. "And now, Dr. James Mortimer—"

"Mister, sir, Mister—a humble M.R.C.S."

"And a man of exact mind, clearly."

"A dabbler in science, Mr. Holmes, a picker up of shells on the shores of the great unknown ocean. I presume that it is Mr. Sherlock Holmes whom I am addressing and not—"

"No, this is my friend Dr. Watson."

"Glad to meet you, sir. I have heard your name mentioned in connection with that of your friend. You interest me very much, Mr. Holmes. I had hardly expected so dolichocephalic a skull or such well-marked supra-orbital development. Would you have any objection to my running my finger along your parietal fissure? A cast of your skull, sir, until the original is available, would be an ornament to any anthropological museum. It is not my intention to be fulsome, but I confess that I covet your skull."

Sherlock Holmes waved our strange visitor into a chair. "You are an enthusiast in your line of thought, I perceive, sir, as I am in mine," said he. "I observe from your forefinger that you make your own cigarettes. Have no hesitation in lighting one."

The man drew out paper and tobacco and rolled the one up in the other with surprising skill. He had long, shaking fingers as quick and restless as the antennæ of an insect.

Holmes was silent, but his little quick glances showed me the interest which he took in our curious companion. "I presume, sir," said he at last, "that it was not merely for the purpose of examining my skull that you have done me the honour to call here last night and again today?"

"No, sir, no; though I am happy to have had the opportunity of doing that as well. I came to you, Mr. Holmes, because I recognized that I am myself an unpractical man and because I am suddenly faced with a most serious and extraordinary problem. Recognizing, as I do, that you are the second highest expert in Europe—"

"Indeed, sir! May I inquire who has the honour to be the first?" asked Holmes with some sharpness.

"To the man of exactly scientific mind the work of Monsieur Bertillon must always appeal strongly."

"Then had you not better consult him?"

"I said, sir, to the exactly scientific mind. But as a practical man of affairs it is acknowledged that you stand alone. I trust, sir, that I have not inadvertently—"

"Just a little," said Holmes. "I think, Dr. Mortimer, you would do wisely if without more ado you would kindly tell me plainly what the exact nature of the problem is in which you demand my assistance."

CHAPTER 2. The Curse of the Baskervilles

The Curse of the Baskervilles

“I have an old document in my pocket,” said Dr. James Mortimer.

“I saw it when you came into the room,” said Holmes.

“It is a very old document.”

“From the early 1700s, unless it is a fake.”

“How can you say that, sir?”

“You showed me a small part of it while we talked. Even a poor expert can tell the date of a document within about ten years. You may have read my small book on this subject. I gave that date as 1730.”

“The exact date is 1742.” Dr. Mortimer took it from his breast pocket. “Sir Charles Baskerville gave this family paper to me to keep. His sudden and tragic death three months ago caused much excitement in Devonshire. I was his personal friend and also his doctor. He was a strong-minded man, sir. He was smart, practical, and had as little imagination as I do myself. Yet he took this document very seriously. His mind was ready for just such an end as finally came to him.”

Holmes reached out his hand for the document and smoothed it on his knee. “You will see, Watson, the different use of the long _s_ and the short. It is one of several signs that helped me fix the date.”

I looked over his shoulder at the yellow paper and the faded writing. At the top was written: “Baskerville Hall.” Below, in large, rough figures: “1742.”

“It seems to be a statement of some kind.”

“Yes, it is a statement of a certain legend in the Baskerville family.”

“But I understand you want to ask me about something more modern and practical?”

“Most modern. A most practical, pressing matter, which must be decided within twenty-four hours. But the document is short and is closely connected with the affair. With your permission I will read it to you.”

Holmes leaned back in his chair, put his fingertips together, and closed his eyes with a look of acceptance. Dr. Mortimer turned the document to the light and read in a high, cracking voice the following strange, old-world story:

“There have been many statements about the origin of the Hound of the Baskervilles. Yet I come in a direct line from Hugo Baskerville. I had the story from my father, who also had it from his. I have written it down with full belief that it happened even as is here set forth. And I would have you believe, my sons, that the same Justice which punishes sin may also most kindly forgive it. No curse is so heavy that it cannot be removed by prayer and repentance. Learn then from this story not to fear the results of the past, but rather to be careful in the future. Then those foul passions which have caused our family so much pain may not again be let loose to destroy us.

“Know then that in the time of the Great Rebellion (I most strongly urge you to read the history of it by the learned Lord Clarendon) this Manor of Baskerville was held by Hugo of that name. It cannot be denied that he was a most wild, bad, and godless man. In truth, his neighbors might have forgiven this, since good people have never done well in those parts. But there was in him a certain wild and cruel humor which made his name a bad word through the West. It happened that this Hugo came to love (if, indeed, so dark a passion may be known under so bright a name) the daughter of a farmer who held lands near the Baskerville estate. But the young woman was careful and of good repute. She would always avoid him, for she feared his evil name. So it happened that one Michaelmas this Hugo, with five or six of his lazy and bad companions, came down upon the farm and carried off the young woman. Her father and brothers were away from home, as he well knew. When they had brought her to the Hall the young woman was placed in an upper room. Hugo and his friends sat down to a long drinking party, as was their nightly custom. Now the poor girl upstairs was like to have her mind turned by the singing and shouting and terrible oaths which came up to her from below. They say that the words used by Hugo Baskerville, when he was drunk, were such as might strike down the man who said them. At last in the stress of her fear she did that which might have frightened the bravest or most active man. By the aid of the growth of ivy which covered (and still covers) the south wall she came down from under the eaves. So she went homeward across the moor, there being three leagues between the Hall and her father’s farm.

“It happened that some little time later Hugo left his guests to carry food and drink—with other worse things, perhaps—to his captive. So he found the cage empty and the bird escaped. Then, as it would seem, he became as one who has a devil. He rushed down the stairs into the dining-hall and jumped upon the great table, flagons and plates flying before him. He cried aloud before all the company that he would that very night give his body and soul to the Powers of Evil if he might but catch the girl. And while the party-goers stood shocked at the fury of the man, one more bad or, it may be, more drunk than the rest, cried out that they should put the dogs upon her. At that Hugo ran from the house, crying to his grooms that they should saddle his mare and let out the pack of dogs. He gave the dogs a cloth of the girl’s, swung them to the line, and so off full cry in the moonlight over the moor.

“Now, for some space the party-goers stood open-mouthed, unable to understand all that had been done in such haste. But soon their confused minds awoke to the nature of the deed which was like to be done upon the moorlands. Everything was now in an uproar. Some called for their pistols, some for their horses, and some for another bottle of wine. But at length some sense came back to their crazed minds. The whole of them, thirteen in number, took horse and started in pursuit. The moon shone clear above them, and they rode swiftly abreast. They took that course which the girl must needs have taken if she were to reach her own home.

They had gone a mile or two when they passed one of the night shepherds on the moorlands. They called out to him to ask if he had seen the hunt. The man, as the story goes, was so mad with fear that he could hardly speak. But at last he said that he had indeed seen the unhappy maiden, with the dogs following her. 'But I have seen more than that,' said he. 'For Hugo Baskerville passed me on his black horse. And there ran silent behind him such a hell-dog as God should never let come near me.' So the drunken squires cursed the shepherd and rode on. But soon their skin turned cold. For there came a sound of running across the moor. And the black horse, with white foam on its body, went past with loose reins and an empty seat. Then the party rode close together, for a great fear was on them. But they still followed over the moor. Yet each one, if he had been alone, would have been very glad to turn his horse around. Riding slowly in this way they came at last upon the dogs. These dogs, though known for their courage and their kind, were crying in a group at the top of a deep hollow, as we call it, on the moor. Some were moving away. Some, with raised neck hair and staring eyes, were looking down the narrow valley in front of them.

The group had come to a stop, more calm men, as you may guess, than when they started. Most of them would not go forward at all. But three of them, the bravest, or perhaps the most drunken, rode forward down the hollow. Now, it opened into a wide space. In it stood two of those great stones, still to be seen there, which were set by certain forgotten peoples in the days of old. The moon was shining bright on the open space. And there in the middle lay the unhappy girl where she had fallen, dead of fear and of tiredness. But it was not the sight of her body, nor was it the body of Hugo Baskerville lying near her, that made the hair stand up on the heads of these three wild men. It was that, standing over Hugo and pulling at his throat, there stood a foul thing, a great, black beast, shaped like a dog, yet larger than any dog that ever a human eye has rested upon. And even as they looked the thing tore the throat out of Hugo Baskerville. Then it turned its burning eyes and dripping jaws upon them. The three screamed with fear and rode for dear life, still screaming, across the moor. One, it is said, died that very night of what he had seen. And the other two were broken men for the rest of their days.

Such is the tale, my sons, of the coming of the dog which is said to have troubled the family so badly ever since. If I have written it down it is because that which is clearly known has less terror than that which is only hinted at and guessed. Nor can it be denied that many of the family have been unhappy in their deaths, which have been sudden, bloody, and mysterious. Yet may we shelter ourselves in the infinite goodness of Providence, which would not forever punish the innocent beyond that third or fourth generation which is threatened in Holy Writ. To that Providence, my sons, I hereby commend you. And I advise you by way of caution to keep from crossing the moor in those dark hours when the powers of evil are raised high.

[This from Hugo Baskerville to his sons Rodger and John, with instructions that they say nothing of it to their sister Elizabeth.]

When Dr. Mortimer had finished reading this strange story he pushed his glasses up on his forehead and stared across at Mr. Sherlock Holmes. The latter yawned and tossed the end of his cigarette into the fire.

'Well?' said he.

'Do you not find it interesting?'

'To a collector of fairy tales.'

Dr. Mortimer drew a folded newspaper out of his pocket.

'Now, Mr. Holmes, we will give you something a little more recent. This is the _Devon County Chronicle_ of May 14th of this year. It is a short account of the facts brought out at the death of Sir Charles Baskerville which happened a few days before that date.'

My friend leaned a little forward and his expression became intent. Our visitor adjusted his glasses and began:

'The recent sudden death of Sir Charles Baskerville, whose name has been mentioned as the probable Liberal candidate for Mid-Devon at the next election, has cast a gloom over the county. Though Sir Charles had lived at Baskerville Hall for a comparatively short period his friendly character and extreme generosity had won the affection and respect of all who had been brought into contact with him. In these days of _nouveaux riches_ it is refreshing to find a case where the son of an old county family which has fallen on evil days is able to make his own fortune and to bring it back with him to restore the fallen greatness of his line. Sir Charles, as is well known, made large sums of money in South African speculation. More wise than those who go on until the wheel turns against them, he realised his gains and returned to England with them. It is only two years since he took up his residence at Baskerville Hall, and it is common talk how large were those plans of rebuilding and improvement which have been interrupted by his death. Being himself childless, it was his openly expressed desire that the whole countryside should, within his own lifetime, profit by his good fortune. And many will have personal reasons for mourning his untimely end. His generous donations to local and county charities have been frequently recorded in these columns.

'The circumstances connected with the death of Sir Charles cannot be said to have been entirely cleared up by the inquest. But at least enough has been done to put aside those rumours to which local superstition has given rise. There is no reason whatever to suspect foul play, or to imagine that death could be from any but natural causes. Sir Charles was a widower, and a man who may be said to have been in some ways of an eccentric habit of mind. In spite of his considerable wealth he was simple in his personal tastes. And his indoor servants at Baskerville Hall consisted of a married couple named Barrymore, the husband acting as butler and the wife as housekeeper. Their evidence, supported by that of several friends, tends to show that Sir Charles's health has for some time been weakened. And it points especially to some affection of the heart, showing itself in changes of colour, breathlessness, and sharp attacks of nervous depression. Dr. James Mortimer, the friend and medical attendant of the deceased, has given evidence to the same effect.

“The facts of the case are simple. Sir Charles Baskerville had a habit every night before going to bed. He walked down the famous yew alley of Baskerville Hall. The evidence of the Barrymores shows that this had been his custom. On the fourth of May Sir Charles had declared his intention. He would start next day for London. He had ordered Barrymore to prepare his luggage. That night he went out as usual for his night walk. During this walk he had a habit of smoking a cigar. He never returned. At twelve o’clock Barrymore found the hall door still open. He became alarmed. He lit a lantern and went in search of his master. The day had been wet. Sir Charles’s footmarks were easily traced down the alley. Halfway down this walk there is a gate. This gate leads out on to the moor. There were signs that Sir Charles had stood here for some little time. He then went on down the alley. It was at the far end of it that his body was discovered. One fact has not been explained. It is the statement of Barrymore. He said his master’s footprints changed their character after he passed the moor-gate. From that point on he appeared to have been walking on his toes. One Murphy, a gipsy horse-dealer, was on the moor at no great distance at the time. But he appears by his own confession to have been the worse for drink. He declares that he heard cries. But he is unable to state from what direction they came. No signs of violence were found upon Sir Charles’s person. The doctor’s evidence pointed to an almost incredible facial distortion. It was so great that Dr. Mortimer refused at first to believe that it was indeed his friend and patient who lay before him. But it was explained that this is a symptom which is not unusual in cases of difficult breathing and death from heart exhaustion. This explanation was supported by the post-mortem examination. It showed long-standing organic disease. The coroner’s jury returned a verdict in accordance with the medical evidence. It is well that this is so. It is obviously of the utmost importance that Sir Charles’s heir should settle at the Hall. He should continue the good work which has been so sadly interrupted. The prosaic finding of the coroner finally put an end to the romantic stories. These stories have been whispered in connection with the affair. Had it not done so, it might have been difficult to find a tenant for Baskerville Hall. It is understood that the next of kin is Mr. Henry Baskerville, if he is still alive. He is the son of Sir Charles Baskerville’s younger brother. The young man was in America when last heard of. Inquiries are being instituted with a view to informing him of his good fortune.”

Dr. Mortimer refolded his paper and replaced it in his pocket. “Those are the public facts, Mr. Holmes, in connection with the death of Sir Charles Baskerville.”

“I must thank you,” said Sherlock Holmes, “for calling my attention to a case which certainly presents some features of interest. I had observed some newspaper comment at the time. But I was exceedingly preoccupied by that little affair of the Vatican cameos. In my anxiety to oblige the Pope I lost touch with several interesting English cases. This article, you say, contains all the public facts?”

“It does.”

“Then let me have the private ones.” He leaned back, put his finger-tips together, and assumed his most impassive and judicial expression.

“In doing so,” said Dr. Mortimer, who had begun to show signs of some strong emotion, “I am telling that which I have not confided to anyone. My motive for withholding it from the coroner’s inquiry is this. A man of science shrinks from placing himself in the public position of seeming to indorse a popular superstition. I had the further motive that Baskerville Hall, as the paper says, would certainly remain untenanted. That would happen if anything were done to increase its already rather grim reputation. For both these reasons I thought that I was justified in telling rather less than I knew. No practical good could result from it. But with you there is no reason why I should not be perfectly frank.

“The moor is very sparsely inhabited. Those who live near each other are thrown very much together. For this reason I saw a good deal of Sir Charles Baskerville. There is Mr. Frankland, of Lafter Hall, and Mr. Stapleton, the naturalist. Apart from them, there are no other men of education within many miles. Sir Charles was a retiring man. But the chance of his illness brought us together. A community of interests in science kept us so. He had brought back much scientific information from South Africa. Many a charming evening we have spent together discussing the comparative anatomy of the Bushman and the Hottentot.

“Within the last few months it became increasingly plain to me that Sir Charles’s nervous system was strained to the breaking point. He had taken this legend which I have read you exceedingly to heart. He took it so much to heart that nothing would induce him to go out upon the moor at night. He would still walk in his own grounds. Incredible as it may appear to you, Mr. Holmes, he was honestly convinced that a dreadful fate overhung his family. Certainly the records which he was able to give of his ancestors were not encouraging. The idea of some ghastly presence constantly haunted him. On more than one occasion he has asked me a question. Had I on my medical journeys at night ever seen any strange creature? Or had I heard the baying of a hound? He put the latter question to me several times. He always put it with a voice which vibrated with excitement.

“I can well remember driving up to his house in the evening some three weeks before the fatal event. He chanced to be at his hall door. I had descended from my gig and was standing in front of him. Then I saw his eyes fix themselves over my shoulder. He stared past me with an expression of the most dreadful horror. I whisked round. I had just time to catch a glimpse of something. I took it to be a large black calf passing at the head of the drive. He was so excited and alarmed that I was compelled to go down to the spot where the animal had been. I had to look around for it. It was gone, however. The incident appeared to make the worst impression upon his mind. I stayed with him all the evening. It was on that occasion that he confided to my keeping that narrative which I read to you when first I came. He did this to explain the emotion which he had shown. I mention this small episode because it assumes some importance in view of the tragedy which followed. But I was convinced at the time that the matter was entirely trivial. I was also convinced that his excitement had no justification.

"It was because of my advice that Sir Charles was about to go to London. I knew his heart was weak. He lived with constant worry, and although the cause of it might not be real, it was clearly having a serious effect on his health. I thought that a few months among the busy pleasures of town would send him back a new man. Mr. Stapleton, a friend of us both who was much worried about his health, felt the same way. At the very last moment came this terrible disaster.

"On the night of Sir Charles's death Barrymore the butler, who found the body, sent Perkins the groom on a horse to me. I was sitting up late, so I was able to reach Baskerville Hall within an hour of the event. I checked and confirmed all the facts that were mentioned at the official inquiry. I followed the steps down the yew alley. I saw the spot at the gate to the open land where he seemed to have waited. I noticed the change in the shape of the marks after that point. I noted that there were no other steps except those of Barrymore on the soft gravel. And finally I carefully examined the body, which had not been touched until my arrival. Sir Charles lay on his face, his arms out, his fingers dug into the ground. His face was twisted by some strong feeling so much that I could hardly have sworn it was him. There was certainly no physical injury of any kind. But Barrymore made one false statement at the official inquiry. He said that there were no marks upon the ground around the body. He did not see any. But I did—some small distance away, but fresh and clear."

"Prints of feet?"

"Prints of feet."

"A man's or a woman's?"

Dr. Mortimer looked at us in a strange way for a moment, and his voice sank almost to a whisper as he answered.

"Mr. Holmes, they were the prints of feet of a very large hound!"
