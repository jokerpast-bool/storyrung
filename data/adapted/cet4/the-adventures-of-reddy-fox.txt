THE ADVENTURES OF REDDY FOX

By Thornton W. Burgess

I. Granny Fox Gives Reddy a Scare

Reddy Fox lived with Granny Fox. You see, Reddy was one of a large family. The family was so large that Mother Fox had hard work to feed so many hungry little mouths. So she had let Reddy go to live with old Granny Fox. Granny Fox was the wisest, most clever, smartest fox in all the country round. Now that Reddy had grown so big, she thought it was about time that he began to learn the things that every fox should know. So every day she took him hunting with her. She taught him all the things that she had learned about hunting. She taught him how to steal Farmer Brown's chickens without waking Bowser the Hound. She also taught him all about the thousand and one ways of fooling a dog which she had learned.

This morning Granny Fox had taken Reddy across the Green Meadows, up through the Green Forest, and over to the railroad track. Reddy had never been there before. He did not know just what to make of it. Granny ran ahead until they came to a long bridge. Then she stopped.

“Come here, Reddy, and look down,” she commanded.

Reddy did as he was told. But a glance down made him dizzy. He was so dizzy that he nearly fell. Granny Fox smiled.

“Come across,” said she, and ran lightly across to the other side.

But Reddy Fox was afraid. Yes, Sir, he was afraid to take one step on the long bridge. He was afraid that he would fall through into the water or onto the hard rocks below. Granny Fox ran back to where Reddy sat.

“For shame, Reddy Fox!” said she. “What are you afraid of? Just do not look down and you will be safe enough. Now come along over with me.”

But Reddy Fox held back. He begged to go home and made small sad sounds. Suddenly Granny Fox jumped to her feet, as if in great fear. “Bowser the Hound! Come, Reddy, come!” she cried. She started across the bridge as fast as she could go.

Reddy did not stop to look or to think. His one idea was to get away from Bowser the Hound. “Wait, Granny! Wait!” he cried. He started after her as fast as he could run. He was in the middle of the bridge before he remembered it at all. When he was at last safely across, he found old Granny Fox sitting down laughing at him. Then for the first time Reddy looked behind him to see where Bowser the Hound might be. He was nowhere to be seen. Could he have fallen off the bridge?

“Where is Bowser the Hound?” cried Reddy.

“Home in Farmer Brown's yard,” replied Granny Fox in a flat voice. Reddy stared at her for a minute. Then he began to understand that Granny Fox had simply scared him into running across the bridge. Reddy felt very small, very small indeed. “Now we will run back again,” said Granny Fox. And this time Reddy did.

II. Granny Shows Reddy a Trick

Every day Granny Fox led Reddy Fox over to the long railroad bridge. She made him run back and forth across it until he had no fear of it at all. At first it had made him dizzy. But now he could run across at the top of his speed and not mind it in the least. “I do not see what good it does to be able to run across a bridge; anyone can do that!” said Reddy one day.

Granny Fox smiled. “Do you remember the first time you tried to do it?” she asked.

Reddy hung his head. Of course he remembered. He remembered that Granny had had to scare him into crossing that first time.

Suddenly Granny Fox lifted her head. “Listen!” she said.

Reddy raised his sharp, pointed ears. Way off back, in the direction from which they had come, they heard the long call of a dog. It was not the voice of Bowser the Hound but of a younger dog. Granny listened for a few minutes. The voice of the dog grew louder as it came nearer.

“He certainly is following our track,” said Granny Fox. “Now, Reddy, you run across the bridge and watch from the top of the little hill over there. Perhaps I can show you a trick that will teach you why I have made you learn to run across the bridge.”

Reddy ran across the long bridge and up to the top of the hill, as Granny had told him to. Then he sat down to watch. Granny ran out into the middle of a field and sat down. Pretty soon a young dog broke out of the low plants, his nose in Granny's track. Then he looked up and saw her. His voice grew still more wild and eager. Granny Fox started to run as soon as she was sure that the dog had seen her. But she did not run very fast. Reddy did not know what to make of it. Granny seemed simply to be playing with the dog and not really trying to get away from him at all. Pretty soon Reddy heard another sound. It was a long, low rolling sound. Then there was a distant whistle. It was a train.

Granny heard it, too. As she ran, she began to work back toward the long bridge. The train was in sight now. Suddenly Granny Fox started across the bridge so fast that she looked like a little red line. The dog was close behind her when she started. He was so eager to catch her that he did not see either the bridge or the train. But he could not begin to run as fast as Granny Fox. Oh, my, no! When she had reached the other side, he was not halfway across. Right behind him, whistling for him to get out of the way, was the train.

The dog gave one frightened cry. Then he did the only thing he could do. He jumped down, down into the fast water below. The last Reddy saw of him, he was wildly trying to swim to the shore.

“Now you know why I wanted you to learn to cross a bridge; it is a very nice way of getting rid of dogs,” said Granny Fox, as she climbed up beside Reddy.

III. Bowser the Hound Is Not Fooled

Reddy Fox had been taught so much by Granny Fox that he began to feel very wise and very important. Reddy is naturally smart. He had been very quick to learn the tricks that old Granny Fox had taught him. But Reddy Fox likes to talk big. Every day he walked about proudly on the Green Meadows and said how smart he was. Blacky the Crow grew tired of Reddy's big talk.

“If you are so smart, what is the reason you always keep out of sight of Bowser the Hound?” asked Blacky. “For my part, I do not believe that you are smart enough to fool him.”

Many little animals of the meadow heard Blacky say this. Reddy knew it. He also knew that if he did not prove Blacky wrong, everyone would laugh at him forever. Suddenly he remembered the trick that Granny Fox had played on the young dog at the railroad bridge. Why not play the same trick on Bowser and invite Blacky the Crow to watch? He would do it.

“If you come to the railroad bridge when the train comes this afternoon, I'll show you how easy it is to fool Bowser the Hound,” said Reddy.

Blacky agreed to be there. Reddy went off to find out where Bowser was. Blacky told everyone he met how Reddy Fox had promised to fool Bowser the Hound. Every time he told it, he laughed as if he thought it was the best joke ever.

Blacky the Crow was there on time that afternoon. His cousin, Sammy Jay, came with him. Soon they saw Reddy Fox running across the fields. Behind him came Bowser the Hound, barking loudly. Just as old Granny Fox had done with the young dog, Reddy let Bowser get very near him. Then the train came roaring along. Reddy ran across the long bridge just ahead of it. He had thought Bowser would be so focused on catching him that he would not notice the train until he was on the bridge. Then it would be too late, as it had been for the young dog. Then Bowser would have to jump down into the fast river or be run over. As soon as Reddy was across the bridge, he jumped off the track and turned to see what would happen to Bowser the Hound. The train was halfway across the bridge, but Bowser was nowhere to be seen. He must have jumped already. Reddy sat down and smiled in the most self-satisfied way.

The long train roared past. Reddy closed his eyes to shut out the dust and smoke. When he opened them again, he looked right into the wide-open mouth of Bowser the Hound. Bowser was not ten feet away.

“Did you think you could fool me with that old trick?” roared Bowser.

Reddy did not stop to answer. He just started off at top speed, a very frightened little fox.

You see, Bowser the Hound knew all about that trick. He had just waited until the train had passed. Then he had run across the bridge right behind it.

Reddy Fox was out of breath and tired. He ran to seek the aid of Granny Fox in getting rid of Bowser the Hound. As he ran, he heard a sound that made him grind his teeth.

“Haw, haw, haw! How smart we are!”

It was Blacky the Crow.

IV. Reddy Fox Grows Bold

Reddy Fox was growing bold. Everybody said so, and what everybody says must be so. Reddy Fox had always been very sly and not bold at all. The truth is Reddy Fox had fooled Bowser the Hound and Farmer Brown's boy so many times that he had begun to think himself very smart indeed. He had really fooled himself. Yes, Sir, Reddy Fox had fooled himself. He thought himself so smart that nobody could fool him.

Now it is one of the worst habits in the world to think too much of yourself. And Reddy Fox had the habit. Oh, my, yes! Reddy Fox certainly did have the habit! When anyone mentioned Bowser the Hound, Reddy would turn up his nose and say: “Pooh! It's the easiest thing in the world to fool him.”

You see, he had forgotten all about the time Bowser had fooled him at the railroad bridge.

Whenever Reddy saw Farmer Brown's boy, he would say with the greatest scorn: “Who's afraid of him? Not I!”

Reddy Fox thought more and more of his own smartness. He grew bolder and bolder. Almost every night he visited Farmer Brown's henyard. Farmer Brown set traps all around the yard. But Reddy always found them and kept out of them. It got so that Unc' Billy Possum and Jimmy Skunk did not dare go to the henhouse for eggs any more. They were afraid they would get into one of the traps set for Reddy Fox. Of course they missed those fresh eggs. Of course they blamed Reddy Fox.

“Never mind,” said Jimmy Skunk, scowling down on the Green Meadows where Reddy Fox was taking a sun bath. “Farmer Brown's boy will get him yet! I hope he does!” Jimmy said this a little spitefully and just as if he really meant it.

Now when people think that they are very, very smart, they like to show off. You know it is not any fun at all to feel smart unless others can see how smart you are. So Reddy Fox, just to show off, grew very bold, very bold indeed. He actually went up to Farmer Brown's henyard in broad daylight. Almost under the nose of Bowser the Hound, he caught the pet chicken of Farmer Brown's boy. Ol' Mistah Buzzard was sailing overhead high up in the blue, blue sky. He saw Reddy Fox and shook his bald head:

“Ah see Trouble on the way; Yes, Ah do! Yes, Ah do! Hope it ain't a-gwine to stay; Yes, Ah do! Yes, Ah do! Trouble am a spry ol' man, Bound to find yo' if he can; If he finds yo' bound to stick. When Ah sees him, Ah runs quick! Yes, Ah do! Yes, Ah do!”

But Reddy Fox thought himself so smart that it seemed as if he really were hunting for Ol' Mr. Trouble. And when he caught the pet chicken of Farmer Brown's boy, Ol' Mr. Trouble was right at his heels.

V. Reddy Grows Careless

Ol' Mistah Buzzard was right. Trouble was right at the heels of Reddy Fox, although Reddy would not have believed it if he had been told. He had stolen that plump pet chicken of Farmer Brown's boy for no reason under the sun but to show off. He wanted everyone to know how bold he was. He thought himself so smart that he could do just exactly what he pleased and no one could stop him. He liked to strut around through the Green Forest and over the Green Meadows. He liked to brag about what he had done and what he could do.

Now people who brag and boast and who like to show off are almost sure to come to grief. And when they do, very few people are sorry for them. None of the little meadow and forest people liked Reddy Fox, anyway. They were getting so tired of his boasting that they just ached to see him get into trouble. Yes, Sir, they just ached to see Reddy get into trouble.

Peter Rabbit, happy-go-lucky Peter Rabbit, shook his head gravely when he heard how Reddy had stolen that pet chicken of Farmer Brown's boy. Reddy was boasting about it to everyone.

“Reddy Fox is getting so proud that soon he will not be able to see his own feet,” said Peter Rabbit.

“Well, what if he does not?” asked Jimmy Skunk.

Peter looked at Jimmy with strong dislike:

“He comes to harm, however fast, Who does not watch his flying feet.

“Jimmy Skunk, if you did not have that little bag of smell that everybody is afraid of, you would be much more careful where you step,” replied Peter. “If Reddy does not watch out, someday he will step right into a trap.”

Jimmy Skunk laughed quietly. “I wish he would!” said he.

Now when Farmer Brown's boy heard about the boldness of Reddy Fox, he shut his mouth tight in a way that was ugly to see and reached for his gun. “I cannot afford to raise chickens to feed foxes!” said he. Then he called Bowser the Hound with a whistle, and together they started out. It was not long before Bowser found Reddy's tracks.

“Bow, wow, wow, wow!” cried Bowser the Hound in a loud voice.

Reddy Fox was sleeping on the edge of the Green Forest. He heard Bowser's big, deep voice. He raised his ears, then he smiled. “I feel just like a good run today,” said he, and ran off along the Crooked Little Path down the hill.

Now this was a beautiful summer day and Reddy knew that in summer men and boys rarely hunt foxes. “It is only Bowser the Hound,” thought Reddy, “and when I have had a good run, I will play a trick on him so that he will lose my track.” So Reddy did not use his eyes as he should have done. You see, he thought himself so clever that he had grown careless. Yes, Sir, Reddy Fox had grown careless. He kept looking back to see where Bowser the Hound was, but did not look around to make sure that no other danger was near.

Ol' Mistah Buzzard, sailing round and round, way up in the blue, blue sky, could see everything going on down below. He could see Reddy Fox running along the edge of the Green Forest and every few minutes stopping to laugh quietly and listen to Bowser the Hound trying to pick out the trail Reddy had made so hard to follow by his twists and turns. And he saw something else, did Ol' Mistah Buzzard. It looked to him very much like the barrel of a gun sticking out from behind an old tree just ahead of Reddy.

“Ah reckon it is just like Ah said: Reddy Fox is gwine to meet trouble right smart soon,” said Ol' Mistah Buzzard in a low voice.

VI. Drummer the Woodpecker Drums in Vain

Once upon a time, before he had grown to think himself so very, very clever, Reddy Fox would never, never have thought of running without watching out in every direction. He would have seen that thing that looked like the barrel of a gun sticking out from behind the old tree toward which he was running, and he would have been very suspicious, very suspicious indeed. But now all Reddy could think of was what a wonderful chance he had to show all the little meadow and forest people what a bold, clever fellow he was.

So once more Reddy sat down and waited until Bowser the Hound was almost up to him. Just then Drummer the Woodpecker began to make a very loud noise--rat-a-tat-tat-tat, rat-a-tat-tat-tat, rat-a-tat-tat-tat! Now everybody who heard that rat-a-tat-tat-tat knew that it was a danger signal. Drummer the Woodpecker never drums just that way for pleasure. But Reddy Fox paid no attention to it. He did not notice it at all. You see, he was so full of the idea of his own cleverness that he did not have room for anything else.

“Stupid thing!” said Drummer the Woodpecker to himself. “I do not know what I am trying to warn him for, anyway. The Green Meadows and the Green Forest would be better off without him, a lot better off! Nobody likes him. He is a terrible bully and is all the time trying to catch or scare to death those who are smaller than he. Still, he is so handsome!” Drummer turned his head to one side and looked over at Reddy Fox.

Reddy was laughing to see how hard Bowser the Hound was working to untangle Reddy's mixed-up trail.

“Yes, Sir, he certainly is handsome,” said Drummer once more.

Then he looked down at the foot of the old tree on which he was sitting, and what he saw caused Drummer to make up his mind. “I surely would miss seeing that beautiful red coat of his! I surely would!” he said in a low voice. “If he does not hear and heed now, it will not be my fault!” Then Drummer the Woodpecker began such a furious rat-a-tat-tat-tat on the trunk of the old tree that it rang through the Green Forest and out across the Green Meadows almost to the Purple Hills.

Down at the foot of the tree a freckled face on which there was a black scowl looked up. It was the face of Farmer Brown's boy.

“What ails that pesky woodpecker?” he said in a low voice. “If he does not keep still, he will scare that fox!”

He shook a fist at Drummer, but Drummer did not appear to notice. He kept right on, rat-a-tat-tat-tat, rat-a-tat-tat-tat, rat-a-tat-tat-tat!

VII. Too Late Reddy Fox Hears

Drummer the Woodpecker was pounding out his danger signal so fast and so hard that his red head flew back and forth almost too fast to see. Rat-a-tat-tat-a-tat-tat, beat Drummer on the old tree trunk on the edge of the Green Forest. When he stopped for breath, he looked down into the scowling face of Farmer Brown's boy, who was hiding behind the old tree trunk.

Drummer did not like the looks of that scowl, not a bit. And he did not like the looks of the gun which Farmer Brown's boy had. He knew that Farmer Brown's boy was hiding there to shoot Reddy Fox, but Drummer was beginning to be afraid that Farmer Brown's boy might guess what all that drumming meant--that it was a warning to Reddy Fox. And if Farmer Brown's boy did guess that, why--why--anyway, on the other side of the tree there was a better place to drum. So Drummer the Woodpecker crept around to the other side of the tree and in a minute was drumming harder than ever. Whenever he stopped for breath, he looked out over the Green Meadows to see if Reddy Fox had heard his warning.

But if Reddy had heard, he had not heeded. Just to show off before all the little meadow and forest people, Reddy had waited until Bowser the Hound had almost reached him. Then, with a saucy flirt of his tail, Reddy Fox started to show how fast he could run, and that is very fast indeed. It made Bowser the Hound seem very slow, as, with his nose to the ground, he came racing after Reddy, making a very loud noise with his great voice.

Now Reddy Fox had grown as careless as he had grown bold. Instead of looking sharply ahead, he looked this way and that way to see who was watching and admiring him. So he took no note of where he was going. He started straight for the old tree trunk where Drummer the Woodpecker was pounding out his warning of danger.

Now Reddy Fox has sharp eyes and very quick ears. My, my, indeed he has! But just now Reddy was as deaf as if he had cotton stuffed in his ears. He was laughing to himself to think how he was going to fool Bowser the Hound. He was thinking how smart everyone would think him. Then all of a sudden, he heard the rat-a-tat-tata-tat-tat of Drummer the Woodpecker. He knew that that meant "Danger!"

For just a very little second it seemed to Reddy Fox that his heart stopped beating. He could not stop running, because he had let Bowser the Hound get too close for that. Reddy's sharp eyes saw Drummer the Woodpecker near the top of the old tree trunk. He noticed that Drummer seemed to be looking at something down below. Reddy Fox gave one quick look at the foot of the old tree trunk. He saw a gun pointed at him. Behind the gun was the face of Farmer Brown's boy, covered with small brown spots. Reddy Fox gave a little quick breath of fear and turned so suddenly that he almost fell flat. Then he began to run as never in his life had he run before. It seemed as though his flying feet hardly touched the grass. His eyes were popping out with fear. With every jump he tried to run just a little bit faster.

Bang! Bang! Two flashes of fire and two clouds of smoke came from behind the old tree trunk. Drummer the Woodpecker gave a frightened scream and flew deep into the Green Forest. Peter Rabbit pressed himself flat under a friendly thorny bush. Johnny Chuck jumped headfirst down his doorway.

Reddy Fox gave a short, high cry of pain, and suddenly began to go lame. But Farmer Brown's boy did not know that. He thought he had missed. He said to himself in a low, angry voice:

"I'll get that fox yet for stealing my pet chicken!"

VIII. Granny Fox Takes Care of Reddy

Reddy Fox was so sore and lame that he could hardly walk with difficulty. He had had the hardest kind of work to get far enough ahead of Bowser the Hound. He had to mix his trail up so that Bowser could not follow it. Then he had limped home, big tears running down his nose, although he tried hard not to cry. "Oh! Oh! Oh!" moaned Reddy Fox, as he came in slowly at the doorway of his home.

"What's the matter now?" said old Granny Fox sharply. She had just waked up from a sun nap.

"I--I've got hurt," said Reddy Fox, and began to cry harder. Granny Fox looked at Reddy sharply. "What have you been doing now--tearing your clothes on a wire fence with sharp points, or trying to crawl through a thorny thicket? I should think you were big enough by this time to look out for yourself!" said Granny Fox in an angry way, as she came over to look at Reddy's hurts.

"Please do not scold, please do not, Granny Fox," begged Reddy. He was beginning to feel sick to his stomach as well as lame, and to smart dreadfully.

Granny Fox took one look at Reddy's wounds, and knew right away what had happened. She made Reddy stretch himself out at full length. Then she went to work on him, washing his wounds with the greatest care and binding them up. She was very gentle, was old Granny Fox, as she touched the sore places. But all the time she was at work her tongue flew, and that was not gentle at all. Oh, my, no! There was nothing gentle about that!

You see, old Granny Fox is wise and very, very sharp and shrewd. Just as soon as she saw Reddy's hurts, she knew that they were made by shot from a gun. That meant that Reddy Fox had been careless. Or he never, never would have been where he was in danger of being shot.

"I hope this will teach you a lesson!" said Granny Fox. "What are your eyes and your ears and your nose for? To keep you out of just such trouble as this.

"A little Fox must use his eyes Or get someday a sad surprise.

"A little Fox must use his ears And know what makes each sound he hears.

"A little Fox must use his nose And try the wind where'er he goes.

"A little Fox must use all three To live to grow as old as me.

"Now tell me all about it, Reddy Fox. This is summer and men do not hunt foxes now. I do not see how it happens that Farmer Brown's boy was waiting for you with a gun."

So Reddy Fox told Granny Fox all about how he had run too near the old tree trunk. Farmer Brown's boy had been hiding behind it. But Reddy did not tell how he had been trying to show off. He did not tell how in broad daylight he had stolen the pet chicken of Farmer Brown's boy. You may be sure he was very careful not to mention that.

And so old Granny Fox pulled her brows together and thought and thought. She was trying to find some good reason why Farmer Brown's boy should have been hunting in the summertime.

"Caw, caw, caw!" shouted Blacky the Crow.

The face of Granny Fox cleared. "Blacky the Crow has been stealing, and Farmer Brown's boy was out after him when Reddy came along," said Granny Fox, talking out loud to herself.

Reddy Fox grew very red in the face, but he never said a word.

IX. Peter Rabbit Hears the News

Johnny Chuck came running up to the edge of the Old Briarpatch quite out of breath. You see, he is so round and fat and roly-poly that to run makes him puff and blow. Johnny Chuck's eyes danced with excitement as he looked into the Old Briar-patch, trying to see Peter Rabbit.

"Peter! Peter Rabbit! Oh, Peter!" he called. No one answered. Johnny Chuck looked disappointed. It was the middle of the morning, and he had thought that Peter would surely be at home then. He would try once more. "Oh, you Peter Rabbit!" he shouted in such a high voice that it was almost a squeal.

"What you want?" asked a sleepy voice from the middle of the Old Briar-patch.

Johnny Chuck's face lighted up. "Come out here, Peter, where I can look at you," cried Johnny.

"Go away, Johnny Chuck! I'm sleepy," said Peter Rabbit. His voice sounded just a little bit cross, for Peter had been out all night, a habit which Peter has.

"I've got some news for you, Peter," called Johnny Chuck eagerly.

"How do you know it's news to me?" asked Peter. Johnny noticed that his voice was not quite so cross.

“I am almost sure it is, because I just heard it myself. I hurried right down here to tell you because I think you will want to know it,” replied Johnny Chuck.

“Pooh!” said Peter Rabbit. “It is probably very old news to me. You people who go to bed with the sun do not hear the news until it is old. What is it?”

“It is about Reddy Fox,” began Johnny Chuck, but Peter Rabbit stopped him.

“Shucks, Johnny Chuck! You are slow! Why, everyone in Green Meadows heard last night how Farmer Brown's boy shot Reddy Fox!” said Peter Rabbit with a laugh. “That is no news. And here you woke me up to tell me something I knew before you went to bed last night! Reddy Fox deserves it. I hope he will be lame for a week,” added Peter Rabbit.

“He cannot walk at all!” cried Johnny Chuck with joy, sure now that Peter Rabbit had not heard the news.

“What is that?” asked Peter, and Johnny Chuck could hear him begin to jump along one of his little private paths in the heart of the Old Briar-patch. He knew now that Peter Rabbit wanted to know more, and he smiled to himself.

In a few minutes Peter put a sleepy face out from the Old Briar-patch and smiled in a shy way. “What was that you were saying about Reddy Fox?” he asked again.

“I have a good mind not to tell you, Mr. Know-it-all,” said Johnny Chuck.

“Oh, please, Johnny Chuck,” begged Peter Rabbit.

Finally Johnny gave in. “I said that Reddy Fox cannot walk. Are you not glad, Peter?”

“How do you know?” asked Peter, because Peter is very unsure about Reddy Fox, and has to watch out for his tricks all the time.

“Jimmy Skunk told me. He was up by Reddy's house early this morning and saw Reddy try to walk. He tried and tried and could not. You will not have to watch out for Reddy Fox for some time, Peter. He deserves it, does he not?”

“Let us go up and see if it really is true!” said Peter suddenly.

“All right,” said Johnny Chuck, and off they started.

X. Poor Reddy Fox

Peter Rabbit and Johnny Chuck moved quietly up the hill toward the home of Reddy Fox. As they came near, they moved slowly from one bunch of grass to another and from one small tree to another, stopping behind each to look and listen. They were not taking any chances. Johnny Chuck was not much afraid of Reddy Fox, because he had beaten him once, but he was afraid of old Granny Fox. Peter Rabbit was afraid of both. The nearer he got to the home of Reddy Fox, the more worried and nervous he grew. You see, Reddy Fox had played so many tricks to try and catch Peter that Peter was not quite sure that this was not another trick. So he watched closely in every direction, ready to run at the least sign of danger.

When they had moved quietly and slowly to a point where they could see the doorstep of the Fox home, Peter Rabbit and Johnny Chuck lay down in a group of small trees and watched. Pretty soon they saw old Granny Fox come out. She smelled the wind and then she started off at a quick run down the Lone Little Path. Johnny Chuck gave a sigh of relief, because he was not afraid of Reddy and now he felt safe. But Peter Rabbit was just as watchful as ever.

“I have to see Reddy for myself before I will go a step nearer,” he whispered. Just then Johnny Chuck put a hand on his lips and pointed with the other hand. There was Reddy Fox moving slowly out of his doorway into the sun. Peter Rabbit leaned forward to see better. Was Reddy Fox really so badly hurt, or was he only pretending?

Reddy Fox moved slowly and with pain out onto his doorstep. He tried to stand and walk, but he could not because he was too stiff and sore. So he just moved slowly. He did not know that anyone was watching him, and with every movement he made a face. That was because it hurt so.

Peter Rabbit, watching from the group of small trees, knew then that Reddy was not pretending. He knew that he had nothing, not the least little thing, to fear from Reddy Fox. So Peter gave a loud cry of joy and sprang out into view.

Reddy looked up and tried to grin, but made a face of pain instead. You see, it hurt so to move.

“I suppose you are very happy to see me like this,” he said in a low angry voice to Peter Rabbit.

Now Peter had every reason to be glad, because Reddy Fox had tried his best to catch Peter Rabbit to give to old Granny Fox for her dinner, and time and again Peter had just barely escaped. So at first Peter Rabbit had cried out with joy. But as he saw how very helpless Reddy really was and how much pain he felt, suddenly Peter Rabbit's big, soft eyes filled with tears of pity.

He forgot all about the threats of Reddy Fox and how Reddy had tried to trick him. He forgot all about how mean Reddy had been.

“Poor Reddy Fox,” said Peter Rabbit. “Poor Reddy Fox.”

XI. Granny Fox Returns

Up over the hill ran old Granny Fox. She was on her way home with a tender young chicken for Reddy Fox. Poor Reddy! Of course, it was his own fault, because he had been showing off and he had been careless or he never would have gone so near to the old tree trunk behind which Farmer Brown's boy was hiding.

But old Granny Fox did not know this. She never makes such mistakes herself. Oh, my, no! So now, as she came up over the hill to a place where she could see her home, she laid the chicken down and then she moved slowly behind a little small tree and looked all over the Green Meadows to see if the way was clear. She knew that Bowser the Hound was chained up. She had seen Farmer Brown and Farmer Brown's boy working in the cornfield, so she had nothing to fear from them.

Looking over to her doorstep, she saw Reddy Fox lying in the sun, and then she saw something else, something that made her eyes flash and her teeth come together with a snap. It was Peter Rabbit sitting up very straight, not ten feet from Reddy Fox.

“So that is that young scamp of a Peter Rabbit whom Reddy was going to catch for me when I was sick and could not! I will just show Reddy Fox how easily it can be done, and he shall have tender young rabbit with his chicken!” said Granny Fox to herself.

So first she studied and studied every bunch of grass and every small tree behind which she could move slowly. She saw that she could get almost to where Peter Rabbit was sitting and never once show herself to him. Then she looked this way and looked that way to make sure that no one was watching her.

She saw no one on the Green Meadows who was looking her way. Then Granny Fox began to crawl from one small group of grass to another. She moved from one low plant to another. Sometimes she moved along flat on her stomach. Little by little she was coming nearer and nearer to Peter Rabbit.

"If it's hot or if it's cold, Jenny Wren will always scold. From morning till night the whole day long Her flexible tongue is going strong.

“I am going to find out what it means,” said Reddy, talking to himself.

Reddy Fox put his head out and looked straight into the freckled face of Farmer Brown's boy and the mouth of that terrible gun!

XXII. Ol' Mistah Buzzard's Keen Sight

Old Granny Fox had thought that when she tricked Bowser the Hound up in the old field on the edge of the mountain, she could take her time going home. She was tired and hot. She had planned to pick the most shaded paths going back. She had thought that Farmer Brown's boy would soon join Bowser the Hound. Bowser had made such a big noise about finding the old house into which Granny Fox had run.

But Farmer Brown's boy had not yet appeared. Granny Fox was getting worried. Could it be that he had not followed Bowser the Hound, after all? Granny Fox went out on a high point and looked. She could see nothing of Farmer Brown's boy and his gun. Just then Ol' Mistah Buzzard came sailing down out of the blue, blue sky. He settled himself on a tall, dead tree. Granny Fox had not forgotten how Ol' Mistah Buzzard had warned Peter Rabbit just as she was about to jump on him. But she suddenly thought that Ol' Mistah Buzzard might be of use to her.

So old Granny Fox smoothed out her skirts and walked over to the foot of the tree where Ol' Mistah Buzzard sat.

“How are you today, neighbor Buzzard?” asked Granny Fox, smiling up at Ol' Mistah Buzzard.

“Ah'm so as to be up and about, thank yo',” replied Ol' Mistah Buzzard. He spread his wings out so that air could blow under them.

“My!” cried old Granny Fox. “What fine great wings you have, Mistah Buzzard! It must be grand to be able to fly. I suppose you can see a great deal from way up there in the blue, blue sky, Mistah Buzzard.”

Ol' Mistah Buzzard felt pleased. “Yes,” said he. “Ah can see all that's going on on the Green Meadows and in the Green Forest.”

“Oh, Mistah Buzzard, you do not really mean that!” cried old Granny Fox. She spoke just as if she wanted to believe it, but could not.

“Yes, Ah can!” replied Ol' Mistah Buzzard.

“Really, Mistah Buzzard? Really? Oh, I can't believe that your eyes are so sharp as all that! Now I know where Bowser the Hound is and where Farmer Brown's boy is. But I do not believe you can see them,” said Granny Fox.

Ol' Mistah Buzzard never said a word. He spread his broad wings and in a few minutes he had sailed up, up, up. He looked like just a tiny dot to old Granny Fox. Now old Granny Fox had not told the truth when she said she knew where Farmer Brown's boy was. She thought she would trick Ol' Mistah Buzzard into telling her.

In a few minutes down came Ol' Mistah Buzzard. “Bowser the Hound is up in the old back field,” said he.

“Right!” cried old Granny Fox, clapping her hands. “And where is Farmer Brown's boy?”

“Farmer Brown's boy is...” Ol' Mistah Buzzard paused.

“Where? Where?” asked Granny Fox. She asked so eagerly that Ol' Mistah Buzzard looked at her sharply.

“Yo' said you knew, so what's the use of telling yo'?” said Ol' Mistah Buzzard. Then he added: “But if Ah was yo', Ah cert'nly would get home right fast soon.”

“Why? Do, do tell me what you saw, Mistah Buzzard!” begged Granny Fox.

But Ol' Mistah Buzzard would not say another word. So old Granny Fox started for home as fast as she could run.

“Oh dear, I do hope Reddy Fox minded me and stayed in the house,” she muttered.

XXIII. Granny Fox Has a Terrible Scare

Old Granny Fox felt her heart sink way down to her toes. She felt sure Ol' Mistah Buzzard had seen Farmer Brown's boy and his gun over near the house where Reddy Fox was nursing his wounds. If not, he would not have advised her to hurry home. She was already very tired and hot from the long run to lead Bowser the Hound away from the Green Meadows. She had thought to walk home along shaded paths and cool off. But now she must run faster than ever, for she must know if Farmer Brown's boy had found her house.

“It's lucky I told Reddy Fox to go inside and not come out till I returned. It's very lucky I did that,” thought Granny Fox as she ran. Presently she heard voices singing. They seemed to be in the treetops over her head.

“Happily we dance and play All the livelong sunny day! Happily we run and race And win or lose with smiling face!”

Granny Fox knew the voices, and she looked up. Just as she expected, she saw the Merry Little Breezes of Old Mother West Wind playing among the leaves. Just then one of them looked down and saw her.

“There's old Granny Fox! Just see how hot and tired she looks. Let's go down and cool her off!” shouted the Merry Little Breeze.

In a flash they were all down out of the treetops and dancing around old Granny Fox, cooling her off. Of course, Granny Fox kept right on running. She was too worried not to. But the Merry Little Breezes kept right beside her. It was not nearly as hard running now as it had been.

“Have you seen Farmer Brown's boy?” panted Granny Fox.

“Oh, yes! We saw him just a little while ago over near your house, Granny Fox. We pulled his hat off, just to hear him scold,” shouted the Merry Little Breezes. Then they tickled and laughed as if they had had a good time with Farmer Brown's boy.

But old Granny Fox did not laugh--oh, my, no, indeed! Her heart went lower still, and she did her best to run faster. Pretty soon she came out on the top of the hill where she could look. Then it seemed as if her heart came right up in her mouth and stopped beating. Her eyes popped almost out of her head. There was Farmer Brown's boy standing right in front of the door of her home. And while she was watching, what should Reddy Fox do but stick his head out the door.

Old Granny Fox saw the gun of Farmer Brown's boy pointed right at Reddy. She clapped both hands over her eyes to shut out the terrible sight. Then she waited for the bang of the gun. It did not come. Then Granny peeped through her fingers. Farmer Brown's boy was still there, but Reddy Fox had disappeared inside the house.

Granny Fox sighed in relief. It had been a terrible scare, the worst she could remember.

XXIV. Granny and Reddy Have To Move

“I do not want to move,” whined Reddy Fox. “I'm too sore to walk.”

Old Granny Fox gave him a push. "You go along and do as I say!" she said sharply. "If you had listened to me, we would not have to move. It is all your own fault. It is a wonder that you were not killed when you put your head out right in front of Farmer Brown's boy. Now that he knows where we live, he will give us no peace. Move along quickly now! This is the best home I have ever had, and now I have to leave it. Oh dear! Oh dear!"

Reddy Fox walked slowly up the long hall and out the front door. He was walking on three legs. At every step he made a face because, you know, it hurt so much to walk.

The little stars, looking down from the sky, saw Reddy Fox walk slowly out the door of the house he had lived in so long. Right behind him came old Granny Fox. Granny sighed and wiped away a tear as she said good-by to her old home. Reddy Fox was thinking too much of his own troubles to notice how badly Granny Fox was feeling. Every few steps he had to sit down and rest because it hurt him so much to walk.

"I do not see why we have to move tonight," said Reddy Fox. "It would be a lot easier and nicer when the sun is shining. This night air makes me so stiff that I know I will never get over it."

Old Granny Fox listened to him for a while, and then she lost patience. Yes, Sir, Granny Fox lost patience. She hit Reddy Fox first on one ear and then on the other. Reddy began to cry.

"Stop that!" said Granny Fox sharply. "Do you want all the neighbors to know that we have to move? They will find it out soon enough. Now come along without any more trouble. If you do not, I will just go off and leave you to take care of yourself. Then how will you get anything to eat?"

Reddy Fox wiped his eyes on his coat sleeve and walked slowly along as best he could. Granny Fox would run a little way ahead to see that the way was safe and then come back for Reddy. Poor Reddy. He did his best not to complain, but it was such hard work. And somehow Reddy Fox did not believe that it was necessary at all. He had been very scared when he had not obeyed Granny Fox that afternoon and put his head out the door. He had looked right into the face of Farmer Brown's boy, which had many small brown spots. He had pulled his head back out of sight again too quickly for Farmer Brown's boy to shoot. Now he could not see why old Granny Fox wanted to move that very night.

"She is getting old. She is getting old and easily scared and worried about small things," said Reddy Fox in a low voice, as he walked slowly along behind her.

It seemed to Reddy as if they had walked for miles and miles. He really thought that they had been walking nearly all night when old Granny Fox stopped in front of the worst-looking old fox house Reddy had ever seen.

"Here we are!" said she.

"What! Are we going to live in that thing?" cried Reddy. "It is not fit for any good fox to put his nose into."

"It is where I was born!" said old Granny Fox sharply. "If you want to keep out of danger, do not start putting on airs now.

"Who looks down on the simple things of life And turns up his nose at all he sees, Is almost sure to feel the knife Of need cut through his pleasant ease.

"Now do not let me hear another word from you, but get inside at once!"

Reddy Fox did not quite understand all Granny Fox said, but he knew when she was to be obeyed. So he crawled carefully through the broken-down doorway.

XXV. Peter Rabbit Makes a Discovery

Jolly, round, red Mr. Sun had just thrown off his nightcap and come out from his home behind the Purple Hills for his daily climb up in the blue, blue sky. Then Farmer Brown's boy started down the Lone Little Path through the Green Forest.

Peter Rabbit had been out all night and was just then on his way home. He saw him. Peter stopped and sat up to rub his eyes and look again. He was not quite sure that he had seen right the first time. But he had. There was Farmer Brown's boy, sure enough, and at his heels ran Bowser the Hound.

Peter Rabbit rubbed his eyes once more and moved his eyebrows up and down. Farmer Brown's boy certainly had a gun over one shoulder and a spade over the other. Where could he be going down the Lone Little Path with a spade? Farmer Brown's garden certainly was not in that direction. Peter watched him out of sight and then he hurried down to the Green Meadows to tell Johnny Chuck what he had seen. My, how Peter's long legs did fly! He was so excited that he had forgotten how sleepy he had felt a few minutes before.

Halfway down to Johnny Chuck's house, Peter Rabbit almost ran right into Bobby Coon and Jimmy Skunk. They had been arguing and were calling each other names. They stopped when they saw Peter Rabbit.

"Peter Rabbit runs away From his shadow, so they say. Peter, Peter, what a sight! Tell us why this sudden fright,"

shouted Bobby Coon.

Peter Rabbit stopped short. Indeed, he stopped so short that he almost fell over. "Say," he panted, "I have just seen Farmer Brown's boy."

"You do not say so!" said Jimmy Skunk, pretending to be very much surprised. "You do not say so! Why, now I think of it, I believe I have seen Farmer Brown's boy a few times myself."

Peter Rabbit made a friendly face at Jimmy Skunk, and then he told all about how he had seen Farmer Brown's boy with gun and spade and Bowser the Hound going down the Lone Little Path. "You know there is not any garden down that way," he concluded.

Bobby Coon's face wore a serious look. Yes, Sir, all the fun was gone from Bobby Coon's face.

"What is the matter?" asked Jimmy Skunk.

"I was just thinking that Reddy Fox lives over in that direction and he is so stiff that he cannot run," replied Bobby Coon.

Jimmy Skunk pulled up his trousers and started toward the Lone Little Path. "Come on!" said he. "Let us follow him and see what he is about."

Bobby Coon followed at once, but Peter Rabbit said he would hurry over and get Johnny Chuck and then join the others.

All this time Farmer Brown's boy had been hurrying down the Lone Little Path to the home old Granny Fox and Reddy Fox had moved out of the night before. Of course, he did not know that they had moved. He put down his gun. By the time Jimmy Skunk and Bobby Coon and Peter Rabbit and Johnny Chuck reached a place where they could look out and see what was going on, he had dug a great hole.

“Oh!” cried Peter Rabbit. “He is digging into the house of Reddy Fox, and he will catch poor Reddy!”

XXVI. Farmer Brown's Boy Works for Nothing

The grass around the doorstep of the house where Reddy Fox had always lived was all wet with dew. Farmer Brown's boy laid his gun down. He took off his coat. He rolled up his shirt sleeves. Then he picked up his spade. It was cool and beautiful there on the edge of the Green Meadows. Jolly, round, red Mr. Sun had just begun his long climb up in the blue, blue sky. Mr. Redwing was singing for joy over in the bulrushes on the edge of the Smiling Pool. Yes, it was very beautiful, very beautiful indeed. It did not seem as if harm could come to anyone on such a beautiful morning.

But there was Farmer Brown's boy. He had crawled on his hands and knees without making a sound. He wanted to get near enough to the home of Reddy Fox to shoot if Reddy was outside. But there was no sign of Reddy. So Farmer Brown's boy had hopped up. Now he was whistling as he began to dig. His freckled face looked good-natured. It did not seem as if he could mean harm to anyone.

But there lay the gun. And he was working as if he meant to get to the very bottom of Reddy Fox's home!

Deeper and deeper grew the hole. Bigger and bigger grew the pile of sand which he threw out. He did not know that anyone was watching him, except Bowser the Hound. He did not see Johnny Chuck peeping from behind a tall bunch of meadow grass. He did not see Peter Rabbit peeping from behind a tree on the edge of the Green Forest. He did not see Bobby Coon looking from a safe hiding place in the top of that same tree. He did not see Jimmy Skunk or Unc' Billy Possum or Happy Jack Squirrel or Digger the Badger. He did not see one of them, but they saw him. They saw every shovelful of sand that he threw. Their hearts went pit-a-pat as they watched. Each one felt sure that something dreadful was going to happen to Reddy Fox.

Only Ol' Mistah Buzzard knew better. From way up high in the blue, blue sky he could look down and see many things. He could see all the little meadow and forest people who were watching Farmer Brown's boy. The harder Farmer Brown's boy worked, the more Ol' Mistah Buzzard chuckled to himself. What was he laughing at? Why, he could see the sharp face of old Granny Fox. She was peeping out from behind an old fence corner, and she was grinning. So Ol' Mistah Buzzard knew Reddy Fox was safe.

But the other little people of the Green Forest and the Green Meadows did not know that old Granny Fox and Reddy Fox had moved. Their faces grew longer and longer as they watched Farmer Brown's boy go deeper and deeper into the ground.

“Reddy Fox has worried me almost to death. He would eat me if he could catch me. But somehow things would not be quite the same without him around. Oh dear, I do not want him killed,” moaned Peter Rabbit.

“Perhaps he is not home,” said Jimmy Skunk.

“Of course he is home. He is so stiff and sore he can hardly walk at all. He has to stay home,” replied Johnny Chuck. “Hello, what is the matter now?”

Everybody looked. Farmer Brown's boy had climbed out of the hole. He looked tired and cross. He rested for a few minutes. As he rested, he scowled. Then he began to shovel the sand back into the hole. He had reached the bottom and found no one there.

“Hurrah!” shouted Peter Rabbit. He struck his heels together as he jumped up in the air.

And the others were just as glad as Peter Rabbit. Johnny Chuck was especially glad. You see, Farmer Brown's boy had once found Johnny's snug home. Johnny had had to move as suddenly as did Granny and Reddy Fox. Johnny knew just how Reddy must feel. He had had many narrow escapes in his short life.
