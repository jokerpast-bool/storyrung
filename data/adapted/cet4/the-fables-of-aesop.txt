Aesop’s Fables

The Cock and the Pearl

A cock was once walking proudly up and down the farmyard among the female chickens. Suddenly he saw something bright in the straw. “Ho! ho!” he said. “That is for me.” He soon pulled it out from under the straw. “What did it turn out to be but a Pearl that by some chance had been lost in the yard? You may be a treasure,” said Master Cock, “to men who value you. But for me I would rather have a single grain of barley than a large amount of pearls.”

Precious things are for those who can value them.

The Wolf and the Lamb

Once upon a time a Wolf was drinking at a spring on a hillside. He looked up and saw a Lamb just beginning to drink a little lower down. “There is my supper,” he thought, “if only I can find some excuse to seize it.” Then he called out to the Lamb, “How dare you make the water dirty that I am drinking?”

“No, master, no,” said Lambikin. “If the water is dirty up there, I cannot be the cause of it. It runs down from you to me.”

“Well, then,” said the Wolf, “why did you call me bad names this time last year?”

“That cannot be,” said the Lamb. “I am only six months old.”

“I do not care,” said the Wolf with a snarl. “If it was not you it was your father.” And with that he rushed upon the poor little Lamb and

ate her all up. But before she died she said with difficulty—

“Any excuse will serve a tyrant.”

The Dog and the Shadow

It happened that a Dog had got a piece of meat and was carrying it home in his mouth to eat it in peace. On his way home he had to cross a board lying across a running brook. As he crossed, he looked down and saw his own shadow reflected in the water beneath. He thought it was another dog with another piece of meat. He decided to have that also. So he made a snap at the shadow in the water. But as he opened his mouth the piece of meat fell out. It dropped into the water and was never seen again.

Be careful that you do not lose the real thing by trying to take hold of the shadow.

The Lion’s Share

The Lion went once hunting along with the Fox, the Jackal, and the Wolf. They hunted and they hunted until at last they surprised a Stag. They soon killed it. Then came the question how the meat should be divided. “Cut this Stag into four parts for me,” said the Lion with a roar. So the other animals skinned it and cut it into four parts. Then the Lion took his stand in front of the dead body and said his judgment: The first quarter is for me because I am King of Beasts. The second is mine as judge. Another share comes to me for my part in the chase. And as for the fourth quarter, well, as for that, I should like to see which of you will dare to lay a paw upon it.”

“Humph,” said the Fox as he walked away with his tail between his legs. But he spoke in a low growl—

“You may share the work of the great, but you will not share the meat.”

The Wolf and the Crane

A Wolf had been eating too much of an animal he had killed. Suddenly a small bone in the meat stuck in his throat and he could not swallow it. He soon felt terrible pain in his throat. He ran up and down making sounds of pain and looking for something to relieve the pain. He tried to get every one he met to remove the bone. “I would give anything,” said he, “if you would take it out.” At last the Crane agreed to try. It told the Wolf to lie on his side and open his jaws as wide as he could. Then the Crane put its long neck down the Wolf’s throat. With its beak it loosened the bone, until at last it got it out.

“Will you kindly give me the reward you promised?” said the Crane.

The Wolf smiled and showed his teeth and said: “Be content. You have put your head inside a Wolf’s mouth and taken it out again in safety. That ought to be reward enough for you.”

Gratitude and greed do not go together.

The Man and the Serpent

A Countryman’s son by accident stepped on a Serpent’s tail. It turned and bit him so that he died. The father in a rage got his axe. He chased the Serpent and cut off part of its tail. So the Serpent in return began stinging several of the Farmer’s cattle and caused him severe loss. Well, the Farmer thought it best to make peace with the Serpent. He brought food and honey to the mouth of its home and said to it: “Let us forget and forgive. Perhaps you were right to punish my son, and take vengeance on my cattle. But surely I was right in trying to take revenge for him. Now that we are both satisfied why should we not be friends again?”

“No, no,” said the Serpent. “Take away your gifts. You can never forget the death of your son, nor I the loss of my tail.”

Injuries may be forgiven, but not forgotten.

The Town Mouse and the Country Mouse

Now you must know that a Town Mouse once upon a time went on a visit to his cousin in the country. He was rough and ready, this cousin, but he loved his town friend and made him heartily welcome. Beans and bacon, cheese and bread, were all he had to offer, but he offered them freely. The Town Mouse rather turned up his long nose at this country fare. He said: “I cannot understand, Cousin, how you can put up with such poor food as this. But of course you cannot expect anything better in the country. Come with me and I will show you how to live. When you have been in town a week you will wonder how you could ever have stood a country life.” No sooner said than done: the two mice set off for the town and arrived at the Town Mouse’s home late at night. “You will want some refreshment after our long journey,” said the polite Town Mouse. He took his friend into the grand dining-room. There they found the remains of a fine feast, and soon the two mice were eating up jellies and cakes and all that was nice. Suddenly they heard growling and barking. “What is that?” said the Country Mouse. “It is only the dogs of the house,” answered the other. “Only!” said the Country Mouse. “I do not like that music at my dinner.” Just at that moment the door flew open, in came two huge mastiffs, and the two mice had to run down and run off. “Good-bye, Cousin,” said the Country Mouse. “What! going so soon?” said the other. “Yes,” he replied;

"Better beans and bacon in peace than cakes and ale in fear."

The Fox and the Crow

A Fox once saw a Crow fly off with a piece of cheese in its mouth. The Crow landed on a branch of a tree. "That's for me, since I am a Fox," said Master Reynard. He walked up to the foot of the tree. "Good day, Mistress Crow," he cried. "How well you look today! How bright your feathers are! How bright your eye is! I feel sure your voice must be better than that of other birds, just as your shape is. Let me hear just one song from you. Then I can greet you as the Queen of Birds." The Crow lifted up her head and began to make her best sound. But the moment she opened her mouth, the piece of cheese fell to the ground. Master Fox quickly caught it up. "That will do," said he. "That was all I wanted. In exchange for your cheese, I will give you a piece of advice for the future—

"Do not trust flatterers."

The Flatterer takes by secret ways, His victim, both of Wit and Wealth.

The Sick Lion

A Lion had come to the end of his days. He lay sick and near death at the mouth of his cave, breathing with difficulty. The animals, his subjects, came round him. They drew nearer as he grew more and more weak. When they saw him on the point of death, they thought to themselves: "Now is the time to pay off old grudges." So the Boar came up and drove at him with his tusks. Then a Bull struck him with his horns. Still the Lion lay weak before them. So the Ass, feeling quite safe from danger, came up. He turned his tail to the Lion and kicked his heels into his face. "This is a double death," said the Lion in a low, angry voice.

Only cowards insult dying majesty.

The Ass and the Lapdog

A Farmer one day came to the stables to see to his work animals. Among them was his favourite Ass, that was always well fed and often carried his master. With the Farmer came his Lapdog, who danced about and licked his hand and jumped about as happy as could be. The Farmer felt in his pocket and gave the Lapdog some fine food. He sat down while he gave his orders to his servants. The Lapdog jumped into his master's lap and lay there blinking while the Farmer touched his ears. The Ass, seeing this, broke loose from his rope and began jumping about in imitation of the Lapdog. The Farmer could not stop laughing. So the Ass went up to him and put his feet upon the Farmer's shoulder. He attempted to climb into his lap. The Farmer's servants rushed up with sticks and pitchforks and soon taught the Ass that

Clumsy jesting is no joke.

The Lion and the Mouse

Once when a Lion was asleep, a little Mouse began running up and down upon him. This soon wakened the Lion, who placed his huge foot upon him. He opened his big jaws to swallow him. "Pardon, O King," cried the little Mouse. "Forgive me this time. I shall never forget it. Who knows but what I may be able to do you a turn some of these days?" The Lion was so pleased at the idea of the Mouse being able to help him that he lifted up his foot and let him go. Some time after, the Lion was caught in a trap. The hunters who desired to carry him alive to the King tied him to a tree. Then they went in search of a waggon to carry him on. Just then the little Mouse happened to pass by. Seeing the sad state the Lion was in, he went up to him. He soon bit through the ropes that bound the King of the Beasts. "Was I not right?" said the little Mouse.

Little friends may prove great friends.

The Swallow and the Other Birds

It happened that a Countryman was sowing some hemp seeds in a field. A Swallow and some other birds were hopping about picking up their food. "Beware of that man," said the Swallow. "Why, what is he doing?" said the others. "That is hemp seed he is sowing. Be careful to pick up every one of the seeds, or else you will be sorry." The birds paid no attention to the Swallow's words. By and by the hemp grew up and was made into cord. Of the cords, nets were made. Many a bird that had despised the Swallow's advice was caught in nets made out of that very hemp. "What did I tell you?" said the Swallow.

Destroy the seed of evil, or it will grow up to your ruin.

The Frogs Desiring a King

The Frogs were living as happy as could be in a wet, soft swamp that just suited them. They went splashing about caring for nobody and nobody troubling with them. But some of them thought that this was not right. They should have a king and a proper set of laws. So they determined to send up a petition to Jove to give them what they wanted. "Mighty Jove," they cried, "send to us a king that will rule over us and keep us in order." Jove laughed at their croaking and threw down into the swamp a huge Log. It came down splashing into the swamp. The Frogs were frightened out of their lives by the noise made in their midst. They all rushed to the bank to look at the horrible monster. But after a time, seeing that it did not move, one or two of the boldest of them ventured out towards the Log. They even dared to touch it. Still it did not move. Then the greatest hero of the Frogs jumped upon the Log and began dancing up and down upon it. Thereupon all the Frogs came and did the same. For some time the Frogs went about their business every day without taking the slightest notice of their new King Log lying in their midst. But this did not suit them. So they sent another petition to Jove and said to him, "We want a real king, one that will really rule over us." Now this made Jove angry. So he sent among them a big Stork that soon set to work eating them all up. Then the Frogs were sorry when too late.

Better no rule than cruel rule.

The Mountains in Labour

One day the Countrymen noticed that the Mountains were in labour. Smoke came out of their tops. The earth was shaking at their feet. Trees were falling, and huge rocks were rolling down. They felt sure that something terrible was going to happen. They all gathered together in one place to see what terrible thing this could be. They waited and they waited, but nothing came. At last there was a still more violent earthquake, and a huge gap appeared in the side of the Mountains. They all fell down upon their knees and waited. At last, and at last, a very, very small mouse put its little head and short stiff hairs out of the gap. It came running down towards them. And ever after they used to say:

“Much noise, little result.”

The Hares and the Frogs

The Hares were so badly treated by the other beasts that they did not know where to go. As soon as they saw a single animal come near them, off they used to run. One day they saw a troop of wild Horses running wildly about. In quite a panic all the Hares ran off quickly to a lake close by. They were determined to kill themselves in the water rather than live in such a continual state of fear. But just as they got near the bank of the lake, a troop of Frogs were frightened in their turn by the approach of the Hares. They ran off quickly and jumped into the water. “Truly,” said one of the Hares, “things are not so bad as they seem:

“There is always someone worse off than yourself.”

The Wolf and the Kid

A Kid was sitting high up on the top of a house. Looking down, he saw a Wolf passing under him. Immediately he began to call his enemy bad names and attack him with words. “Murderer and thief,” he cried, “what do you do here near honest folks’ houses? How dare you appear where your evil deeds are known?”

“Curse away, my young friend,” said the Wolf.

“It is easy to be brave from a safe distance.”

The Woodman and the Serpent

One cold winter day a Woodman was walking home from his work when he saw something black lying on the snow. When he came closer he saw it was a Serpent that looked dead. But he took it up and put it inside his coat to warm it while he hurried home. As soon as he got indoors he put the Serpent down on the floor before the fire. The children watched it and saw it slowly come to life again. Then one of them bent down to touch it, but the Serpent raised its head and put out its long sharp teeth. It was about to bite the child to death. So the Woodman seized his axe, and with one stroke cut the Serpent in two. “Ah,” said he,

“No thanks from the evil.”

The Bald Man and the Fly

There was once a Man with no hair who sat down after work on a hot summer day. A Fly came up and kept making a low noise around his head with no hair. It bit him from time to time. The Man aimed a blow at his little enemy, but—whack—his hand came on his own head instead. Again the Fly troubled him, but this time the Man was wiser and said:

“You will only hurt yourself if you take notice of hateful enemies.”

The Fox and the Stork

At one time the Fox and the Stork were on visiting terms and seemed very good friends. So the Fox invited the Stork to dinner. For a joke he put nothing before her but some soup in a very shallow dish. The Fox could easily drink it up, but the Stork could only wet the end of her long bill in it. She left the meal as hungry as when she began. “I am sorry,” said the Fox, “the soup is not to your liking.”

“Please do not say sorry,” said the Stork. “I hope you will return this visit, and come and dine with me soon.” So a day was fixed when the Fox should visit the Stork. But when they were seated at table, all that was for their dinner was inside a very long-necked jar with a narrow mouth. The Fox could not put his nose into it, so all he could do was to lick the outside of the jar.

“I will not say sorry for the dinner,” said the Stork:

“One bad turn deserves another.”

The Fox and the Mask

A Fox had by some means got into the store-room of a theatre. Suddenly he saw a face looking down at him and began to be very afraid. But looking more closely he found it was only a Mask such as actors use to put over their face. “Ah,” said the Fox, “you look very fine. It is a pity you have not got any brains.”

Outside show is a poor substitute for inner worth.

The Jay and the Peacock

A Jay went into a yard where Peacocks used to walk. He found there a number of feathers which had fallen from the Peacocks when they were losing their old feathers. He tied them all to his tail and walked proudly down towards the Peacocks. When he came near them they soon discovered the trick. They walked up to him and pecked at him and pulled away his borrowed feathers. So the Jay could do no better than go back to the other Jays, who had watched his behaviour from a distance. But they were equally angry with him, and told him:

“It is not only fine feathers that make fine birds.”

The Frog and the Ox

“Oh Father,” said a little Frog to the big one sitting by the side of a pool, “I have seen such a terrible monster! It was as big as a mountain, with horns on its head, and a long tail, and it had hoofs divided in two.”

“Tush, child, tush,” said the old Frog, “that was only Farmer White’s Ox. It isn’t so big either. He may be a little bit taller than I, but I could easily make myself quite as broad. Just you see.” So he blew himself out, and blew himself out, and blew himself out. “Was he as big as that?” asked he.

“Oh, much bigger than that,” said the young Frog.

Again the old one blew himself out, and asked the young one if the Ox was as big as that.

“Bigger, father, bigger,” was the reply.

So the Frog took a deep breath, and blew and blew and blew, and swelled and swelled and swelled. And then he said: “I’m sure the Ox is not as big as this.” But at this moment he burst.

Self-conceit may lead to self-destruction.

Androcles

A slave named Androcles once escaped from his master and ran away to the forest. As he was walking around there, he came upon a Lion lying down and making sad sounds. At first he turned to run away. But he found that the Lion did not follow him, so he turned back and went up to him. As he came near, the Lion put out his foot, which was all swollen and bleeding. Androcles found that a huge thorn had got into it, and was causing all the pain. He pulled out the thorn and tied up the foot of the Lion. The Lion was soon able to get up and lick the hand of Androcles like a dog. Then the Lion took Androcles to his cave. Every day he used to bring him meat to live on. But soon after, both Androcles and the Lion were caught. The slave was sentenced to be thrown to the Lion, after the Lion had been kept without food for several days. The Emperor and all his Court came to see the show. Androcles was led out into the middle of the arena. Soon the Lion was let loose from his cave, and rushed jumping and roaring towards his victim. But as soon as he came near to Androcles, he knew his friend. He showed love to him and licked his hands like a friendly dog. The Emperor was surprised at this and called Androcles to him. Androcles told him the whole story. Then the slave was forgiven and freed, and the Lion was let loose to go back to his native forest.

Gratitude is the sign of a good heart.

The Bat, the Birds, and the Beasts

A great fight was about to start between the Birds and the Beasts. When the two armies came together, the Bat did not know which side to join. The Birds that passed his resting place said: “Come with us.” But he said: “I am a Beast.” Later, some Beasts who were passing below him looked up and said: “Come with us.” But he said: “I am a Bird.” Luckily, at the last moment, peace was made. No battle took place. So the Bat came to the Birds and wanted to join the celebration. But they all turned against him, and he had to fly away. He then went to the Beasts, but soon had to run away, or else they would have torn him to pieces. “Ah,” said the Bat, “I see now,

“He that is neither one thing nor the other has no friends.”

The Hart and the Hunter

The Hart was once drinking from a pool and admiring the fine shape he made there. “Ah,” said he, “where can you see such fine horns as these, with such branches! I wish I had legs more fit to carry such a fine crown. It is a shame they are so thin and weak.” At that moment a Hunter came near and sent an arrow flying after him. Away ran the Hart, and soon, with the help of his quick legs, he was almost out of sight of the Hunter. But he did not notice where he was going. He passed under some trees with branches growing low down, and his horns were caught. So the Hunter had time to come up. “Alas! alas!” cried the Hart:

“We often hate what is most useful to us.”

The Serpent and the File

A Serpent in its travels came into a shop where tools were made. As it moved over the floor, it felt its skin cut by a file lying there. In a rage it turned round upon it and tried to drive its teeth into it. But it could do no harm to the heavy iron and soon had to give up its anger.

It is no use attacking something that cannot feel.

The Man and the Wood

A Man came into a Wood one day with a cutting tool in his hand. He begged all the Trees to give him a small branch which he wanted for a special purpose. The Trees were kind and gave him one of their branches. What did the Man do but fix it into the head of the tool, and soon set to work cutting down tree after tree. Then the Trees saw how foolish they had been in giving their enemy the means of destroying themselves.

The Dog and the Wolf

A thin Wolf was almost dead with hunger when he happened to meet a House-dog who was passing by. “Ah, Cousin,” said the Dog. “I knew how it would be. Your wild life will soon be the ruin of you. Why do you not work steadily as I do, and get your food given to you regularly?”

“I would have no problem,” said the Wolf, “if I could only get a place.”

“I will easily arrange that for you,” said the Dog. “Come with me to my master and you shall share my work.”

So the Wolf and the Dog went towards the town together. On the way there the Wolf noticed that the hair on a certain part of the Dog’s neck was very much worn away. So he asked him how that had come about.

“Oh, it is nothing,” said the Dog. “That is only the place where the collar is put on at night to keep me chained up. It rubs a bit, but one soon gets used to it.”

“Is that all?” said the Wolf. “Then good-bye to you, Master Dog.”

Better starve free than be a fat slave.

The Belly and the Members

One fine day it occurred to the Members of the Body that they were doing all the work and the Belly was having all the food. So they held a meeting, and after a long discussion, decided to stop work until the Belly agreed to take its proper share of the work. So for a day or two, the Hands refused to take the food, the Mouth refused to receive it, and the Teeth had no work to do. But after a day or two the Members began to find that they themselves were not in a very active condition. The Hands could hardly move, and the Mouth was all dry, while the Legs were unable to support the rest. So they found that even the Belly, in its dull quiet way, was doing necessary work for the Body. And all must work together or the Body will fall apart.

The Hart in the Ox-Stall

A Hart, hotly chased by the hounds, ran for shelter into an ox-stall, and buried itself in a bundle of hay, leaving nothing to be seen but the tips of its horns. Soon after, the Hunters came up and asked if anyone had seen the Hart. The stable boys, who had been resting after their dinner, looked round, but could see nothing, and the Hunters went away. Shortly afterwards the master came in, and looking round, saw that something unusual had taken place. He pointed to the bundle of hay and said: “What are those two curious things sticking out of the hay?” And when the stable boys came to look they discovered the Hart, and soon made an end of him. He thus learnt that

Nothing escapes the master’s eye.

The Fox and the Grapes

One hot summer’s day a Fox was walking through an orchard until he came to a bunch of Grapes just ripening on a vine which had been trained over a high branch. “Just the thing to quench my thirst,” said he. Drawing back a few paces, he took a run and a jump, and just missed the bunch. Turning round again with a One, Two, Three, he jumped up, but with no greater success. Again and again he tried after the tempting morsel, but at last had to give it up, and walked away with his nose in the air, saying: “I am sure they are sour.”

It is easy to hate what you cannot get.

The Peacock and Juno

A Peacock once placed a petition before Juno desiring to have the voice of a nightingale in addition to his other attractions. But Juno refused his request. When he persisted, and pointed out that he was her favourite bird, she said:

“Be content with your lot. One cannot be first in everything.”

The Horse, Hunter, and Stag

A fight had started between the Horse and the Stag. So the Horse came to a Hunter to ask for his help to hurt the Stag in return. The Hunter agreed, but said: "If you want to beat the Stag, you must let me put this piece of iron between your jaws. Then I can guide you with these ropes. Allow this seat to be placed on your back. Then I can stay steady on you as we follow the enemy." The Horse agreed to the conditions. The Hunter soon put the seat and ropes on him. Then with the help of the Hunter the Horse soon beat the Stag. He said to the Hunter: "Now, get off, and take those things off my mouth and back."

"Not so fast, friend," said the Hunter. "I have now got you under my control. I prefer to keep you as you are at present."

If you let men use you for your own purposes, they will use you for their own.

The Fox and the Lion

When the Fox first saw the Lion he was very scared. He ran away and hid himself in the wood. The next time however he came near the King of Beasts. He stopped at a safe distance and watched him pass by. The third time they came near one another the Fox went straight up to the Lion. He said hello to him. He asked him how his family were, and when he should have the pleasure of seeing him again. Then turning his tail, he left the Lion without much ceremony.

Knowing someone well can lead to a lack of respect.

The Lion and the Statue

A Man and a Lion were talking about the strength of men and lions in general. The Man said that he and his fellows were stronger than lions because of their greater intelligence. "Come now with me," he cried, "and I will soon prove that I am right." So he took him into the public gardens. He showed him a statue of Hercules beating the Lion and tearing his mouth in two.

"That is all very well," said the Lion, "but proves nothing, for it was a man who made the statue."

We can easily show things as we wish them to be.

The Ant and the Grasshopper

In a field one summer day a Grasshopper was jumping about. It was making sounds and singing as much as it liked. An Ant passed by, carrying with great effort an ear of corn he was taking to the nest.

"Why not come and talk with me," said the Grasshopper, "instead of working so hard in that way?"

"I am helping to store up food for the winter," said the Ant, "and suggest you do the same."

"Why worry about winter?" said the Grasshopper; we have plenty of food at present." But the Ant went on its way and continued its hard work. When the winter came the Grasshopper had no food. It found itself dying of hunger. It saw the ants giving out every day corn and grain from the stores they had collected in the summer. Then the Grasshopper knew:

It is best to prepare for the days of need.

The Tree and the Reed

"Well, little one," said a Tree to a Reed that was growing at its foot, "why do you not put your feet deeply in the ground, and raise your head bravely in the air as I do?"

"I am happy with my lot," said the Reed. "I may not be so grand, but I think I am safer."

"Safe!" said the Tree with a mean smile. "Who shall pull me up by the roots or bend my head to the ground?" But it soon had to feel sorry for its proud words. A great storm arose which tore it up from its roots. It threw it a useless log on the ground. The little Reed bent to the force of the wind. It soon stood straight again when the storm had passed over.

Being unknown often brings safety.

The Fox and the Cat

A Fox was telling a Cat proudly about its clever ways for escaping its enemies. "I have a whole bag of tricks," he said, "which contains a hundred ways of escaping my enemies."

"I have only one," said the Cat; "but I can usually manage with that." Just at that moment they heard the cry of a pack of dogs coming towards them. The Cat immediately ran up a tree and hid herself in the branches. "This is my plan," said the Cat. "What are you going to do?" The Fox thought first of one way, then of another. While he was deciding the dogs came nearer and nearer. At last the Fox in his confusion was caught up by the dogs. He was soon killed by the hunters. Miss Puss, who had been looking on, said:

"Better one safe way than a hundred on which you cannot count."

The Wolf in Sheep's Clothing

A Wolf found great difficulty in getting at the sheep owing to the watchfulness of the shepherd and his dogs. But one day it found the skin of a sheep that had been removed and thrown aside. So it put it on over its own skin and walked down among the sheep. The Lamb that belonged to the sheep, whose skin the Wolf was wearing, began to follow the Wolf in the Sheep's clothing. So, leading the Lamb a little apart, he soon made a meal off her. For some time he succeeded in fooling the sheep, and enjoying big meals.

Looks can fool you.

The Dog in the Manger

A Dog looking for its afternoon nap jumped into the Manger of an Ox. It lay there comfortably upon the dry grass. But soon the Ox, returning from its afternoon work, came up to the Manger. It wanted to eat some of the dry grass. The Dog in a rage, being woken from its sleep, stood up and barked at the Ox. Whenever it came near the Dog tried to bite it. At last the Ox had to give up the hope of getting at the dry grass. It went away saying quietly:

"Ah, people often refuse others what they cannot enjoy themselves."

The Man and the Wooden God

In the old days men used to worship sticks and stones and statues. They prayed to them to give them luck. It happened that a Man had often prayed to a wooden statue he had received from his father. But his luck never seemed to change. He prayed and he prayed, but still he remained as unlucky as ever. One day in the greatest rage he went to the Wooden God. With one blow he swept it down from its base. The statue broke in two. What did he see? A huge number of coins flying all over the place.

The Fisher

A Fisher once took his bagpipes to the bank of a river. He played them with the hope of making the fish rise. But not one fish put his nose out of the water. So he cast his net into the river and soon pulled it out full of fish. Then he took his bagpipes again. As he played, the fish jumped up in the net. “Ah, you dance now when I play,” he said.

“Yes,” said an old Fish:

“When you are in a man’s power you must do as he tells you.”

The Shepherd’s Boy

There was once a young Shepherd Boy who watched his sheep at the foot of a mountain near a dark forest. It was rather lonely for him all day. So he thought of a plan to get a little company and some excitement. He ran down towards the village calling out “Wolf, Wolf.” The villagers came out to meet him. Some of them stayed with him for a long time. This pleased the boy so much that a few days later he tried the same trick. Again the villagers came to his help. But shortly after this a Wolf actually did come out from the forest. It began to attack the sheep. The boy of course cried out “Wolf, Wolf,” still louder than before. When the boy complained, the wise man of the village said:

“A liar will not be believed, even when he speaks the truth.”

The Young Thief and His Mother

A young Man had been caught in a daring act of theft. He had been sentenced to be executed for it. He said he wanted to see his Mother and speak with her before he was led to execution. Of course this was granted. When his Mother came to him he said: “I want to whisper to you.” When she brought her ear near him, he nearly bit it off. All the people standing nearby were horrified. They asked him what he could mean by such cruel and inhuman conduct. “It is to punish her,” he said. “When I was young I began with stealing little things. I brought them home to Mother. Instead of scolding and punishing me, she laughed and said: ‘It will not be noticed.’ It is because of her that I am here to-day.”

“He is right, woman,” said the Priest; “the Lord has said:

“Train up a child in the way he should go; and when he is old he will not depart from it.”

The Man and His Two Wives

In the old days, when men were allowed to have many wives, a middle-aged Man had one wife that was old and one that was young. Each loved him very much and wanted to see him look like herself. Now the Man’s hair was turning grey. The young Wife did not like this, as it made him look too old for her husband. So every night she used to comb his hair and pick out the white ones. But the elder Wife saw her husband growing grey with great pleasure. She did not like to be mistaken for his mother. So every morning she used to arrange his hair and pick out as many of the black ones as she could. The result was that the Man soon found himself completely bald.

Yield to all and you will soon have nothing to yield.

The Nurse and the Wolf

“Be quiet now,” said an old Nurse to a child sitting on her lap. “If you make that noise again I will throw you to the Wolf.”

Now it happened that a Wolf was passing close under the window as this was said. So he crouched down by the side of the house and waited. “I am in good luck to-day,” he thought. “It is sure to cry soon. I haven’t had a finer morsel for many a long day.” So he waited, and he waited, and he waited. At last the child began to cry. The Wolf came forward before the window and looked up to the Nurse, wagging his tail. But all the Nurse did was to shut the window and call for help. The dogs of the house came rushing out. “Ah,” said the Wolf as he galloped away,

“Enemies’ promises were made to be broken.”

The Tortoise and the Birds

A Tortoise wanted to change its place of residence. So he asked an Eagle to carry him to his new home. He promised her a rich reward for her trouble. The Eagle agreed and seized the Tortoise by the shell with her talons. She soared high up. On their way they met a Crow. The Crow said to the Eagle: “Tortoise is good eating.” “The shell is too hard,” said the Eagle in reply. “The rocks will soon crack the shell,” was the Crow’s answer. The Eagle took the hint and let the Tortoise fall on a sharp rock. The two birds made a hearty meal of the Tortoise.

Never soar high on an enemy’s wings.

The Two Crabs

One fine day two Crabs came out from their home to take a walk on the sand. “Child,” said the mother, “you are walking very awkwardly. You should get used to walking straight forward without twisting from side to side.”

“Please, mother,” said the young one, “just set the example yourself, and I will follow you.”

Example is the best rule.

The Ass in the Lion’s Skin

An Ass once found a Lion’s skin which the hunters had left out in the sun to dry. He put it on and went towards his native village. All fled at his approach, both men and animals. He was a proud Ass that day. In his delight he lifted up his voice and brayed. But then everyone knew him. His owner came up and gave him a sound beating for the fright he had caused. And shortly after a Fox came up to him and said: “Ah, I knew you by your voice.”

Fine clothes may hide, but silly words will show a fool.

The Two Fellows and the Bear

Two Fellows were travelling together through a wood when a Bear rushed out upon them. One of the travellers happened to be in front. He seized hold of the branch of a tree and hid himself among the leaves. The other, seeing no help for it, threw himself flat down upon the ground, with his face in the dust. The Bear, coming up to him, put its muzzle close to his ear and sniffed and sniffed. But at last with a growl it shook its head and walked slowly off, for bears will not touch dead meat. Then the fellow in the tree came down to his comrade. Laughing, he said, “What was it that Master Bruin whispered to you?”

"He told me," said the other man,

"Never trust a friend who leaves you when you are in trouble."

The Two Pots

Two Pots had been left on the bank of a river. One was made of brass, and one was made of clay. When the tide rose, they both floated off down the stream. Now the clay pot tried its best to keep away from the brass one. The brass pot cried out: “Fear nothing, friend, I will not hit you.”

“But I may touch you,” said the other, “if I come too close. And if I hit you, or you hit me, I shall be the one who suffers.”

The strong and the weak cannot be friends.

The Four Oxen and the Lion

A Lion used to move quietly around a field where Four Oxen lived. Many times he tried to attack them. But whenever he came near, they turned their tails to one another. So in whatever way he came at them, he met the horns of one of them. At last, however, they began to fight among themselves. Each went off to eat grass alone in a separate corner of the field. Then the Lion attacked them one by one and soon killed all four.

United we stand, divided we fall.

The Fisher and the Little Fish

It happened that a Fisher, after fishing all day, caught only a little fish. “Please, let me go, master,” said the Fish. “I am much too small for you to eat just now. If you put me back into the river, I shall soon grow. Then you can make a fine meal off me.”

“No, no, my little Fish,” said the Fisher, “I have you now. I may not catch you later.”

A little thing in hand is worth more than a great thing in the future.

Avaricious and Envious

Two neighbors came before Jupiter and prayed to him to grant their hearts’ desire. Now one was full of greed, and the other was eaten up with envy. So to punish them both, Jupiter granted that each might have whatever he wished for himself. But this was only on condition that his neighbor had twice as much. The greedy man prayed to have a room full of gold. It was done at once. But all his joy turned to grief when he found that his neighbor had two rooms full of the precious metal. Then came the turn of the envious man. He could not bear to think that his neighbor had any joy at all. So he prayed that he might have one of his own eyes put out. By this means his companion would become totally blind.

Vices are their own punishment.

The Crow and the Pitcher

A Crow, half-dead with thirst, came upon a Pitcher which had once been full of water. But when the Crow put its beak into the mouth of the Pitcher, he found that only very little water was left in it. He could not reach far enough down to get at it. He tried, and he tried, but at last had to give up in despair. Then a thought came to him. He took a small stone and dropped it into the Pitcher. Then he took another small stone and dropped it into the Pitcher. Then he took another small stone and dropped that into the Pitcher. Then he took another small stone and dropped that into the Pitcher. Then he took another small stone and dropped that into the Pitcher. Then he took another small stone and dropped that into the Pitcher. At last, at last, he saw the water rise up near him. After throwing in a few more small stones, he was able to drink and save his life.

Little by little does the trick.

The Man and the Satyr

A Man had lost his way in a wood one bitter cold winter night. As he was walking about, a Satyr came up to him. The Satyr found that he had lost his way and promised to give him a place to stay for the night. He also promised to guide him out of the forest in the morning. As he went along to the Satyr’s cell, the Man raised both his hands to his mouth and kept blowing on them. “What do you do that for?” said the Satyr.

“My hands are numb with the cold,” said the Man, “and my breath warms them.”

After this they arrived at the Satyr’s home. Soon the Satyr put a hot dish of porridge before him. But when the Man raised his spoon to his mouth, he began blowing on it. “And what do you do that for?” said the Satyr.

“The porridge is too hot, and my breath will cool it.”

“Out you go,” said the Satyr. “I will have nothing to do with a man who can blow hot and cold with the same breath.”

The Goose With the Golden Eggs

One day a farmer went to the nest of his Goose and found there an egg all yellow and shining. When he took it up, it was as heavy as lead. He was going to throw it away, because he thought someone had played a trick on him. But he took it home on second thought. Soon he found to his delight that it was an egg of pure gold. Every morning the same thing happened, and he soon became rich by selling his eggs. As he grew rich, he grew greedy. He thought to get at once all the gold the Goose could give. He killed it and opened it, only to find nothing.

Greed often reaches too far.

The Labourer and the Nightingale

A Labourer lay listening to a Nightingale’s song throughout the summer night. He was so pleased with it that the next night he set a trap for it and caught it. “Now that I have caught you,” he cried, “you shall always sing to me.”

“We Nightingales never sing in a cage,” said the bird.

“Then I’ll eat you,” said the Labourer. “I have always heard say that a nightingale on toast is a fine little meal.”

“No, kill me not,” said the Nightingale. “But let me free, and I’ll tell you three things far better worth than my poor body.” The Labourer let him loose. The bird flew up to a branch of a tree and said: “Never believe a prisoner’s promise; that’s one thing. Then again: Keep what you have. And the third piece of advice is: Do not be sad over what is lost forever.” Then the song-bird flew away.

The Fox, the Cock, and the Dog

One moonlit night a Fox was moving quietly around a farmer’s hen house. He saw a Cock resting high up beyond his reach. “Good news, good news!” he cried.

“Why, what is that?” said the Cock.

“King Lion has declared a universal truce. No beast may hurt a bird from now on, but all shall live together in brotherly friendship.”

“Why, that is good news,” said the Cock. “And there I see someone coming, with whom we can share the good tidings.” And so saying, he stretched his neck forward and looked far off.

“What is it you see?” said the Fox.

“It is only my master's Dog that is coming towards us. What, going so soon?” he went on, as the Fox began to turn away as soon as he had heard the news. “Will you not stop and say well done to the Dog on the new rule of peace for all?”

“I would be glad to do so,” said the Fox, “but I fear he may not have heard of King Lion's order.”

Clever plans often catch themselves.

The Wind and the Sun

The Wind and the Sun were arguing about which was the stronger. Suddenly they saw a man walking down the road. The Sun said: “I see a way to settle our argument. Whichever of us can make that man take off his coat shall be called the stronger. You begin.” So the Sun went behind a cloud. The Wind began to blow as hard as it could on the man. But the harder he blew, the more closely the man held his coat around him. At last the Wind had to give up in hopelessness. Then the Sun came out and shone with all his power on the man. Soon the man found it too hot to walk with his coat on.

Kindness does more than harshness.

Hercules and the Waggoner

A Waggoner was once driving a heavy load along a very muddy road. At last he came to a part of the road where the wheels sank halfway into the soft mud. The more the horses pulled, the deeper the wheels sank. So the Waggoner threw down his whip. He got down on his knees and prayed to Hercules the Strong. “O Hercules, help me in this hour of trouble,” he said. But Hercules appeared to him and said:

“Come, man, do not lie there. Get up and put your shoulder to the wheel.”

The gods help those who help themselves.

The Miser and His Gold

Once upon a time there was a Miser who used to hide his gold at the foot of a tree in his garden. But every week he used to go and dig it up and look at his gains with joy. A thief, who had noticed this, went and dug up the gold and ran away with it. When the Miser next came to look at his treasure with joy, he found nothing but the empty hole. He tore his hair and raised such a loud cry that all the people living near came around him. He told them how he used to come and visit his gold. “Did you ever take any of it out?” asked one of them.

“No,” said he, “I only came to look at it.”

“Then come again and look at the hole,” said one of them; “it will do you just as much good.”

Wealth that is never used might as well not exist.

The Man, the Boy, and the Donkey

A Man and his son were once going with their Donkey to market. As they were walking along by its side, a man from the area passed them and said: “You fools, what is a Donkey for but to ride upon?”

So the Man put the Boy on the Donkey and they went on their way. But soon they passed a group of men. One of them said: “See that lazy boy, he lets his father walk while he rides.”

So the Man ordered his Boy to get off, and got on himself. But they had not gone far when they passed two women. One of them said to the other: “Shame on that lazy rude man to let his poor little son walk along.”

Well, the Man did not know what to do. But at last he took his Boy up before him on the Donkey. By this time they had come to the town, and people passing by began to laugh and point at them. The Man stopped and asked what they were laughing at. The men said: “Are you not ashamed of yourself for loading that poor donkey of yours with you and your big son?”

The Man and Boy got off and tried to think what to do. They thought and they thought. At last they cut down a pole, tied the donkey's feet to it, and raised the pole and the donkey to their shoulders. They went along amid the laughter of all who met them. At last they came to Market Bridge. Then the Donkey got one of his feet loose and kicked out. This caused the Boy to drop his end of the pole. In the struggle the Donkey fell over the bridge. His front feet were tied together, and he was drowned.

“That will teach you,” said an old man who had followed them:

“Please all, and you will please none.”

The Fox and the Mosquitoes

A Fox after crossing a river got its tail caught in a bush, and could not move. A number of Mosquitoes saw its trouble and settled upon it. They enjoyed a good meal without being disturbed by its tail. A small animal with sharp spines walking by took pity upon the Fox and went up to him. “You are in a bad way, friend,” said the small animal; “shall I help you by driving off those Mosquitoes who are sucking your blood?”

“Thank you, Master Hedgehog,” said the Fox, “but I would rather not.”

“Why, how is that?” asked the small animal.

“Well, you see,” was the answer, “these Mosquitoes have had their fill. If you drive these away, others will come with fresh hunger and bleed me to death.”

The Fox Without a Tail

It happened that a Fox caught its tail in a trap. In struggling to free himself, he lost all of it but the stump. At first he was ashamed to show himself among his fellow foxes. But at last he decided to put a bolder face upon his bad luck. He called all the foxes to a general meeting to consider a proposal which he had to place before them. When they had gathered together, the Fox proposed that they should all do away with their tails. He pointed out how inconvenient a tail was when they were chased by their enemies, the dogs. He said how much it was in the way when they wished to sit down and have a friendly talk with one another. He failed to see any advantage in carrying about such a useless burden. “That is all very well,” said one of the older foxes; “but I do not think you would have advised us to give up our chief ornament if you had not happened to lose it yourself.”

Do not trust advice from someone who gains from it.

The One-Eyed Doe

A Doe had had the bad luck to lose one of her eyes. She could not see anyone coming toward her on that side. So to avoid any danger she always used to feed on a high cliff near the sea, with her good eye looking toward the land. By this means she could see whenever the hunters came toward her on land, and often escaped by this means. But the hunters found out that she was blind of one eye. They hired a boat, rowed under the cliff where she used to feed, and shot her from the sea. “Ah,” cried she with her dying voice,

“You cannot escape your fate.”

Belling the Cat

Long ago, the mice held a general meeting to think about what they could do to beat their common enemy, the Cat. Some said this, and some said that. At last a young mouse stood up and said he had a plan to offer, which he thought would solve the problem. “You will all agree,” he said, “that our main danger lies in the secret and false way the enemy comes near us. Now, if we could get some signal of her coming, we could easily run away from her. So I dare to suggest that we get a small bell and tie it with a ribbon around the neck of the Cat. In this way we would always know when she was near, and could easily move away while she was in the area.”

This plan was met with general cheers, until an old mouse stood up and said: “That is all very well, but who will put the bell on the Cat?” The mice looked at one another and nobody spoke. Then the old mouse said:

“It is easy to suggest impossible fixes.”

The Hare and the Tortoise

The Hare was once bragging about his speed before the other animals. “I have never yet been beaten,” he said, “when I use my full speed. I challenge anyone here to race with me.”

The Tortoise said quietly, “I accept your challenge.”

“That is a good joke,” said the Hare; “I could dance around you all the way.”

“Keep your bragging until you have won,” answered the Tortoise. “Shall we race?”

So a course was set and a start was made. The Hare rushed almost out of sight at once, but soon stopped and, to show his lack of respect for the Tortoise, lay down to sleep. The Tortoise walked on and on, and when the Hare woke from his sleep, he saw the Tortoise just near the winning post and could not run fast enough to win the race. Then said the Tortoise:

“Slow and steady wins the race.”

The Old Man and Death

An old worker, bent double with age and hard work, was gathering sticks in a forest. At last he grew so tired and without hope that he threw down the bundle of sticks, and cried out: “I cannot bear this life any longer. Ah, I wish Death would only come and take me!”

As he spoke, Death, a terrible skeleton, appeared and said to him: “What do you want, Mortal? I heard you call me.”

“Please, sir,” replied the woodcutter, “would you kindly help me to lift this bundle of sticks onto my shoulder?”

We would often be sorry if our wishes came true.

The Hare With Many Friends

A Hare was very popular with the other animals, who all said they were her friends. But one day she heard the dogs coming and hoped to escape them with the help of her many Friends. So she went to the horse, and asked him to carry her away from the dogs on his back. But he refused, saying that he had important work to do for his master. “He felt sure,” he said, “that all her other friends would come to help her.” She then asked the bull, and hoped that he would drive the dogs away with his horns. The bull replied: “I am very sorry, but I have a meeting with a lady; but I feel sure that our friend the goat will do what you want.” The goat, however, feared that his back might do her some harm if he took her on it. The ram, he felt sure, was the right friend to ask. So she went to the ram and told him the situation. The ram replied: “Another time, my dear friend. I do not like to get involved on this occasion, as dogs have been known to eat sheep as well as hares.” The Hare then asked, as a last hope, the calf, who was sorry that he was unable to help her, as he did not like to take the responsibility himself, as so many older ones than himself had refused the task. By this time the dogs were quite near, and the Hare ran away fast and luckily escaped.

He that has many friends, has no friends.

The Lion in Love

A Lion once fell in love with a beautiful young woman and asked her parents for marriage. The old people did not know what to say. They did not like to give their daughter to the Lion, yet they did not wish to make the King of Beasts angry. At last the father said: “We feel highly honoured by your Majesty’s proposal, but you see our daughter is a tender young thing, and we fear that in the strong force of your love you might possibly do her some injury. Might I dare to suggest that your Majesty should have your claws removed, and your teeth pulled out, then we would gladly consider your proposal again.” The Lion was so much in love that he had his claws cut and his big teeth taken out. But when he came again to the parents of the young girl they simply laughed in his face, and told him to do his worst.

Love can tame the wildest.

The Bundle of Sticks

An old man near death called his sons around him to give them some parting advice. He ordered his servants to bring in a bundle of sticks, and said to his eldest son: “Break it.” The son tried and tried, but with all his efforts was unable to break the bundle. The other sons also tried, but none of them succeeded. “Untie the bundle,” said the father, “and each of you take a stick.” When they had done so, he called out to them: “Now, break,” and each stick was easily broken. “You see my meaning,” said their father.

Union gives strength.

The Lion, the Fox, and the Beasts

The Lion once announced that he was sick and near death and called the animals to come and hear his last Will and Testament. So the Goat came to the Lion’s cave, and stopped there listening for a long time. Then a Sheep went in, and before she came out a Calf came up to receive the last wishes of the Lord of the Beasts. “I beg your Majesty’s pardon,” said the Fox, “but I noticed the track of the animals that have already come to you; and while I see many hoof marks going in, I see none coming out. Until the animals that have entered your cave come out again I prefer to remain in the open air.”

The Ass’s Brains

The Lion and the Fox went hunting together. The Lion, on the advice of the Fox, sent a message to the Ass. He proposed to make a family alliance between them. The Ass came to the meeting place. He was full of joy at the hope of a royal alliance. But when he came there the Lion simply jumped on the Ass. Then he said to the Fox: “Here is our dinner for today. Cleverness always has an answer ready.

The Eagle and the Arrow

An Eagle was flying high through the air. Suddenly it heard the sound of an Arrow and felt itself wounded to death. Slowly it came down to the earth, with its life blood pouring out of it. It looked down at the Arrow that had pierced it. It found that the Arrow's shaft had been given feathers from one of its own. “Alas!” it cried, as it died,

The Cat-Maiden

The gods were once arguing about whether a living being could change its nature. Jupiter said “Yes,” but Venus said “No.” So, to test the question, Jupiter turned a Cat into a Maiden. He gave her to a young man as a wife. The wedding was performed in the proper way and the young couple sat down to the wedding meal. “See,” said Jupiter, to Venus, “how well she behaves. Who could tell that yesterday she was only a Cat? Surely her nature is changed?”

“Wait a minute,” replied Venus, and let a mouse go free into the room. As soon as the bride saw this she jumped up from her seat and tried to jump on the mouse. “Ah, you see,” said Venus,

The Milkmaid and Her Pail

Patty the Milkmaid was going to market. She carried her milk in a Pail on her head. As she went along she began to think about what she would do with the money she would get for the milk. “I’ll buy some birds from Farmer Brown,” said she, “and they will lay eggs each morning. “Ah, my child,” said the mother,

“Do not count your chickens before they are hatched.”

The Horse and the Ass

A Horse and an Ass were travelling together. The Horse moved along proudly in its fine coverings. The Ass carried with difficulty the heavy weight in its baskets. “I wish I were you,” sighed the Ass; “nothing to do and well fed, and all that fine harness upon you.” Next day, however, there was a great battle. The Horse was wounded to death in the final charge of the day. His friend, the Ass, happened to pass by soon after and found him near death. “I was wrong,” said the Ass:

“Better humble safety than gilded danger.”

The Trumpeter Taken Prisoner

A Trumpeter during a battle went too near the enemy and was captured by them. They were about to go ahead and put him to death when he begged them to hear his plea for mercy. “I do not fight,” said he, “and indeed carry no weapon. I only blow this trumpet, and surely that cannot harm you. Then why should you kill me?”

“You may not fight yourself,” said the others, “but you encourage and guide your men to the fight.”

Words may be deeds.

The Buffoon and the Countryman

At a country fair there was a Buffoon who made all the people laugh by copying the cries of various animals. He finished by making a pig sound so well that the people watching thought that he had a pig hidden about him. But a Countryman who stood by said: “Call that a pig’s sound! Nothing like it. Give me till tomorrow and I will show you what it’s like.” The audience laughed. But next day, sure enough, the Countryman appeared on the stage. He put his head down and made such a terrible sound that the people watching made angry sounds and threw stones at him to make him stop. “You fools!” he cried, “see what you have been making angry sounds at,” and held up a little pig. He had been pinching its ear to make it utter the sounds.

Men often applaud a copy and make angry sounds at the real thing.

The Old Woman and the Wine-Jar

You must know that sometimes old women like a glass of wine. One of this sort once found a Wine-jar lying in the road. She eagerly went up to it hoping to find it full. But when she took it up she found that all the wine had been drunk out of it. Still she took a long sniff at the mouth of the Jar. “Ah,” she cried,

“What memories cling around the instruments of our pleasure.”

The Fox and the Goat

By an unlucky chance a Fox fell into a deep well from which he could not get out. A Goat passed by soon after, and asked the Fox what he was doing down there. “Oh, have you not heard?” said the Fox; “there is going to be a great drought, so I jumped down here in order to be sure to have water by me.
