PREFACE AND DEDICATION

As I write this, I have before me on my desk, leaning against the telephone, an old cloth doll. Dear old Raggedy Ann!

The same Raggedy Ann with which my mother played when a child.

There she sits, a little loose and loose-jointed, looking me straight in the face in a direct, honest manner, a bright light where her shoe-button eyes reflect the electric light.

Clearly Raggedy has been to a "tea party" today, for her face is covered with chocolate.

She smiles in a happy way and without stopping.

True, mice have bitten her, and they have made nests out of the soft cotton with which she has been filled. But Raggedy smiled just as widely when the mice bit her, for her smile is painted on.

What adventures you must have had, Raggedy!

What joy and happiness you have brought into this world!

And no matter what treatment you have received, how patient you have been!

What lessons of goodness and strength you might teach if you could talk; you with your wisdom of fifty-nine years. No wonder Rag Dolls are the most loved! You are so good, so patient, so easy to love.

The more you become torn, worn and loose-jointed, Rag Dolls, the more children love you.

Who knows but that Fairyland is full of old, easy-to-love Rag Dolls--soft, loose Rag Dolls who ride through all the wonders of Fairyland in the bend of arms with small hollows, held close to children's chests within which beat hearts filled with endless sunshine.

So, to the millions of children and grown-ups who have loved a Rag Doll, I dedicate these stories of Raggedy Ann.

JOHNNY GRUELLE.

INTRODUCTION

Marcella liked to play up in the attic at Grandma's old and charming house, far out in the country, for there were so many old forgotten things to find up there.

One day when Marcella was up in the attic and had played with the old spinning wheel until she had grown tired of it, she lay down on an old horse-hair sofa to rest.

"I wonder what is in that barrel, far back in the corner?" she thought, as she jumped from the sofa and climbed over two dusty trunks to the barrel standing back under the roof edge.

It was quite dark back there, so when Marcella had pulled a large bundle of things from the barrel she took them over to the small window where she could see better. There was a funny little bonnet with long white ribbons. Marcella put it on.

In an old leather bag she found a number of tin-types of strange looking men and women in old-fashioned clothes. And there was one picture of a very pretty little girl with long curls tied tightly back from her forehead and wearing a long dress and strange pantaloons which reached to her shoe-tops. And then out of the heap she pulled an old cloth doll with only one shoe-button eye and a painted nose and a smiling mouth. Her dress was of soft material, blue with pretty little flowers and dots all over it.

Forgetting everything else in the happiness of her find, Marcella caught up the cloth doll and ran downstairs to show it to Grandma.

"Well! Well! Where did you find it?" Grandma cried. "It's old Raggedy Ann!" she went on as she held the doll to her chest. "I had forgotten her. She has been in the attic for fifty years, I guess! Well! Well! Dear old Raggedy Ann! I will sew another button on her right away!" and Grandma went to the machine drawer and got her needle and thread.

Marcella watched the sewing while Grandma told how she had played with Raggedy Ann when she was a little girl.

"Now!" Grandma laughed, "Raggedy Ann, you have two fine shoe-button eyes and with them you can see the changes that have taken place in the world while you have been shut up so long in the attic! For, Raggedy Ann, you have a new playmate and owner now, and I hope you both will have as much happiness together as you and I used to have!"

Then Grandma gave Raggedy Ann to Marcella, saying very seriously, "Marcella, let me introduce my very dear friend, Raggedy Ann. Raggedy, this is my grand-daughter, Marcella!" And Grandma gave the doll a quick pull with her fingers in such a way that the cloth doll nodded her head to Marcella.

"Oh, Grandma! Thank you ever and ever so much!" Marcella cried as she gave Grandma a hug and kiss. "Raggedy Ann and I will have just loads of fun."

And this is how Raggedy Ann joined the doll family at Marcella's house, where she began the adventures of Raggedy Ann, told in the following stories.

RAGGEDY ANN LEARNS A LESSON

One day the dolls were left all to themselves.

Their little owner had placed them all around the room and told them to be nice children while she was away.

And there they sat and never even moved a finger, until their owner had left the room.

Then the soldier doll turned his head and seriously winked at Raggedy Ann.

And when the front gate clicked and the dolls knew they were alone in the house, they all got up quickly to their feet.

"Now let's have a good time!" cried the tin soldier. "Let's all go in search of something to eat!"

"Yes! Let's all go in search of something to eat!" cried all the other dolls.

"When my owner had me out playing with her this morning," said Raggedy Ann, "she carried me by a door near the back of the house and I smelled something which smelled as if it would taste delicious!"

"Then you lead the way, Raggedy Ann!" cried the French doll.

"I think it would be a good plan to choose Raggedy Ann as our leader on this trip!" said the Indian doll.

At this all the other dolls clapped their hands together and shouted, "Hurrah! Raggedy Ann will be our leader."

So Raggedy Ann, very proud indeed to have the trust and love of all the other dolls, said that she would be very glad to be their leader.

"Follow me!" she cried as her unsteady legs carried her across the floor at a lively pace.

The other dolls followed, racing about the house until they came to the pantry door. "This is the place!" cried Raggedy Ann, and sure enough, all the dolls smelled something which they knew must be very good to eat.

But none of the dolls was tall enough to open the door and, although they pushed and pulled with all their might, the door remained tightly closed.

The dolls were talking and pulling and pushing and every once in a while one would fall over and the others would step on her in their efforts to open the door. Finally Raggedy Ann drew away from the others and sat down on the floor.

When the other dolls found Raggedy Ann sitting there, moving her cloth hands through her hair, they knew she was thinking.

"Sh! Sh!" they said to each other. They went over quietly and sat down in front of Raggedy Ann.

"There must be a way to get inside," said Raggedy Ann.

"Raggedy says there must be a way to get inside!" cried all the dolls.

"I cannot seem to think clearly today," said Raggedy Ann. "It feels as if my head were torn."

At this the French doll ran to Raggedy Ann and took off her hat. "Yes, there is a tear in your head, Raggedy!" she said. She pulled a pin from her skirt and pinned up Raggedy's head. "It is not a very tidy job, for I made some folds in it!" she said.

"Oh that is much better!" cried Raggedy Ann. "Now I can think quite clearly."

"Now Raggedy can think quite clearly!" cried all the dolls.

"My thoughts must have run out through the tear before!" said Raggedy Ann.

"They must have run out before, dear Raggedy!" cried all the other dolls.

"Now that I can think so clearly," said Raggedy Ann, "I think the door must be locked. To get in we must open the lock!"

"That will be easy!" said the Dutch doll who says "Mamma" when he is tipped backward and forward. "For we will have the brave tin soldier shoot the key out of the lock!"

"I can easily do that!" cried the tin soldier, as he raised his gun.

"Oh, Raggedy Ann!" cried the French doll. "Please do not let him shoot!"

"No!" said Raggedy Ann. "We must think of a quieter way!"

After thinking quite hard for a moment, Raggedy Ann jumped up and said: "I have it!" She caught up the Jumping Jack and held him up to the door. Then Jack slid up his stick and opened the lock.

Then the dolls all pushed and the door swung open.

My! Such a rush! The dolls piled over one another in their wish to be the first at the treats.

They crowded upon the shelves and in their eagerness spilled a pitcher of cream. It ran all over the French doll's dress.

The Indian doll found some corn bread. He dipped it in the sweet syrup and sat down for a good feast.

A jar of berry jam was knocked over. The dolls ate from it until their faces were all purple.

The tin soldier fell from the shelf three times and bent one of his tin legs. But he climbed right back up again.

Never had the dolls had so much fun and excitement. They had all eaten their fill when they heard the click of the front gate.

They did not take time to climb from the shelves. They all rolled or jumped off to the floor and ran back to their room as fast as they could. They left a trail of bread crumbs and jam along the way.

Just as their mistress came into the room the dolls dropped in whatever positions they happened to be in.

"This is funny!" cried Mistress. "They were all left sitting in their places around the room! I wonder if Fido has been shaking them up!" Then she saw Raggedy Ann's face and picked her up. "Why Raggedy Ann, you are all sticky! I do believe you are covered with jam!" Mistress tasted Raggedy Ann's hand. "Yes! It is JAM! Shame on you, Raggedy Ann! You have been in the pantry and all the others, too!" With this the dolls' mistress dropped Raggedy Ann on the floor and left the room.

When she came back she had on an apron and her sleeves were rolled up.

She picked up all the sticky dolls and put them in a basket. She carried them out under the apple tree in the garden.

There she had placed her little tub and wringer. She took the dolls one at a time and rubbed them with a brush. She moved them up and down and this way and that in the soap bubbles until they were clean.

Then she hung them all out on the clothes-line in the sunshine to dry.

There the dolls hung all day, swinging and twisting about as the breeze moved the clothes-line.

"I do believe she rubbed my face so hard she wore off my smile!" said Raggedy Ann, after an hour of silence.

"No, it is still there!" said the tin soldier, as the wind turned him around so he could see Raggedy. "But I do believe my arms will never work without squeaking. They feel so rusted," he added.

Just then the wind twisted the little Dutch doll and loosened his clothes-pin. He fell to the grass below with a soft bump. As he rolled over he said, "Mamma!" in a squeaky voice.

Late in the afternoon the back door opened and the little mistress came out with a table and chairs. After setting the table she took all the dolls from the line and placed them around the table.

They had lemonade with grape jelly in it, which made it a beautiful light purple color. They also had little "Baby-teeny-weeny-cookies" with powdered sugar on them.

After this lovely dinner, the dolls were taken in the house. There they had their hair brushed and nice clean nightclothes put on.

Then they were placed in their beds and Mistress kissed each one good night and walked quietly from the room.

All the dolls lay as still as mice for a few minutes. Then Raggedy Ann raised up on her cotton-stuffed elbows and said: "I have been thinking!"

"Sh!" said all the other dolls, "Raggedy has been thinking!"

"Yes," said Raggedy Ann, "I have been thinking. Our mistress gave us the nice dinner out under the trees to teach us a lesson. She wished us to know that we could have had all the treats we wished, whenever we wished, if we had behaved ourselves. And our lesson was that we must never take without asking what we could always have for the asking! So let us all remember and try never again to do anything which might cause those who love us any unhappiness!"

"Let us all remember," said all the other dolls together.

And Raggedy Ann, with a merry twinkle in her shoe-button eyes, lay back in her little bed. Her cotton head was filled with thoughts of love and happiness.

RAGGEDY ANN AND THE WASHING

"Why, Dinah! How could you!"

Mamma looked out of the window and saw Marcella run up to Dinah. Marcella took something out of her hand and then put her head in her arm and began to cry.

"What is the trouble, Dear?" Mamma asked. She came out the door and knelt beside the little figure shaking with sobs.

Marcella held out Raggedy Ann. But such a funny looking Raggedy Ann!

Mamma had to smile in spite of her sympathy, for Raggedy Ann looked ridiculous!

Dinah's big eyes moved in a worried way. Marcella had taken Raggedy Ann from Dinah's hand. She cried, "Why, Dinah! How could you?"

Dinah did not quite understand. She loved Marcella very much, so she was worried.

Raggedy Ann was not sad at all. She felt she must look very funny. She kept smiling, but with a bigger smile than ever before.

Raggedy Ann knew just how it all happened. Her one remaining shoe-button eye shone bright.

She remembered that morning. Marcella came to the nursery to take the night clothes from the dolls and dress them. She had been angry.

Raggedy Ann thought at the time, "Perhaps she had climbed out of bed the wrong way!" Marcella complained to each doll as she dressed them.

And when it was Raggedy's time to be dressed, Marcella was very angry. She had cut her finger on a pin when dressing the French doll.

So when Marcella heard the little girl next door calling to her, she ran out of the nursery. She threw Raggedy Ann away from her as she ran.

Now it happened that Raggedy fell into the clothes basket. There she lay all folded up in a tight ball.

A few minutes later, Dinah came through the hall with her arms full of clothes. She piled them in the basket on top of Raggedy Ann.

Then Dinah carried the basket out in back of the house where she did the washing.

Dinah dumped all the clothes into the big pot and poured water on them.

The pot was then placed upon the stove.

When the water began to get warm, Raggedy Ann moved around. She climbed up among the clothes to the top of the pot to look out. There was too much steam and she could see nothing. For that matter, Dinah could not see Raggedy Ann either, because of the steam.

So Dinah used an old broom handle to stir the clothes in the pot. The clothes and Raggedy Ann were stirred and turned around until all were fully boiled.

Dinah took the clothes a piece at a time from the pot and washed them hard. Finally she came upon Raggedy Ann.

Now Dinah did not know that Marcella had placed Raggedy in the clothes basket to be washed. So she soaped Raggedy well and rubbed her up and down over the rough wash-board.

Two buttons from the back of Raggedy's dress came off. One of Raggedy Ann's shoe-button eyes was loosened as Dinah gave her face a final wash.

Then Dinah put Raggedy Ann's feet in the wringer and turned the handle. It was hard work getting Raggedy through the wringer, but Dinah was very strong. And of course it happened! Raggedy Ann came through as flat as a pancake.

It was just then that Marcella returned and saw Raggedy.

"Why, Dinah! How could you!" Marcella cried as she took the flattened Raggedy Ann from the confused Dinah's hand.

Mamma patted Marcella's hand and soon gently persuaded her to stop crying.

Dinah explained that the first she knew of Raggedy being in the wash was when she took her from the pot. Then Marcella began crying again.

"It was all my fault, Mamma!" she cried. "I remember now that I threw dear old Raggedy Ann from me as I ran out the door. She must have fallen in the clothes basket! Oh dear! Oh dear!" and she hugged Raggedy Ann tight.

Mamma did not tell Marcella that she had been angry and bad. She knew Marcella felt very sorry. Instead Mamma put her arms around her and said,

"Just see how Raggedy Ann takes it! She does not seem to be unhappy!"

Marcella brushed her tears away and looked at Raggedy Ann. She was flat as a pancake and had a happy smile upon her painted face. Marcella had to laugh. And Mamma and Dinah had to laugh too, for Raggedy Ann's smile was almost twice as broad as it had been before.

"Just let me hang Miss Raggedy on the line in the bright sunshine for half an hour," said Dinah, "and you will not know her when she comes off!"

So Raggedy Ann was pinned to the clothes-line, out in the bright sunshine. She moved and turned in the breeze and listened to the talk of the robins in a nearby tree.

Every once in a while Dinah went out and rolled and patted Raggedy until her cotton stuffing was soft and dry and fluffy. Her head and arms and legs were nice and round again.

Then she took Raggedy Ann into the house and showed Marcella and Mamma how clean and sweet she was.

Marcella took Raggedy Ann right up to the nursery. She told all the dolls just what had happened. She told them how sorry she was that she had been so angry and cross when she dressed them. The dolls said never a word. They looked at their little mistress with love in their eyes as she sat in the little red rocking chair and held Raggedy Ann tightly in her arms.

And Raggedy Ann's remaining shoe-button eye looked up at her little mistress in rather a bold manner. But upon her face was the same old smile of happiness, good humor and love.

RAGGEDY ANN AND THE KITE

Raggedy Ann watched the preparations with interest.

A number of sticks were being tied together with strings and covered with light cloth.

Raggedy Ann heard some of the boys talk of "The Kite," so Raggedy Ann knew this must be a kite.

A tail had been tied to the kite. A large ball of heavy string was tied to the front. Then one of the boys held the kite up in the air and another boy walked off, unwinding the ball of string.

There was a nice breeze blowing, so the boy with the string called, "Let 'er go" and started running.

Marcella held Raggedy up so that she could watch the kite sail through the air.

How nicely it climbed! But suddenly the kite acted strangely. All the children shouted advice to the boy with the ball of string. The kite began darting this way and that. Finally it made four or five loop-the-loops and crashed to the ground.

"It needs more tail on it!" one boy shouted.

Then the children asked each other where they might get more rags to tie to the tail of the kite.

"Let's tie Raggedy Ann to the tail!" suggested Marcella. "I know she would enjoy a trip 'way up in the sky!"

The boys all shouted with delight at this new suggestion. So Raggedy Ann was tied to the tail of the kite.

This time the kite rose straight in the air and stayed steady. The boy with the ball of string unwound it until the kite and Raggedy Ann were far, far up and far away. How Raggedy Ann enjoyed being up there! She could see for miles and miles! And how tiny the children looked!

Suddenly a great rush of wind came and carried Raggedy Ann far out behind the kite! She could hear the wind singing on the string as the pull increased.

Suddenly Raggedy Ann felt something tear. It was the cloth to which she was tied. As each rush of wind caught her the tear grew wider.

When Marcella watched Raggedy Ann rise high above the field, she wondered how much Raggedy Ann enjoyed it, and wished that she, too, might have gone along. But after the kite had been up in the air for five or ten minutes, Marcella grew unable to sit still. Kites were rather boring. There was more fun in tea parties out under the apple tree.

"Will you please pull down the kite now?" she asked the boy with the string. "I want Raggedy Ann."

"Let her ride up there!" the boy replied. "We'll bring her home when we pull down the kite! We're going to get another ball of string and let her go higher!"

Marcella did not like to leave Raggedy Ann with the boys, so she sat down upon the ground to wait until they pulled down the kite.

But while Marcella watched Raggedy Ann, a dot in the sky, she could not see the wind tearing the cloth to which Raggedy was tied.

Suddenly the cloth broke apart and Raggedy Ann went sailing away as the wind caught in her skirts.

Marcella jumped from the ground, too surprised to say anything. The kite, released from the weight of Raggedy Ann began moving quickly and diving to the ground.

"We'll get her for you!" some of the boys said when they saw Marcella's troubled face, and they started running in the direction Raggedy Ann had fallen. Marcella and the other girls ran with them. They ran, and they ran, and they ran, and at last they found the kite upon the ground with one of the sticks broken, but they could not find Raggedy Ann anywhere.

"She must have fallen almost in your yard!" a boy said to Marcella, "for the kite was directly over here when the doll fell!"

Marcella was very sad. She went in the house and lay on the bed. Mamma went out with the children and tried to find Raggedy Ann, but Raggedy Ann was nowhere to be seen.

When Daddy came home in the evening he tried to find Raggedy, but met with no success. Marcella had eaten hardly any dinner, nor could she be comforted by Mamma or Daddy. The other dolls in the children's room lay forgotten and were not put to bed that night, for Marcella lay and cried and tossed about her bed.

Finally she said a little prayer for Raggedy Ann, and went to sleep. And as she slept Marcella dreamed that the little magic people came and took Raggedy Ann with them to their magic land for a visit, and then sent Raggedy Ann home to her. She woke with a cry. Of course Mamma came to her bed right away and said that Daddy would offer a reward in the morning for the return of Raggedy.

"It was all my fault, Mamma!" Marcella said. "I should not have offered the boys dear old Raggedy Ann to tie on the tail of the kite! But I just know the little magic people will send her back."

Mamma took her in her arms and comforted her with kind words, although she felt indeed that Raggedy Ann was truly lost and would never be found again.

Now, where do you suppose Raggedy Ann was all this time?

When Raggedy Ann dropped from the kite, the wind caught in her skirts and carried her along until she fell in the fork of the large tree directly over Marcella's house. When Raggedy Ann fell with a heavy sound, face up in the fork of the tree, two birds who had a nest near by flew away making noise.

Presently the birds returned and argued at Raggedy Ann for laying so close to their nest, but Raggedy Ann only smiled at them and did not move.

When the birds quieted down and stopped their arguing, one of them jumped up closer to Raggedy Ann in order to investigate.

It was Mamma Bird. She called to Daddy Bird and told him to come. "See the nice thread! We could use it to line the nest with," she said.

So the birds jumped closer to Raggedy Ann and asked if they might have some of her thread hair to line their nest. Raggedy Ann smiled at them. So the two birds pulled and tugged at Raggedy Ann's thread hair until they had enough to line their nest nice and soft.

Evening came and the birds sang their good night songs, and Raggedy Ann watched the stars come out, shine all night and disappear in the morning light. In the morning the birds again pulled thread from Raggedy Ann's head, and made her loose so she could look over the side of the branch, and when the sun came up Raggedy Ann saw she was in the trees in her own yard.

Now before she could eat any breakfast, Marcella started out to find Raggedy Ann. And, it was Marcella herself who found her. And this is how she did it.

Mamma Bird had seen Marcella with Raggedy Ann out in the yard many times, so she began calling "Cheery! Cheery!" and Daddy Bird started calling "Cheery! Cheery! Cheer up! Cheer up! Cheerily Cheerily! Cheery! Cheery!" And Marcella looking up into the tree above the house to see the birds, discovered Raggedy Ann looking over the branch at her.

Oh, how her heart beat with happiness. "Here is Raggedy Ann," she shouted.

And Mamma and Daddy came out and saw Raggedy smiling at them, and Daddy got the long pole and climbed out of the top window and pushed Raggedy Ann out of the tree and she fell right into Marcella's arms where she was hugged in a tight embrace.

"You'll never go up on a kite again, Raggedy Ann!" said Marcella, "for I felt so lost without you. I will never let you leave me again."

So Raggedy Ann went into the house and had breakfast with her little owner and Mamma and Daddy smiled at each other when they looked through the door into the breakfast room, for Raggedy Ann's smile was wide and very yellow. Marcella, her heart full of happiness, was feeding Raggedy Ann part of her egg.

RAGGEDY ANN RESCUES FIDO

It was almost midnight and the dolls were asleep in their beds; all except Raggedy Ann.

Raggedy lay there, her shoe-button eyes staring straight up at the ceiling. Every once in a while Raggedy Ann ran her cloth hand up through her thread hair. She was thinking.

When she had thought for a long, long time, Raggedy Ann raised herself on her weak elbows and said, "I have thought it all out."

At this the other dolls shook each other and got up saying, "Listen! Raggedy has thought it all out!"

"Tell us what you have been thinking, dear Raggedy," said the tin soldier. "We hope they were pleasant thoughts."

"Not very pleasant thoughts!" said Raggedy, as she wiped a tear from her shoe-button eyes. "You have not seen Fido all day, have you?"

"Not since early this morning," the French dolly said.

"It has troubled me," said Raggedy. "If my head was not filled with lovely new white cotton, I am sure it would have hurt with the worry! This afternoon Mistress took me into the living-room. She was crying. I heard her mamma say, 'We will find him! He is sure to come home soon!' I knew they were talking of Fido! He must be lost!"

The tin soldier jumped out of bed and ran over to Fido's basket. His tin feet clicked on the floor as he went. "He is not here," he said.

"When I was sitting in the window around noon-time," said the Indian doll, "I saw Fido and a yellow thin dog playing out on the lawn. They ran out through a hole in the fence!"

"That was Priscilla's dog, Peterkins!" said the French doll.

"I know poor Mistress is very sad about Fido," said the Dutch doll. "I was in the dining-room at supper-time. I heard her daddy tell her to eat her supper and he would go out and find Fido. But I had forgotten all about it until now."

"That is the trouble with all of us except Raggedy Ann!" cried the little penny doll, in a high voice. "She has to think for all of us!"

"I think it would be a good plan for us to show our love for Mistress and try and find Fido!" said Raggedy.

"It is a good plan, Raggedy Ann!" cried all the dolls. "Tell us how to start about it."

"Well, first let us go out upon the lawn and see if we can follow the dogs' tracks!" said Raggedy.

"I can follow them easily!" the Indian doll said. "Indians are good at following tracks!"

"Then let us waste no more time in talking!" said Raggedy Ann, as she jumped from bed, followed by the rest.

The nursery window was open. The dolls helped each other up on the window ledge and then jumped to the soft grass below. They fell in all sorts of strange positions, but of course the fall did not hurt them.

At the hole in the fence the Indian doll found the tracks of the two dogs. The dolls followed behind him in a line until they came to Peterkins' house. Peterkins was surprised to see the strange little figures in white night clothes come up the path to the dog house.

Peterkins was too large to sleep in the nursery, so he had a nice comfortable dog-house under the grape arbor.

"Come in," Peterkins said when he saw and recognized the dolls. So all the dollies went into Peterkins' house and sat about while Raggedy told him why they had come.

"It has worried me, too!" said Peterkins. "But I had no way of telling your mistress where Fido was, for she cannot understand dog language! You see," Peterkins continued, "Fido and I were having the grandest play over in the park. A great big man with a funny thing on the end of a stick came running towards us. We barked at him. Fido thought he was trying to play with us and went up too close. Do you know, that bad man caught Fido in the thing at the end of the stick! He carried him to a wagon and put him in with a lot of other dogs!"

"The Dog Catcher!" cried Raggedy Ann.

"Yes!" said Peterkins, as he wiped his eyes with his paws. "It was the dog catcher! I followed the wagon at a distance. I saw him put all the dogs into a big wire pen, so that none could get out!"

"Then you know the way there, Peterkins?" asked Raggedy Ann.

"Yes, I can find it easily," Peterkins said.

"Then show us the way!" Raggedy Ann cried, "for we must try to rescue Fido."

So Peterkins led the way up alleys and across streets. The dolls all pattered along behind him. It was a strange procession. Once a strange dog ran out at them, but Peterkins told him to mind his own business. The strange dog returned to his own yard.

At last they came to the dog catcher's place. Some of the dogs in the pen were barking at the moon. Others were whining and crying.

There was Fido, all covered with mud. His pretty red ribbon was dragging on the ground. My, but he was glad to see the dolls and Peterkins! All the dogs came to the side of the pen and twisted their heads from side to side. They gazed in wonder at the strange figures of the dolls.

"We will try and let you out," said Raggedy Ann.

At this all the dogs barked happily.

Then Raggedy Ann, the other dolls and Peterkins went to the gate.

The catch was too high for Raggedy Ann to reach. But Peterkins held Raggedy Ann in his mouth and stood up on his back legs so that she could raise the catch.

When the catch was raised, the dogs were so eager to get out. They pushed and jumped against the gate so hard it flew open. This knocked Peterkins and Raggedy Ann into the mud. Such a yapping and barking was never heard in the neighborhood as when the dogs poured out of the pen. They jumped over one another and scrambled about in the mad rush out the gate.

Fido picked himself up from where the large dogs had rolled him. He helped Raggedy Ann to her feet. He, Peterkins, and all the dolls ran after the pack of dogs. They turned the corner just as the dog catcher came running out of the house in his nightgown to see what was causing the trouble.

He stopped in surprise when he saw the line of dolls in white night clothes pattering down the alley. He could not imagine what they were.

Well, you may be sure the dolls thanked Peterkins for his kind help. They and Fido ran on home, for a faint light was beginning to show in the east where the sun was getting ready to come up.

When they got to their own home they found an old chair out in the yard. After a great deal of work they finally pulled it to the window. In this way they managed to get into the nursery again.

Fido was very grateful to Raggedy Ann and the other dolls. Before he went to his basket he gave them each a lick on the cheek.

The dolls quickly climbed into bed and pulled up the covers. They were very sleepy. But just as they were falling asleep, Raggedy Ann sat up and said, "If my legs and arms were not filled with nice clean cotton, I feel sure they would hurt. Mamma helped Marcella put all the dolls in a circle around the bed. Now they could all see what was in the bundle.

"A dear soft baby brother for Mistress!" said Uncle Clem.

RAGGEDY ANN AND THE CHICKENS

When Marcella was called into the house, she left Raggedy sitting on the chicken yard fence. "Now you sit quietly and do not move," Marcella told Raggedy Ann. "If you move you may fall and hurt yourself!"

So Raggedy Ann sat quietly, just as Marcella told her. But she smiled at the chickens, because she had fallen many times and it had never hurt her at all. She was filled with nice soft cotton, you see.

So there she sat until a tiny little humming-bird came looking for flower honey. It hummed close to Raggedy Ann's head and stayed near the tall Hollyhocks.

Raggedy Ann turned her rag head to see the humming-bird and lost her balance. Down she went with a plump sound, among the chickens.

The chickens ran in all directions, all except Old Ironsides, the rooster.

He raised his neck feathers and put his head down close to the ground. He made a strange whistling noise as he looked fiercely at Raggedy Ann.

But Raggedy Ann only smiled at Old Ironsides, the rooster. She ran her rag hand through her yarn hair, because she did not fear him.

And then something strange happened. When she made this motion, the old rooster jumped up in the air. He kicked his feet out in front and knocked Raggedy Ann over and over.

When Raggedy Ann stopped rolling, she waved her apron at the rooster and cried, "Shoo!" But instead of going away, Old Ironsides upset her again.

Now two old hens had been watching the rooster jump at Raggedy. They ran up. One old hen placed herself before the rooster. The other old hen caught hold of Raggedy's apron and dragged her into the chicken coop.

It was dark inside. Raggedy could not tell what was going on as she felt herself being pulled up over the nests.

But finally Raggedy could sit up, because the old hen had stopped pulling her. Her shoe-button eyes were very good, so she soon made out the shape of the old hen in front of her.

"My! That is the hardest work I have done in a long time!" said the old hen, when she could catch her breath. "I was afraid Mr. Rooster would tear your dress and apron!"

"That was a strange game he was playing, Mrs. Hen," said Raggedy Ann.

The old hen laughed quietly deep in her throat. "Gracious me! He was not playing a game. He was fighting you!"

"Fighting!" cried Raggedy Ann in surprise.

"Oh yes, indeed!" the old hen answered. "Old Ironsides, the rooster, thought you meant to harm some of the children chickens. He was fighting you!"

"I am sorry that I fell inside the pen. I would not harm anything," Raggedy Ann said.

"If we tell you a secret, you must promise not to tell your mistress!" said the old hens.

"I promise! Cross my candy heart!" said Raggedy Ann.

Then the two old hens took Raggedy Ann far back to the farthest corner of the chicken coop. There, behind a box, they had built two nests. Each old hen had ten eggs in her nest.

"If your folks hear of it, they will take the eggs!" said the hens. "And then we could not raise our families!"

Raggedy Ann felt the eggs and they were nice and warm.

"We just left the nests when you fell into the pen!" explained the old hens.

"But how can the eggs grow if you sit upon them?" said Raggedy. "If Fido sits on any of the garden, the plants will not grow, Mistress says!"

"Eggs are different!" one old hen explained. "To make the eggs hatch properly, we must sit on them three weeks and not let them get cold at any time!"

"And at the end of the three weeks do the eggs sprout?" asked Raggedy Ann.

"You must be thinking of eggplant!" cried one old hen. "These eggs hatch at the end of three weeks. They do not sprout. And then we have a lovely family of soft downy chickies. Little puff balls that we can cuddle under our wings and love dearly!"

"Have you been sitting upon the eggs very long?" Raggedy asked.

"Neither one of us has kept track of the time," said one hen. "So we do not know! You see, we never leave the nests except once in a while to get a drink and to eat a little. So we can hardly tell when it is day and when it is night."

"We were going out to get a drink when you fell in the pen!" said one old hen. "Now we will have to sit upon the eggs and warm them up again!"

The two old hens spread their feathers and settled down upon the nests.

"When you get them good and warm, I would be glad to sit upon the eggs to keep them warm until you get something to eat and drink!" said Raggedy. So the two old hens walked out of the coop to finish their meal, which had been interrupted by Raggedy's fall. While they were gone, Raggedy Ann sat quietly upon the warm eggs. Suddenly down beneath her she heard something go, "Pick, pick!" "I hope it is not a mouse!" Raggedy Ann said to herself, when she felt something move. "I wish the old hens would come back." But when they came back and saw the puzzled expression on her face, they cried, "What is it?"

Raggedy Ann got to her feet and looked down. There were several little soft, cuddly baby chickies, round as little puff-balls.

"Cheep! Cheep! Cheep!" they cried when Raggedy stepped out of the nest.

"Baby Chicks!" Raggedy cried, as she bent down and picked up one of the little puff-balls. "They want to be cuddled!"

The two old hens got on the nests. Their eyes shone with happiness. They spread out their soft warm feathers. "The other eggs will hatch soon!" they said.

So Raggedy helped the two hens hatch the rest of the chickies for several days. Just as they finished, Marcella came inside and looked around.

"How in the world did you get in here, Raggedy Ann?" she cried. "I have been looking all about for you! Did the chickens drag you in here?"

Both old hens down behind the box clucked softly to the chickies beneath them. Marcella heard them.

She lifted the box away and gave a little cry of surprise and happiness.

"Oh you dear old Hennypennies!" she cried. She lifted both old hens from their nests. "You have hidden your nests away back here. Now you have one, two, three, four--twenty chickies!" As she counted them, Marcella placed them in her apron. Then she caught up Raggedy Ann and placed her over the new little chickies.

"Come on, old Hennypennies!" she said. She went out of the coop with the two old hens clucking at her heels.

Marcella called Daddy. Daddy rolled two barrels out under one of the trees and made a nice bed in each. Then he nailed slats across the front and left a place for a door. Each Hennypennie was then given ten little chickies and shut up in the barrel. All the dolls were happy when they heard of Raggedy's adventure. They did not have to wait long before they were all taken out to see the new chickies.

RAGGEDY ANN AND THE MOUSE

Jeanette was a new wax doll. Like Henny, the Dutch doll, she could say "Mamma" when anyone tipped her backward or forward. She had lovely golden brown curls of real hair. It could be combed and braided, or curled or fluffed without tangling. Raggedy Ann was very proud when Jeanette came to live with the dolls.

But now Raggedy Ann was very angry. In fact, Raggedy Ann had just ripped two stitches out of the top of her head. She took her rag hands and pulled her rag face down into a frown. But when she let go of the frown, her face stretched right back into its usual cheery smile.

"If I catch the culprit, I will--well, I don't know what I will do with him!" said the tin soldier. He could be very fierce at times, although he was seldom cross.

"Here is the hole he came from!" cried Uncle Clem from the other end of the nursery. "Come, see!"

All the dolls ran to where Uncle Clem was, down on his hands and knees.

"This must be the place!" said Raggedy Ann. "We will plug up the hole with something, so he will not come out again!"

The dolls hunted around and brought rags and pieces of paper. They pushed them into the mouse's doorway.

"I thought I heard nibbling last night," one of the penny dolls said. "You know I begged for an extra piece of pie last evening, when Mistress had me at the table. It kept me awake!"

While the dolls were talking, Marcella ran down-stairs with Jeanette. She told Daddy and Mamma. They came up-stairs with Marcella and hunted around until they discovered the mouse's doorway.

"Oh, why couldn't it have chewed on me?" Raggedy Ann asked herself when she saw Marcella's sorrowful face. Raggedy Ann was never selfish.

"Daddy will take Jeanette down-town with him and have her fixed up as good as new," said Mamma. So Jeanette was wrapped in soft tissue paper and taken away.

Later in the day Marcella came bouncing into the nursery with a surprise for the dolls. It was a dear fuzzy little kitten.

Marcella introduced the kitten to all the dolls.

"Her name is Boots, because she has four little white feet!" said Marcella. So Boots, the happy little creature, played with the penny dolls. She scraped them over the floor and peeped out from behind chairs. She pounced upon them as if they were mice. The penny dolls enjoyed it hugely.

When Marcella was not in the nursery, Raggedy Ann wrestled with Boots. They would roll over and over upon the floor. Boots had her front feet around Raggedy Ann's neck and kicked with her hind feet.

Then Boots would arch her back and pretend she was very angry. She walked sideways until she was close to Raggedy. Then she would jump at her. Over and over they would roll, their heads hitting the floor bumpity-bump.

Boots slept in the nursery that night and was lonely for her Mamma. It was the first time she had been away from home.

Even though her bed was right on top of Raggedy Ann, she could not sleep. But Raggedy Ann was very glad to have Boots sleep with her, even if she was heavy. When Boots began crying for her Mamma, Raggedy Ann comforted her and soon Boots went to sleep.

One day Jeanette came home. She had a new coating of wax on her face and she was as beautiful as ever.

Now, by this time Boots was one of the family and did not cry at night. Besides, Boots was told of the mouse in the corner and how he had eaten Jeanette's wax. So she promised to sleep with one eye open.

Late that night when Boots was the only one awake, out popped a tiny mouse from the hole. Boots jumped after the mouse and hit against the toy piano. She made the keys tinkle so loudly it awakened the dolls.

They ran over to where Boots sat growling with the tiny mouse in her mouth.

My! how the mouse was squeaking!

Raggedy Ann did not like to hear it squeak. But she did not wish Jeanette to have her wax face chewed again, either.

So Raggedy Ann said to the tiny little mouse, "You should have known better than to come here when Boots is with us. Why don't you go out in the barn and live where you will not destroy anything of value?"

"I did not know!" squeaked the little mouse. "This is the first time I have ever been here!"

"Aren't you the little mouse who nibbled Jeanette's wax face?" Raggedy Ann asked.

"It was too bad they forgot Freddy," said Marcella.
