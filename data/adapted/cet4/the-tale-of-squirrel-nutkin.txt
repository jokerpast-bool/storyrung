A STORY FOR NORAH

This is a Tale about a tail. It was the tail of a little red squirrel, and his name was Nutkin.

He had a brother called Twinkleberry, and a great many cousins. They lived in a wood at the edge of a lake.

In the middle of the lake there is an island covered with trees and nut bushes. Among those trees stands a hollow oak-tree. It is the house of an owl who is called Old Brown.

One fall the nuts were ripe. The leaves on the hazel bushes were golden and green. Nutkin and Twinkleberry and all the other little squirrels came out of the wood. They went down to the edge of the lake.

They made little rafts out of small branches. They rowed away over the water to Owl Island to gather nuts.

Each squirrel had a little sack and a large oar. Each spread out his tail for a sail.

They also took an offering of three fat mice as a present for Old Brown. They put them down upon his door-step.

Then Twinkleberry and the other little squirrels each made a low bow. They said in a kind way:

"Old Mr. Brown, will you let us gather nuts upon your island?"

But Nutkin was too rude in his manners. He jumped up and down like a little red _cherry_. He sang:

"Riddle me, riddle me, rot-tot-tote! A little tiny man, in a red red coat! A staff in his hand, and a stone in his throat; If you'll tell me this riddle, I'll give you a coin."

Now this riddle is as old as the hills. Mr. Brown paid no attention at all to Nutkin.

He shut his eyes and would not change his mind. He went to sleep.

The squirrels filled their little sacks with nuts. They sailed away home in the evening.

But next morning they all came back again to Owl Island. Twinkleberry and the others brought a fine fat mole. They laid it on the stone in front of Old Brown's doorway. They said:

"Mr. Brown, will you kindly let us gather some more nuts?"

But Nutkin had no respect. He began to dance up and down. He tickled old Mr. Brown with a _nettle_ and sang:

"Old Mr. B! Riddle-me-ree! Hitty Pitty within the wall, Hitty Pitty without the wall; If you touch Hitty Pitty, Hitty Pitty will bite you!"

Mr. Brown woke up suddenly. He carried the mole into his house.

He shut the door in Nutkin's face. Soon a little thread of blue _smoke_ from a wood fire came up from the top of the tree. Nutkin looked through the key-hole and sang:

"A house full, a hole full! And you cannot gather a bowl-full!"

The squirrels searched for nuts all over the island. They filled their little sacks.

But Nutkin gathered oak-apples, yellow and scarlet. He sat upon a beech-stump playing marbles. He watched the door of old Mr. Brown.

On the third day the squirrels got up very early. They went fishing. They caught seven fat minnows as a present for Old Brown.

They rowed over the lake. They landed under a crooked chestnut tree on Owl Island.

Twinkleberry and six other little squirrels each carried a fat minnow. But Nutkin had no nice manners. He brought no present at all. He ran in front, singing:

"The man in the wilderness said to me, 'How many strawberries grow in the sea?' I answered him as I thought good: 'As many red herrings as grow in the wood.'"

But old Mr. Brown took no interest in riddles. He did not care even when the answer was given to him.

On the fourth day the squirrels brought a present of six fat beetles. They were as good as plums in _plum-pudding_ for Old Brown. Each beetle was wrapped up carefully in a dock-leaf. Each was fastened with a pine-needle pin.

But Nutkin sang as rudely as ever:

"Old Mr. B! riddle-me-ree! Flour of England, fruit of Spain, Met together in a shower of rain; Put in a bag tied round with a string, If you'll tell me this riddle, I'll give you a ring!"

This was silly of Nutkin. He had not got any ring to give to Old Brown.

The other squirrels hunted up and down the nut bushes. But Nutkin gathered robin's pincushions off a briar bush. He stuck them full of pine-needle pins.

On the fifth day the squirrels brought a present of wild honey. It was so sweet and sticky that they licked their fingers as they put it down upon the stone. They had stolen it out of a bumble _bees'_ nest on the very top of the hill.

But Nutkin skipped up and down, singing:

"Hum-a-bum! buzz! buzz! Hum-a-bum buzz! As I went over Tipple-tine I met a flock of pretty pigs; Some yellow-nacked, some yellow backed! They were the very prettiest pigs That e'er went over Tipple-tine."

Old Mr. Brown turned up his eyes in disgust at the rudeness of Nutkin.

But he ate up the honey!

The squirrels filled their little sacks with nuts.

But Nutkin sat upon a big flat rock. He played ninepins with a crab apple and green fir-cones.

On the sixth day, which was Saturday, the squirrels came again for the last time. They brought a new-laid _egg_ in a little rush basket. It was a last parting present for Old Brown.

But Nutkin ran in front laughing. He shouted:

"Humpty Dumpty lies in the beck, With a white counterpane round his neck, Forty doctors and forty wrights, Cannot put Humpty Dumpty to rights!"

Now old Mr. Brown took an interest in eggs. He opened one eye and shut it again. But still he did not speak.

Nutkin became more and more rude:

"Old Mr. B! Old Mr. B! Hickamore, Hackamore, on the King's kitchen door; All the King's horses, and all the King's men, Couldn't drive Hickamore, Hackamore, Off the King's kitchen door."

Nutkin danced up and down like a _sunbeam_. But still Old Brown said nothing at all.

Nutkin began again:

"Arthur O'Bower has broken his band, He comes roaring up the land! The King of Scots with all his power, Cannot turn Arthur of the Bower!"

Nutkin made a whirring noise to sound like the _wind_. He took a running jump right onto the head of Old Brown!

Then all at once there was a flutterment and a scufflement and a loud "Squeak!"

The other squirrels ran away into the bushes.

They came back very carefully. They looked round the tree. There was Old Brown sitting on his door-step, quite still, with his eyes closed. He looked as if nothing had happened.

* * * * *

_But Nutkin was in his waistcoat pocket!_

This looks like the end of the story. But it isn't.

Old Brown carried Nutkin into his house. He held him up by the tail. He wanted to take off his skin. But Nutkin pulled so hard that his tail broke in two. He ran quickly up the stairs. He escaped out of the small window at the top of the house.

And even today, if you meet Nutkin up a tree, and ask him a question that is hard to answer, he will throw sticks at you. He will hit his feet on the ground. He will speak in a mad way, and shout--

"Cuck-cuck-cuck-cur-r-r-cuck-k-k!"
