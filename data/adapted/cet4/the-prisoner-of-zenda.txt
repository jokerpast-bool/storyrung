CHAPTER 1. The Rassendylls--With a Word on the Elphbergs

The Rassendylls--With a Word on the Elphbergs

“I wonder when in the world you are going to do anything, Rudolf?” said my brother's wife.

“My dear Rose,” I answered, putting down my egg-spoon, “why in the world should I do anything? My position is a comfortable one. I have an income nearly enough for my wants (no person's income is ever quite enough, you know). I enjoy a social position that many would want: I am brother to Lord Burlesdon, and brother-in-law to that lovely lady, his wife. See, it is enough!”

“You are nine-and-twenty,” she said, “and you have done nothing but--”

“Move about? It is true. Our family does not need to do things.”

This remark of mine rather made Rose angry. Everybody knows (and so there can be no harm in saying it) that she is pretty and talented, but her family is hardly of the same standing as the Rassendylls. Besides her good qualities, she had a large fortune. My brother Robert was wise enough not to mind about her family history. Family history is, in fact, a matter about which Rose's next words had some truth.

“Good families are usually worse than any others,” she said.

At this I touched my hair. I knew quite well what she meant.

“I am so glad Robert's is black!” she cried.

At this moment Robert came in. (He gets up at seven and works before breakfast.) He looked at his wife. Her face was slightly red. He touched it gently.

“What is the matter, my dear?” he asked.

“She objects to my doing nothing and having red hair,” said I, in a hurt tone.

“Oh! of course he cannot help his hair,” said Rose.

“It usually appears once in a generation,” said my brother. “So does the nose. Rudolf has got them both.”

“I wish they did not appear,” said Rose, still red in the face.

“I rather like them myself,” said I. Then I stood up and bowed to the portrait of Countess Amelia.

My brother's wife made a sound of anger.

“I wish you would take that picture away, Robert,” she said.

“My dear!” he cried.

“Good heavens!” I added.

“Then it might be forgotten,” she went on.

“Hardly--with Rudolf about,” said Robert, shaking his head.

“Why should it be forgotten?” I asked.

“Rudolf!” cried my brother's wife, turning red in a very pretty way.

I laughed, and went on with my egg. At least I had put aside the question of what (if anything) I ought to do. And, to close the discussion--and also, I must admit, to make my strict little sister-in-law a little more angry--I said:

“I rather like being an Elphberg myself.”

When I read a story, I pass over the explanations. Yet the moment I begin to write one, I find that I must have an explanation. For it is clear that I must explain why my sister-in-law was angry about my nose and hair, and why I dared to call myself an Elphberg. The Rassendylls have been important for many generations, I must say. Yet sharing their blood does not, at first sight, make it right to claim a connection with the greater family of the Elphbergs or to claim to be one of that Royal House. For what relationship is there between Ruritania and Burlesdon, between the Palace at Strelsau or the Castle of Zenda and Number 305 Park Lane, W.?

Well then--and I must say first that I am going, by need, to bring up the very scandal which my dear Lady Burlesdon wishes forgotten. In the year 1733, George II. was then on the throne. Peace ruled for the moment, and the King and the Prince of Wales were not yet in conflict. A certain prince came to visit the English Court. He was later known to history as Rudolf the Third of Ruritania. The prince was a tall, handsome young fellow. He was marked (maybe harmed, it is not for me to say) by a somewhat unusually long, sharp and straight nose, and a mass of dark-red hair. In fact, these were the nose and the hair which have marked the Elphbergs beyond memory. He stayed some months in England, where he was received most politely. Yet, in the end, he left with some shame. For he fought a duel with a nobleman. (People thought it highly well bred of him to set aside all question of his rank.) The nobleman was well known in the society of the day, not only for his own good qualities, but as the husband of a very beautiful wife. In that duel Prince Rudolf received a severe wound. He recovered from it and was cleverly sent away by the Ruritanian ambassador, who had found him a pretty handful. The nobleman was not wounded in the duel. But the morning was cold and wet at the meeting, and he caught a severe chill. He failed to throw it off, and he died some six months after the departure of Prince Rudolf. He had not found fun time to put right his relations with his wife. After another two months, she bore an heir to the title and lands of the family of Burlesdon. This lady was the Countess Amelia, whose picture my sister-in-law wished to remove from the drawing-room in Park Lane. Her husband was James, fifth Earl of Burlesdon and twenty-second Baron Rassendyll, both in the peerage of England, and a Knight of the Garter. As for Rudolf, he went back to Ruritania, married a wife, and went up to the throne. His children in the direct line have sat there from then till this very hour--with one short break. And, finally, if you walk through the picture galleries at Burlesdon, among the fifty portraits or so of the last century and a half, you will find five or six, including that of the sixth earl, marked by long, sharp, straight noses and a quantity of dark-red hair. These five or six also have blue eyes, whereas among the Rassendylls dark eyes are more common.

That is the explanation, and I am glad to have finished it. The marks on honourable family lines are a delicate subject. And certainly this heredity we hear so much about is the finest spreader of scandal in the world. It laughs at care, and writes strange entries between the lines of the “Peerages”.

People will notice that my sister-in-law had a strange way of thinking. She seemed to think my face was almost a crime that I was responsible for. She quickly decided that my outer look showed inner qualities, which I say I did not have. She tried to support this unfair guess by pointing out that my life had been useless. Well, maybe so, but I had gained a good deal of pleasure and a good deal of knowledge. I had been to a German school and a German university. I spoke German as easily and perfectly as English. I was fully at home in French. I had a little Italian and enough Spanish to swear by. I believe I was a strong fighter with a sword, though hardly a fine one, and a good shot. I could ride anything that had a back to sit on. And my head was as cool as you could find, for all its flaming cover. If you say I ought to have spent my time in useful work, I have nothing to say. I can only say that my parents had no business to leave me two thousand pounds a year and a love of wandering.

“The difference between you and Robert,” said my sister-in-law, who often speaks on a platform (bless her!) and even more often as if she were on one, “is that he recognizes the duties of his position, and you see the opportunities of yours.”

“To a man of spirit, my dear Rose,” I answered, “opportunities are duties.”

“Nonsense!” said she, tossing her head. After a moment she went on: “Now, here is Sir Jacob Borrodaile offering you exactly what you might be equal to.”

“A thousand thanks!” I said in a low voice.

“He is to have an Embassy in six months, and Robert says he is sure that he will take you as an attaché. Do take it, Rudolf--to please me.”

Now, when my sister-in-law puts the matter in that way, making lines on her pretty face, twisting her little hands, and growing sad in the eyes, all on account of a lazy good-for-nothing like myself, for whom she has no natural responsibility, I feel sorry. Moreover, I thought it possible that I could pass the time in the position suggested with some acceptable fun. Therefore I said:

“My dear sister, if in six months’ time no unexpected problem has arisen, and Sir Jacob invites me, hang me if I do not go with Sir Jacob!”

“Oh, Rudolf, how good of you! I am glad!”

“Where is he going to?”

“He does not know yet; but it is sure to be a good Embassy.”

“Madame,” said I, “for your sake I will go, if it is no more than a poor Legation. When I do a thing, I do not do it by halves.”

My promise, then, was given. But six months are six months, and seem like forever. They stretched between me and my possible work (I suppose attachés are hard-working; but I know not, for I never became attaché to Sir Jacob or anybody else). So I looked about for some good way of spending them. And it suddenly occurred to me that I would visit Ruritania. It may seem strange that I had never visited that country yet. But my father (in spite of a secret fondness for the Elphbergs, which led him to give me, his second son, the famous Elphberg name of Rudolf) had always been against my going. Since his death, my brother, led by Rose, had accepted the family tradition which taught that a wide distance was to be given to that country. But the moment Ruritania had come into my head I was eaten up with a wish to see it. After all, red hair and long noses are not limited to the House of Elphberg. The old story seemed a very poor reason for keeping myself from knowing a highly interesting and important kingdom. It had played no small part in European history, and might do the like again under the power of a young and strong ruler, such as the new King was said to be. My decision was settled by reading in _The Times_ that Rudolf the Fifth was to be crowned at Strelsau in the course of the next three weeks, and that great splendor was to mark the occasion. At once I made up my mind to be present, and began my preparations. But it has never been my practice to give my relatives a plan of my journeys. In this case I expected opposition to my wishes. So I gave out that I was going for a long walk in the Tyrol--an old favorite place of mine. And I calmed Rose’s anger by declaring that I intended to study the political and social problems of the interesting community which lives in that area.

“Perhaps,” I hinted darkly, “there may be a result of the trip.”

“What do you mean?” she asked.

“Well,” said I carelessly, “there seems a gap that might be filled by a complete work on--”

“Oh! will you write a book?” she cried, clapping her hands. “That would be splendid, would it not, Robert?”

“It is the best of introductions to political life nowadays,” observed my brother, who has, by the way, introduced himself in this manner several times over. _Burlesdon on Ancient Theories and Modern Facts_ and _The Ultimate Outcome, by a Political Student_, are both works of recognized fame.

“I believe you are right, Bob, my boy,” said I.

“Now promise you will do it,” said Rose seriously.

“No, I will not promise; but if I find enough material, I will.”

“That is fair enough,” said Robert.

“Oh, material does not matter!” she said, pushing out her lips.

But this time she could get no more than a limited promise out of me. To tell the truth, I would have bet a large sum that the story of my trip that summer would stain no paper and spoil not a single pen. And that shows how little we know what the future holds. For here I am, fulfilling my limited promise, and writing, as I never thought to write, a book--though it will hardly serve as an introduction to political life, and has not a bit to do with the Tyrol.

Neither would it, I fear, please Lady Burlesdon, if I were to submit it to her critical eye--a step which I have no intention of taking.

CHAPTER 2. Concerning the Colour of Men’s Hair

Concerning the Colour of Men’s Hair

It was a favourite saying of my Uncle William’s that no man should pass through Paris without spending four-and-twenty hours there. My uncle spoke from long experience of the world. I honoured his advice by staying for a day and a night at “The Continental” on my way to the Tyrol. I called on George Featherly at the Embassy. We had a bit of dinner together at Durand’s, and afterwards dropped in to the Opera. After that we had a little supper. After that we called on Bertram Bertrand, a poet of some fame and Paris correspondent to _The Critic_. He had a very comfortable set of rooms. We found some pleasant fellows smoking and talking. It struck me, however, that Bertram himself was not there and was in low spirits. When everybody except ourselves had gone, I teased him about his sad and worried mood. He avoided my questions for a while. At last he threw himself on a couch and cried out:

“Very well; have it your own way. I am in love--terribly in love!”

“Oh, you will write the better poetry,” said I, to comfort him.

He pulled at his hair with his hand and smoked angrily. George Featherly stood with his back to the fireplace and smiled in an unkind way.

“If it is the old affair,” said he, “you may as well give it up, Bert. She is leaving Paris tomorrow.”

“I know that,” said Bertram sharply.

“Not that it would make any difference if she stayed,” went on the unkind George. “She aims higher than the paper trade, my boy!”

“Hang her!” said Bertram.

“It would make it more interesting for me,” I dared to say, “if I knew who you were talking about.”

“Antoinette Mauban,” said George.

“De Mauban,” said Bertram in a low angry voice.

“Oho!” said I, passing by the question of the ‘de’. “You do not mean to say, Bert--?”

“Can you not leave me alone?”

“Where is she going?” I asked, for the lady was rather a famous person.

George shook the coins in his pocket, smiled in a cruel way at poor Bertram, and answered in a pleasant voice:

“Nobody knows. By the way, Bert, I met a great man at her house the other night--at least, about a month ago. Did you ever meet him--the Duke of Strelsau?”

“Yes, I did,” said Bertram in a low angry voice.

“A very accomplished man, I thought him.”

It was not hard to see that George’s mentions of the duke were meant to make poor Bertram suffer more. So I drew the conclusion that the duke had paid special attention to Madame de Mauban. She was a widow, rich, handsome, and, by all accounts, ambitious. It was quite possible that she, as George put it, was aiming as high as a person who was everything he could be, short of being truly royal. For the duke was the son of the late King of Ruritania by a second marriage of lower rank, and half-brother to the new King. He had been his father’s favourite. It had caused some bad comment when he had been made a duke, with a title taken from no less a city than the capital itself. His mother had been of good, but not high, birth.

“He is not in Paris now, is he?” I asked.

“Oh no! He has gone back to be present at the King’s crowning ceremony. I should say he will not enjoy it much. But, Bert, old man, do not give up hope! He will not marry the fair Antoinette--at least, not unless another plan fails. Still perhaps she--” He paused and added, with a laugh: “Royal attentions are hard to resist--you know that, do you not, Rudolf?”

“Curse you!” said I. I rose and left poor Bertram in George’s hands and went home to bed.

The next day George Featherly went with me to the station, where I took a ticket for Dresden.

“Going to see the pictures?” asked George, with a grin.

George is a great gossip. If I had told him that I was off to Ruritania, the news would have been in London in three days and in Park Lane in a week. I was therefore about to give him a vague answer, when he saved me the trouble by leaving me suddenly and running across the platform. Following him with my eyes, I saw him lift his hat and speak to a graceful, well-dressed woman who had just come from the ticket office. She was perhaps a year or two over thirty, tall, dark, and rather full in figure. As George talked, I saw her look at me. My pride was hurt by the thought that, wrapped in a fur coat and a neck cloth (for it was a chilly April day) and wearing a soft travelling hat pulled down to my ears, I must look very far from my best. A moment later, George came back to me.

“You have a charming travelling companion,” he said. “That is poor Bert Bertrand’s goddess, Antoinette de Mauban. Like you, she is going to Dresden--also, no doubt, to see the pictures. It is very strange, though, that she does not at present wish for the honour of your acquaintance.”

“I did not ask to be introduced,” I said, a little annoyed.

“Well, I offered to bring you to her; but she said, ‘Another time.’ Never mind, old fellow, perhaps there will be a crash, and you will have a chance of saving her and beating the Duke of Strelsau!”

No crash, however, happened, either to me or to Madame de Mauban. I can speak for her as surely as for myself. For when, after a night’s rest in Dresden, I continued my journey, she got into the same train. I understood that she wished to be left alone, so I avoided her carefully. But I saw that she went the same way as I did to the very end of my journey. I took chances to have a good look at her when I could do so unseen.

As soon as we reached the Ruritanian border (where the old officer in charge of the Custom House gave me such a stare that I felt surer than before of my Elphberg face), I bought the papers. I found in them news which changed my plans. For some reason, which was not clearly explained, and seemed to be something of a mystery, the date of the crowning ceremony had been suddenly moved forward. The ceremony was to take place on the next day but one. The whole country seemed in a stir about it, and it was clear that Strelsau was crowded. Rooms were all let and hotels overflowing. There would be very little chance of my getting a lodging, and I would certainly have to pay a very high price for it. I made up my mind to stop at Zenda, a small town fifty miles short of the capital, and about ten from the border. My train reached there in the evening. I would spend the next day, Tuesday, walking over the hills, which were said to be very fine, and taking a look at the famous Castle. Then I would go by train to Strelsau on the Wednesday morning, returning at night to sleep at Zenda.

So I got out at Zenda. The train passed the platform where I stood. I saw my friend Madame de Mauban in her place. Clearly she was going on to Strelsau. She had secured rooms there, with more care than I could claim. I smiled to think how surprised George Featherly would have been. He did not know that she and I had been fellow travellers for so long.

I was very kindly received at the hotel. It was really no more than a small hotel. A fat old lady and her two daughters kept it. They were good, quiet people. They seemed very little interested in the great events at Strelsau. The old lady’s hero was the duke. Under the late King’s will, he was now master of the Zenda estates and of the Castle. The Castle rose grandly on its steep hill at the end of the valley. It was a mile or so from the hotel. The old lady did not hesitate to say she was sorry that the duke was not on the throne, instead of his brother.

“We know Duke Michael,” she said. “He has always lived among us. Every Ruritanian knows Duke Michael. But the King is almost a stranger. He has been abroad so much that not one in ten knows him even by sight.”

“And now,” said one of the young women, “they say he has shaved off his beard. So no one at all knows him.”

“Shaved his beard!” exclaimed her mother. “Who says so?”

“Johann, the duke’s keeper. He has seen the King.”

“Ah, yes. The King, sir, is now at the duke’s hunting lodge in the forest here. From here he goes to Strelsau to be crowned on Wednesday morning.”

I was interested to hear this. I decided to walk next day toward the lodge, hoping to meet the King. The old lady talked on and on:

“Ah, and I wish he would stay at his hunting. That and wine (and one thing more) are all he loves, they say. I wish he would let our duke be crowned on Wednesday. That I wish, and I do not care who knows it.”

“Hush, mother!” urged the daughters.

“Oh, many think as I do!” cried the old woman stubbornly.

I threw myself back in my deep armchair and laughed at her zeal.

“For my part,” said the younger and prettier of the two daughters, a fair, smiling girl, “I hate Black Michael! A red Elphberg for me, mother! The King, they say, is as red as a fox or as--”

And she laughed mischievously. She cast a glance at me and tossed her head at her sister’s reproving face.

“Many a man has cursed their red hair before now,” muttered the old lady--and I remembered James, fifth Earl of Burlesdon.

“But never a woman!” cried the girl.

“Ay, and women, when it was too late,” was the stern answer. It reduced the girl to silence and blushes.

“How comes the King here?” I asked, to break an embarrassed silence. “It is the duke’s land here, you say.”

“The duke invited him, sir, to rest here till Wednesday. The duke is at Strelsau, preparing the King’s reception.”

“Then they’re friends?”

“None better,” said the old lady.

But my rosy damsel tossed her head again. She was not to be repressed for long, and she broke out again:

“Ay, they love one another as men do who want the same place and the same wife!”

The old woman glowered. But the last words pricked my curiosity, and I interposed before she could begin scolding:

“What, the same wife, too! How’s that, young lady?”

“All the world knows that Black Michael--well then, mother, the duke--would give his soul to marry his cousin, the Princess Flavia. And she is to be the queen.”

“Upon my word,” said I, “I begin to be sorry for your duke. But if a man will be a younger son, why he must take what the elder leaves. He must be as thankful to God as he can;” and, thinking of myself, I shrugged my shoulders and laughed. And then I thought also of Antoinette de Mauban and her journey to Strelsau.

“It’s little dealing Black Michael has with--” began the girl, braving her mother’s anger. But as she spoke a heavy step sounded on the floor. A gruff voice asked in a threatening tone:

“Who talks of ‘Black Michael’ in his Highness’s own burgh?”

The girl gave a little shriek, half of fright--half, I think, of amusement.

“You’ll not tell of me, Johann?” she said.

“See where your chatter leads,” said the old lady.

The man who had spoken came forward.

“We have company, Johann,” said my hostess, and the fellow plucked off his cap. A moment later he saw me. To my amazement, he started back a step, as though he had seen something wonderful.

“What ails you, Johann?” asked the elder girl. “This is a gentleman on his travels, come to see the coronation.”

The man had recovered himself. But he was staring at me with an intense, searching, almost fierce glance.

“Good evening to you,” said I.

“Good evening, sir,” he muttered, still scrutinizing me. The merry girl began to laugh as she called--

“See, Johann, it is the colour you love! He started to see your hair, sir. It’s not the colour we see most of here in Zenda.”

“I crave your pardon, sir,” stammered the fellow, with puzzled eyes. “I expected to see no one.”

“Give him a glass to drink my health in. And I’ll bid you good night, and thanks to you, ladies, for your courtesy and pleasant conversation.”

So speaking, I rose to my feet. With a slight bow I turned to the door. The young girl ran to light me on the way. The man fell back to let me pass, his eyes still fixed on me. The moment I was by, he started a step forward, asking:

“Pray, sir, do you know our King?”

“I never saw him,” said I. “I hope to do so on Wednesday.”

He said no more. But I felt his eyes following me till the door closed behind me. My saucy conductor looked over her shoulder at me as she went upstairs ahead of me. She said:

“There’s no pleasing Master Johann for one of your colour, sir.”

“He prefers yours, maybe?” I suggested.

“I meant, sir, in a man,” she answered, with a coquettish glance.

“What,” asked I, taking hold of the other side of the candlestick, “does colour matter in a man?”

“Nay, but I love yours--it’s the Elphberg red.”

“Colour in a man,” said I, “is a matter of no more moment than that!”--and I gave her something of no value.

“God send the kitchen door be shut!” said she.

“Amen!” said I, and left her.

In fact, however, as I now know, colour is sometimes of considerable moment to a man.

CHAPTER 3. A Merry Evening with a Distant Relative

A Merry Evening with a Distant Relative

I was not so stupid as to feel anger against the duke’s keeper because he did not like my face. And even if I had felt angry, his most polite and helpful manner the next morning would have calmed me. He heard that I was going to Strelsau. So he came to see me while I was eating breakfast. He told me that his sister had married a rich shop owner and lived in the capital. She had invited him to stay in a room in her house. He had been happy to accept. But now he found that his work would not let him leave. So he asked me to take his place, if such a simple room would please me. He added that it was clean and comfortable. He promised his sister would agree. He also said that travel to and from Strelsau the next day would be hard and crowded for me. I accepted his offer at once, without waiting a second. He went off to send a message to his sister. I packed my things and got ready for the next train. But I still wanted to see the forest and the hunting house. My little servant girl told me I could walk ten miles or so through the forest and reach the railway at a small station by the road. So I decided to send my bags straight to the address Johann had given me. I would take my walk and then go to Strelsau myself. Johann had already left and did not know about this change in my plans. Its only effect was to make me a few hours later at his sister’s house. So there was no reason to take the trouble to tell him. Surely the good lady would not worry about me.

I ate an early lunch. Then I said goodbye to my kind hosts and promised to come back on my way home. I set out to climb the hill that led to the Castle, and from there to the forest of Zenda. Half an hour of easy walking brought me to the Castle. It had been a strong fort in old days. The old central tower was still in good condition and very grand. Behind it stood another part of the original castle. Behind that again, separated by a deep and wide water ditch that ran all around the old buildings, was a fine modern large house. The last king had built it, and now it was the country home of the Duke of Strelsau. A bridge that could be raised connected the old and new parts. This indirect way was the only path between the old building and the outside world. But a wide and handsome road led to the modern house. It was an ideal home. When “Black Michael” wanted company, he could live in his house. If he suddenly wanted to be alone, he only had to cross the bridge and pull it up after him. It ran on wheels. Then nothing less than a regiment of soldiers and a line of big guns could get him out. I went on my way, glad that poor Black Michael could not have the throne or the princess, but at least had as fine a home as any prince in Europe.

Soon I entered the forest and walked on for an hour or more in its cool dark shade. The great trees crossed over one another above my head. The sunlight came through in small patches as bright as diamonds, and hardly bigger. I was delighted with the place. I found a fallen tree trunk and put my back against it. I stretched my legs out and gave myself up to quiet looking at the solemn beauty of the woods. I also enjoyed a good cigar. When the cigar was finished and I had, I suppose, taken in as much beauty as I could, I fell into the most pleasant sleep. I forgot my train to Strelsau and the fast-fading afternoon. To think of a train in such a place would have been a kind of sin. Instead, I began to dream that I was married to the Princess Flavia and lived in the Castle of Zenda. I spent whole days with my love in the open spaces of the forest. It made a very pleasant dream. In fact, I was just pressing a warm kiss on the charming lips of the princess when I heard someone cry out in rough, harsh tones. At first the voice seemed part of the dream.

“Why, the devil’s in it! Shave him, and he’d be the King!”

The idea seemed funny enough for a dream. By losing my heavy moustache and carefully pointed small beard, I was to be turned into a king! I was about to kiss the princess again when I reached the conclusion, very unwillingly, that I was awake.

I opened my eyes and found two men looking at me with much curiosity. Both wore shooting clothes and carried guns. One was rather short and very strongly built. He had a big bullet-shaped head, a stiff grey moustache, and small pale-blue eyes, a little red. The other was a slender young fellow of middle height, dark in face, and he carried himself with grace and distinction. I judged the one to be an old soldier. I took the other for a gentleman used to good society, but not unused to military life either. It turned out later that my guess was a good one.

The older man came up to me and signaled the younger to follow. He did so, politely raising his hat. I rose slowly to my feet.

“He’s the height, too!” I heard the older man murmur as he looked at my six feet two inches of height. Then, with a cheerful touch of the cap, he spoke to me:

“May I ask your name?”

“As you have taken the first step in the acquaintance, gentlemen,” said I, with a smile, “suppose you give me a lead in the matter of names.”

The young man stepped forward with a pleasant smile.

“This,” said he, “is Colonel Sapt, and I am called Fritz von Tarlenheim. We are both in the service of the King of Ruritania.”

I bowed and, taking off my hat, answered:

“I am Rudolf Rassendyll. I am a traveller from England. And once for a year or two I held a commission from her Majesty the Queen.”

“Then we are all brothers of the sword,” answered Tarlenheim, holding out his hand, which I took readily.

“Rassendyll, Rassendyll!” muttered Colonel Sapt. Then a gleam of understanding passed across his face.

“By Heaven!” he cried, “you’re of the Burlesdons?”

“My brother is now Lord Burlesdon,” said I.

“Thy head betrayeth thee,” he chuckled, pointing to my uncovered head. “Why, Fritz, you know the story?”

The young man glanced at me with an apology in his eyes. He felt a care which my sister-in-law would have admired. To put him at his ease, I remarked with a smile:

“Ah! the story is known here as well as among us, it seems.”

“Known!” cried Sapt. “If you stay here, no man in all Ruritania will doubt it--or a woman either.”

I began to feel uncomfortable. If I had known what a very clear family history I carried with me, I would have thought for a long time before I visited Ruritania. But I was in it now.

At this moment a clear voice came from the wood behind us:

“Fritz, Fritz! where are you, man?”

Tarlenheim started and said quickly:

“It is the King!”

Old Sapt laughed quietly again.

Then a young man jumped out from behind a tree and stood beside us. As I looked at him, I gave a surprised cry. He saw me and stepped back in sudden wonder. The King of Ruritania could have been Rudolf Rassendyll, and I, Rudolf, the King. This was true except for the hair on my face. It was also true except for a manner of quiet dignity that his position gave him. It was also true except that he was perhaps a little shorter than me—no, less than that, but still something.

For a moment we stood still, looking at one another. Then I took off my hat again and bowed with respect. The King found his voice and asked in confusion:

“Colonel—Fritz—who is this gentleman?”

I was about to answer when Colonel Sapt stepped between the King and me. He began to talk to His Majesty in a low voice. The King stood over Sapt. As he listened, his eyes now and again looked at mine. I looked at him long and carefully. The similarity was certainly surprising, though I saw the points of difference too. The King’s face was a little fuller than mine. The shape of his face was a little more rounded. And I thought his mouth lacked some of the firmness (or stubbornness) that came from my tightly closed lips. But even so, and above all small differences, the similarity was striking, clear, and wonderful.

Sapt stopped speaking, and the King still frowned. Then, little by little, the corners of his mouth began to move. His nose came down (as mine does when I laugh). His eyes shone, and then he burst into the happiest fit of laughter I could not stop. It rang through the woods and showed him to be a cheerful soul.

“Well met, cousin!” he cried, stepping up to me, hitting me on the back, and still laughing. “You must forgive me if I was surprised. A man does not expect to see double at this time of day, eh, Fritz?”

“I must ask pardon, sire, for my boldness,” said I. “I hope it will not lose me Your Majesty’s favour.”

“By Heaven! you will always enjoy the King’s face,” he laughed, “whether I like it or not. And, sir, I shall very gladly add to it what help I can. Where are you travelling to?”

“To Strelsau, sire—to the coronation.”

The King looked at his friends. He still smiled, though his expression hinted at some unease. But the funny side of the matter caught him again.

“Fritz, Fritz!” he cried, “a thousand crowns for a sight of brother Michael’s face when he sees a pair of us!” and the happy laugh rang out again.

“Seriously,” said Fritz von Tarlenheim, “I question Mr. Rassendyll’s wisdom in visiting Strelsau just now.”

The King lit a cigarette.

“Well, Sapt?” said he, questioningly.

“He must not go,” said the old fellow in a low voice.

“Come, colonel, you mean that I should be in Mr. Rassendyll’s debt, if—”

“Oh, yes! wrap it up in the right way,” said Sapt, pulling a great pipe out of his pocket.

“Enough, sire,” said I. “I will leave Ruritania today.”

“No, by thunder, you shall not—and that is without more words, as Sapt likes it. For you shall eat with me tonight, happen what will afterwards. Come, man, you do not meet a new relation every day!”

“We eat lightly tonight,” said Fritz von Tarlenheim.

“Not us—with our new cousin for a guest!” cried the King. And as Fritz raised his shoulders, he added: “Oh! I will remember our early start, Fritz.”

“So will I—tomorrow morning,” said old Sapt, pulling at his pipe.

“O wise old Sapt!” cried the King. “Come, Mr. Rassendyll—by the way, what name did they give you?”

“Your Majesty’s,” I answered, bowing.

“Well, that shows they were not ashamed of us,” he laughed. “Come, then, cousin Rudolf. I have no house of my own here, but my dear brother Michael lends us a place of his, and we will make shift to entertain you there.” And he put his arm through mine and, signing to the others to come with us, walked me off, westerly, through the forest.

We walked for more than half an hour, and the King smoked cigarettes and talked without stopping. He was full of interest in my family. He laughed loudly when I told him of the portraits with Elphberg hair in our galleries. He laughed even more loudly when he heard that my trip to Ruritania was a secret one.

“You have to visit your bad cousin on the sly, do you?” said he.

Suddenly newly appearing from the wood, we came on a small and rough hunting-lodge. It was a one-storey building, a sort of bungalow, built entirely of wood. As we came near it, a little man in a plain uniform came out to meet us. The only other person I saw about the place was a fat elderly woman. I later discovered she was the mother of Johann, the duke’s keeper.

“Well, is dinner ready, Josef?” asked the King.

The little servant told us that it was, and we soon sat down to a large meal. The food was plain enough. The King ate with pleasure, Fritz von Tarlenheim ate delicately, old Sapt ate hungrily. I used my knife and fork well, as my custom is. The King noticed my performance with approval.

“We are all good eaters, we Elphbergs,” said he. “But what?—we are eating dry! Wine, Josef! wine, man! Are we animals, to eat without drinking? Are we cattle, Josef?”

At this reproof Josef hurried to load the table with bottles.

“Remember tomorrow!” said Fritz.

“Yes—tomorrow!” said old Sapt.

The King drained a full glass to his “Cousin Rudolf,” as he was kind—or merry—enough to call me. And I drank its fellow to the “Elphberg Red,” at which he laughed loudly.

Now, be the meat what it might, the wine we drank was beyond all price or praise, and we did it justice. Fritz ventured once to stop the King’s hand.

“What?” cried the King. “Remember you start before I do, Master Fritz—you must be more sparing by two hours than I.”

Fritz saw that I did not understand.

“The colonel and I,” he explained, “leave here at six. We ride down to Zenda and return with the guard of honour to fetch the King at eight, and then we all ride together to the station.”

“Hang that same guard!” said Sapt in a low voice.

“Oh! it is very civil of my brother to ask the honour for his regiment,” said the King. “Come, cousin, you need not start early. Another bottle, man!”

I had another bottle—or, rather, part of one. The larger half went quickly down his Majesty’s throat. Fritz stopped trying to persuade us. Instead, he let us persuade him. Soon we were all as full of wine as we had any right to be. The King began talking about what he would do in the future. Old Sapt talked about what he had done in the past. Fritz talked about some beautiful girl or other. I talked about the wonderful good points of the Elphberg family line. We all talked at once. We followed Sapt’s strong advice exactly: let the next day take care of itself.

At last the King set down his glass and leant back in his chair.

“I have drunk enough,” said he.

“I would never say the King is wrong,” said I.

Indeed, his remark was most absolutely true—so far as it went.

While I yet spoke, Josef came and set before the King a wonderful old bottle covered in woven branches. It had lain so long in some dark underground room that it seemed to shine in the candle light.

“His Highness the Duke of Strelsau told me to set this wine before the King, when the King was tired of all other wines. He asks the King to drink, for the love that he bears his brother.”

“Well done, Black Michael!” said the King. “Out with the stopper, Josef. Hang him! Did he think I’d step back from his bottle?”

The bottle was opened, and Josef filled the King’s glass. The King tasted it. Then, with a seriousness born of the hour and his own condition, he looked round on us:

“Gentlemen, my friends—Rudolf, my cousin (it is a shocking story, Rudolf, on my word!), everything is yours to the half of Ruritania. But ask me not for a single drop of this divine bottle. I will drink it to the health of that—that clever bad man, my brother, Black Michael.”

And the King seized the bottle and turned it over his mouth. He drained it and threw it from him. Then he laid his head on his arms on the table.

And we drank pleasant dreams to his Majesty—and that is all I remember of the evening. Perhaps it is enough.

CHAPTER 4. The King Keeps His Appointment

The King Keeps His Appointment

I did not know if I had slept a minute or a year. I woke with a start and a shiver. My face, hair and clothes were wet with water. Opposite me stood old Sapt. He had a smiling, unkind look on his face and an empty bucket in his hand. On the table by him sat Fritz von Tarlenheim. He was pale as a ghost and dark as a crow under the eyes.

I jumped to my feet in anger.

“Your joke goes too far, sir!” I cried.

“Tut, man, we have no time for arguing. Nothing else would wake you. It is five o’clock.”

“I will thank you, Colonel Sapt--” I began again, hot in spirit, though I was very cold in body.

“Rassendyll,” interrupted Fritz, getting down from the table and taking my arm, “look here.”

The King lay full length on the floor. His face was red as his hair, and he breathed heavily. Sapt, the disrespectful old dog, kicked him sharply. He did not move, and his breathing did not change. I saw that his face and head were wet with water, as mine were.

“We have spent half an hour on him,” said Fritz.

“He drank three times what either of you did,” said Sapt in a low, rough voice.

I went down on my knees and felt his pulse. It was very slow and weak. We three looked at one another.

“Was it drugged--that last bottle?” I asked in a whisper.

“I do not know,” said Sapt.

“We must get a doctor.”

“There is none within ten miles. And a thousand doctors would not take him to Strelsau today. I know the look of it. He will not move for six or seven hours yet.”

“But the coronation!” I cried in horror.

Fritz lifted his shoulders, as I began to see was his habit on most occasions.

“We must send word that he is ill,” he said.

“I suppose so,” said I.

Old Sapt, who seemed as fresh as a flower, had lit his pipe and was smoking hard.

“If he is not crowned today,” said he, “I will bet a crown he is never crowned.”

“But heavens, why?”

“The whole nation is there to meet him. Half the army--yes, and Black Michael at the head. Shall we send word that the King is drunk?”

“That he is ill,” said I, to correct him.

“Ill!” echoed Sapt, with an unkind laugh. “They know his illnesses too well. He has been ‘ill’ before!”

“Well, we must take the chance of what they think,” said Fritz, unable to help. “I will carry the news and make the best of it.”

Sapt raised his hand.

“Tell me,” said he. “Do you think the King was drugged?”

“I do,” said I.

“And who drugged him?”

“That damned dog, Black Michael,” said Fritz between his teeth.

“Yes,” said Sapt, “so that he might not come to be crowned. Rassendyll here does not know our pretty Michael. What do you think, Fritz, has Michael no king ready? Has half Strelsau no other candidate? As God is alive, man, the throne is lost if the King does not show himself in Strelsau today. I know Black Michael.”

“We could carry him there,” said I.

“And a very pretty picture he makes,” said Sapt with an unkind smile.

Fritz von Tarlenheim buried his face in his hands. The King breathed loudly and heavily. Sapt moved him again with his foot.

“The drunken dog!” he said. “But he is an Elphberg and the son of his father. May I rot in hell before Black Michael sits in his place!”

For a moment or two we were all silent. Then Sapt pulled his thick grey brows together, took his pipe from his mouth and said to me:

“As a man grows old he believes in Fate. Fate sent you here. Fate sends you now to Strelsau.”

I stepped back, saying “Good God!”

Fritz looked up with an eager, confused gaze.

“Impossible!” I said in a low voice. “I should be known.”

“It is a risk--against a certainty,” said Sapt. “If you shave, I will bet you will not be known. Are you afraid?”

“Sir!”

“Come, lad, there, there. But it is your life, you know, if you are known--and mine--and Fritz’s here. But, if you do not go, I swear to you Black Michael will sit tonight on the throne, and the King will lie in prison or his grave.”

“The King would never forgive it,” I said with difficulty.

“Are we women? Who cares for his forgiveness?”

The clock ticked fifty times, and sixty and seventy times, as I stood in thought. Then I suppose a look came over my face, for old Sapt caught me by the hand, crying:

“You will go?”

“Yes, I will go,” said I, and I turned my eyes on the body of the King on the floor.

“Tonight,” Sapt went on in a hasty whisper, “we are to stay in the Palace. The moment they leave us you and I will get on our horses--Fritz must stay there and guard the King’s room--and ride here at a gallop. The King will be ready--Josef will tell him--and he must ride back with me to Strelsau. And you ride as if the devil were behind you to the frontier.”

I understood it all in a second, and nodded my head.

“There is a chance,” said Fritz, with his first sign of hope.

“If I escape detection,” said I.

“If we are detected,” said Sapt. “I will send Black Michael down below before I go myself, so help me heaven! Sit in that chair, man.”

I obeyed him.

He ran from the room, calling “Josef! Josef!” In three minutes he was back, and Josef with him. The latter carried a jug of hot water, soap and razors. He was shaking as Sapt told him how things stood, and told him to shave me.

Suddenly Fritz hit his thigh:

“But the guard! They will know! They will know!”

“Pooh! We will not wait for the guard. We will ride to Hofbau and catch a train there. When they come, the bird will be gone.”

“But the King?”

“The King will be in the wine-cellar. I am going to carry him there now.”

“If they find him?”

“They will not. How should they? Josef will put them off.”

“But--”

Sapt stamped his foot.

“We are not playing,” he roared. “My God! do I not know the risk? If they do find him, he is no worse off than if he is not crowned today in Strelsau.”

So speaking, he opened the door wide and, bending down, put forth a strength I did not dream he had, and lifted the King in his hands. And as he did so, the old woman, Johann the keeper’s mother, stood in the doorway. For a moment she stood, then she turned on her heel, without a sign of surprise, and went noisily down the passage.

“Has she heard?” cried Fritz.

“I will shut her mouth!” said Sapt grimly, and he carried off the King in his arms.

For me, I sat down in an armchair. And as I sat there, half-dazed, Josef cut and shaved me till my moustache and imperial were things of the past and my face was as bare as the King’s. And when Fritz saw me like this he took a long breath and exclaimed:--

“By Jove, we shall do it!”

It was six now, and we had no time to lose. Sapt hurried me into the King’s room. I dressed myself in the uniform of a colonel of the Guard. As I slipped on the King’s boots, I found time to ask Sapt what he had done with the old woman.

“She swore she heard nothing,” he said. “But to make sure, I tied her legs together. I put a cloth in her mouth and tied her hands. I locked her in the coal room next door to the King. Josef will look after them both later on.”

Then I burst out laughing, and even old Sapt smiled a grim smile.

“I think,” he said, “that when Josef tells them the King is gone, they will think it is because we suspected something. You may swear that Black Michael does not expect to see him in Strelsau today.”

I put the King’s helmet on my head. Old Sapt handed me the King’s sword. He looked at me for a long time and very carefully.

“Thank God he shaved his beard!” he said.

“Why did he?” I asked.

“Because Princess Flavia said he rubbed her cheek when he was kind enough to give her a kiss as a cousin. Come though, we must ride.”

“Is all safe here?”

“Nothing is safe anywhere,” said Sapt, “but we can make it no safer.”

Fritz now joined us in the uniform of a captain in the same regiment as mine. In four minutes Sapt had put on his uniform. Josef called that the horses were ready. We jumped on their backs and started at a fast trot. The game had begun. What would the end of it be?

The cool morning air cleared my head. I was able to take in all Sapt said to me. He was wonderful. Fritz hardly spoke and rode like a man asleep. But Sapt, without another word for the King, began at once to teach me most carefully about my past life. He told me about my family, my tastes, my pursuits, my weaknesses, my friends, my companions, and my servants. He told me the rules of behavior at the Ruritanian Court. He promised to be always at my side to point out everybody I ought to know. He would give me hints about how much favor to show them.

“By the way,” he said, “you are a Catholic, I suppose?”

“Not I,” I answered.

“Lord, he is a heretic!” Sapt groaned. Then he began at once a simple lesson in the practices and observances of the Romish faith.

“Luckily,” he said, “you will not be expected to know much. The King is well known to be lax and careless about such matters. But you must be as civil as butter to the Cardinal. We hope to win him over, because he and Michael have a standing quarrel about their precedence.”

We were by now at the station. Fritz had recovered enough nerve to explain to the astonished station master that the King had changed his plans. The train came up. We got into a first-class carriage. Sapt leaned back on the cushions and went on with his lesson. I looked at my watch—the King’s watch it was, of course. It was just eight.

“I wonder if they have gone to look for us,” I said.

“I hope they will not find the King,” said Fritz nervously. This time it was Sapt who shrugged his shoulders.

The train travelled well. At half-past nine, looking out of the window, I saw the towers and spires of a great city.

“Your capital, my liege,” grinned old Sapt, with a wave of his hand. He leaned forward and laid his finger on my pulse. “A little too quick,” he said in his grumbling tone.

“I am not made of stone!” I said.

“You will do,” he said with a nod. “We must say Fritz here has caught the ague. Drain your flask, Fritz, for heaven’s sake, boy!”

Fritz did as he was told.

“We are an hour early,” said Sapt. “We will send word forward for your Majesty’s arrival, for there will be no one here to meet us yet. And meanwhile—”

“Meanwhile,” I said, “the King will be hanged if he does not have some breakfast.”

Old Sapt chuckled and held out his hand.

“You are an Elphberg, every inch of you,” he said. Then he paused, and looking at us, he said quietly, “God send we may be alive tonight!”

“Amen!” said Fritz von Tarlenheim.

The train stopped. Fritz and Sapt leapt out, took off their hats, and held the door for me. I choked down a lump that rose in my throat. I settled my helmet firmly on my head and (I am not ashamed to say it) breathed a short prayer to God. Then I stepped on the platform of the station at Strelsau.

A moment later, all was bustle and confusion. Men hurried up with hats in hand, and hurried off again. Men led me to the buffet. Men mounted and rode in hot haste to the quarters of the troops, to the Cathedral, to the residence of Duke Michael. Even as I swallowed the last drop of my cup of coffee, the bells throughout all the city broke out into a joyful peal. The sound of a military band and of men cheering struck my ear.

King Rudolf the Fifth was in his good city of Strelsau! And they shouted outside—

“God save the King!”

Old Sapt’s mouth wrinkled into a smile.

“God save them both!” he whispered. “Courage, lad!” And I felt his hand press my knee.

CHAPTER 5. The Adventures of an Understudy

The Adventures of an Understudy

With Fritz von Tarlenheim and Colonel Sapt close behind me, I stepped out of the buffet onto the platform. The last thing I did was to check that my revolver was within easy reach. I also checked that my sword moved freely in its holder. A happy group of officers and important officials stood waiting for me. At their head was a tall old man, covered with medals, with a military manner. He wore the yellow and red ribbon of the Red Rose of Ruritania. By the way, that same ribbon also decorated my own chest, though I did not deserve it.

"Marshal Strakencz," whispered Sapt, and I knew I was in the presence of the most famous veteran of the Ruritanian army.

Just behind the Marshal stood a short, thin man, in loose black and dark red clothes.

"The Chancellor of the Kingdom," whispered Sapt.

The Marshal greeted me with a few faithful words. Then he gave me an apology from the Duke of Strelsau. The Duke, it seemed, had been struck by a sudden illness. This made it impossible for him to come to the station. But he asked for permission to wait for his Majesty at the Cathedral. I expressed my concern. I accepted the Marshal's excuses very smoothly. Then I received the kind words of a large number of important people. No one showed the least suspicion. I felt my confidence returning. I felt the wild beating of my heart calming down. But Fritz was still pale. His hand shook like a leaf as he held it out to the Marshal.

Soon we formed a line and made our way to the door of the station. Here I got on my horse, and the Marshal held my foot-holder. The civil officials went off to their carriages. I began to ride through the streets with the Marshal on my right and Sapt on my left. Sapt, as my chief aide-de-camp, had the right to that place. The city of Strelsau is partly old and partly new. Wide modern streets and housing areas surround and hold the narrow, winding, and pretty streets of the original town. In the outer circles the upper classes live. In the inner circles the shops are located. Behind their rich fronts lie hidden crowded but poor lanes and alleys. These are filled with a poverty-stricken, wild, and mostly criminal class. These social and local divisions matched another division that was more important to me. I knew this from Sapt's information. The New Town was for the King. But to the Old Town, Michael of Strelsau was a hope, a hero, and a darling.

The scene was very bright as we passed along the Grand Boulevard and on to the great square where the Royal Palace stood. Here I was among my faithful supporters. Every house was hung with red and decorated with flags and sayings. The streets were lined with raised seats on each side. I passed along, bowing this way and that, under a shower of happy shouts, blessings, and waving handkerchiefs. The balconies were full of brightly dressed ladies. They clapped their hands and curtsied and threw their brightest looks at me. A flood of red roses fell on me. One flower caught in my horse's mane. I took it and stuck it in my coat. The Marshal smiled grimly. I had stolen some glances at his face. But he was too calm to show me whether his feelings were with me or not.

"The red rose for the Elphbergs, Marshal," said I happily, and he nodded.

I have written "happily," and it must seem a strange word. But the truth is, I was drunk with excitement. At that moment I believed, I almost believed, that I was truly the King. With a look of laughing triumph, I raised my eyes to the beauty-filled balconies again. Then I started. For looking down on me, with her handsome face and proud smile, was the lady who had been my fellow traveller: Antoinette de Mauban. I saw her start too. Her lips moved, and she leaned forward and stared at me. I collected myself and met her eyes fully and directly, while again I felt for my revolver. Suppose she had cried aloud, "That's not the King!"

Well, we went by. Then the Marshal turned round in his saddle and waved his hand. The Cuirassiers closed round us, so that the crowd could not come near me. We were leaving my quarter and entering Duke Michael's. This action of the Marshal's showed me more clearly than words what the feeling in the town must be. But if Fate made me a King, the least I could do was to play the part well.

"Why this change in our order, Marshal?" said I.

The Marshal bit his white moustache.

"It is safer, sire," he murmured.

I pulled on the reins.

"Let those in front ride on," said I, "until they are fifty yards ahead. But you, Marshal, and Colonel Sapt and my friends, wait here until I have ridden fifty yards. And see that no one is nearer to me. I will have my people see that their King trusts them."

Sapt laid his hand on my arm. I shook him off. The Marshal hesitated.

"Am I not understood?" said I. He bit his moustache again and gave the orders. I saw old Sapt smiling into his beard, but he shook his head at me. If I had been killed in open day in the streets of Strelsau, Sapt's position would have been a difficult one.

Perhaps I ought to say that I was dressed all in white, except my boots. I wore a silver helmet with gold ornaments. The wide ribbon of the Rose looked well across my chest. I would be paying a poor compliment to the King if I did not set modesty aside and admit that I made a very fine figure. So the people thought. For when I, riding alone, entered the dark, poorly decorated, gloomy streets of the Old Town, there was first a murmur, then a cheer. A woman, from a window above a cookshop, cried the old local saying:

"If he's red, he's right!" I laughed and took off my helmet so she could see that I was of the right colour. They cheered me again at that.

It was more interesting riding alone like this, for I heard the comments of the crowd.

"He looks paler than usual," said one.

"You'd look pale if you lived as he does," was the very rude reply.

"He's a bigger man than I thought," said another.

"So he had a good jaw under that beard after all," commented a third.

"The pictures of him aren't handsome enough," declared a pretty girl, taking great care that I should hear. No doubt it was mere flattery.

But even with these signs of approval and interest, most of the people received me in silence and with angry looks. My dear brother's picture was in most of the windows. This was a mocking kind of greeting to the King. I was quite glad that he had been saved the bad sight. He was a man of quick anger. Perhaps he would not have taken it as calmly as I did.

At last we were at the Cathedral. Its great grey front had hundreds of statues and two of the finest oak doors in Europe. It rose for the first time before me. The sudden sense of my boldness almost overcame me. Everything was in a mist as I got off my horse. I saw the Marshal and Sapt only faintly. I saw faintly the crowd of richly dressed priests who waited for me. And my eyes were still faint as I walked up the great nave. The loud music of the organ was in my ears. I saw nothing of the bright crowd that filled it. I hardly recognized the grand figure of the Cardinal as he rose from his high seat to greet me. Two faces only stood out side by side clearly before my eyes. One was the face of a girl, pale and lovely. She had a crown of the glorious Elphberg hair on her head (for in a woman it is glorious). The other was the face of a man. His full red cheeks, black hair, and dark deep eyes told me that at last I was in the presence of my brother, Black Michael. And when he saw me his red cheeks went pale all in a moment. His helmet fell with a loud noise on the floor. Until that moment I believe that he had not understood that the King had truly come to Strelsau.

Of what followed next I remember nothing. I knelt before the altar and the Cardinal anointed my head. Then I rose to my feet. I stretched out my hand and took from him the crown of Ruritania and set it on my head. I swore the old oath of the King. And (if it were a sin, may it be forgiven me) I received the Holy Sacrament there before them all. Then the great organ sounded out again. The Marshal told the heralds to proclaim me, and Rudolf the Fifth was crowned King. Of that grand ceremony an excellent picture hangs now in my dining-room. The portrait of the King is very good.

Then the lady with the pale face and the glorious hair stepped from her place and came to where I stood. Her long dress was held by two pages. And a herald cried:

"Her Royal Highness the Princess Flavia!"

She curtsied low. She put her hand under mine and raised my hand and kissed it. And for an instant I thought what I had best do. Then I drew her to me and kissed her twice on the cheek. She turned red. Then his Eminence the Cardinal Archbishop slipped in front of Black Michael. He kissed my hand and gave me a letter from the Pope. It was the first and last which I have received from that high place!

And then came the Duke of Strelsau. His step trembled, I swear. He looked to the right and to the left, like a man who thinks of running away. His face was patched with red and white. His hand shook so that it jumped under mine. I felt his lips dry and parched. And I glanced at Sapt, who was smiling again into his beard. I was firmly doing my duty in that station of life to which I had been wonderfully called. So I took my dear Michael by both hands and kissed him on the cheek. I think we were both glad when that was over!

But I did not see the least doubt or questioning in the face of the princess or in that of any other. Yet, if I and the King had stood side by side, she could have told us in an instant, or at least after a little thought. But neither she nor anyone else dreamed or imagined that I could be other than the King. So the likeness served. For an hour I stood there, feeling as weary and bored as though I had been a king all my life. Everybody kissed my hand, and the ambassadors paid me their respects. Among them was old Lord Topham, at whose house in Grosvenor Square I had danced a score of times. Thank heaven, the old man was as blind as a bat. He did not claim my acquaintance.

Then back we went through the streets to the Palace, and I heard them cheering Black Michael. But Fritz told me that he sat biting his nails like a man in deep thought. Even his own friends said that he should have made a braver show. I was in a carriage now, side by side with the Princess Flavia. A rough fellow cried out:

"And when's the wedding?"

As he spoke another struck him in the face, crying "Long live Duke Michael!" The princess coloured--it was a lovely tint--and looked straight in front of her.

Now I felt in a difficulty. I had forgotten to ask Sapt the state of my affections, or how far matters had gone between the princess and myself. Frankly, if I had been the King, the further they had gone the better I should have been pleased. For I am not a slow-blooded man, and I had not kissed Princess Flavia's cheek for nothing. These thoughts passed through my head. But, not being sure of my ground, I said nothing. In a moment or two the princess, recovering her calm, turned to me.

"Do you know, Rudolf," said she, "you look somehow different today?"

The fact was not surprising, but the remark was troubling.

"You look," she went on, "more sober, more sedate. You are almost careworn, and I declare you are thinner. Surely it's not possible that you've begun to take anything seriously?"

The princess seemed to hold much the same opinion of the King that Lady Burlesdon held of me.

I braced myself up to the conversation.

"Would that please you?" I asked softly.

"Oh, you know my views," said she, turning her eyes away.

"Whatever pleases you I try to do," I said. And as I saw her smile and turn red, I thought that I was playing the King's hand very well for him. So I continued, and what I said was perfectly true:

"I assure you, my dear cousin, that nothing in my life has affected me more than the reception I've been greeted with today."

She smiled brightly, but in an instant grew grave again, and whispered:

"Did you notice Michael?"

"Yes," said I, adding, "he wasn't enjoying himself."

"Do be careful!" she went on. "You don't--indeed you don't--keep enough watch on him. You know--"

"I know," said I, "that he wants what I've got."

"Yes. Hush!"

Then I went on. I cannot explain it well. I had promised far more for the King than I had the right to do. I think she made me lose my head.

"And perhaps also something which I do not have yet. But I hope to win it some day."

This was my answer. If I had been the King, I would have thought it encouraging.

"Do you not have enough duties for one day, cousin?"

Bang, bang! Blare, blare! We were at the Palace. Guns were firing and trumpets blowing. Rows of servants stood waiting. I helped the princess up the wide stone steps. Then I took formal control, as a King with a crown, of the House of my ancestors. I sat down at my own table. My cousin was on my right side. On her other side was Black Michael. On my left was his Eminence the Cardinal. Behind my chair stood Sapt. At the end of the table, I saw Fritz von Tarlenheim drink to the bottom his glass of champagne. He did it sooner than he should have.

I wondered what the King of Ruritania was doing.

CHAPTER 6. The Secret of a Cellar

We were in the King’s dressing-room--Fritz von Tarlenheim, Sapt, and I. I threw myself into a big chair. I was very tired. Sapt lit his pipe. He said no words of praise for the great success of our wild risk. But his whole manner showed he was pleased. The victory, helped perhaps by good wine, had made Fritz a new man.

“What a day for you to remember!” he cried. “God, I would like to be King for twelve hours myself! But, Rassendyll, you must not put your heart too much into the part. I am not surprised Black Michael looked blacker than ever--you and the princess had so much to say to one another.”

“How beautiful she is!” I cried.

“Never mind the woman,” said Sapt in a low, rough voice. “Are you ready to start?”

“Yes,” said I, with a sigh.

It was five o’clock, and at twelve I would be no more than Rudolf Rassendyll. I said this in a joking tone.

“You will be lucky,” said Sapt in a serious, hard way, “if you are not the dead Rudolf Rassendyll. By Heaven! I feel my head shaking on my shoulders every minute you are in the city. Do you know, friend, that Michael has had news from Zenda? He went into a room alone to read it. Then he came out looking like a man in a daze.”

“I am ready,” said I. This news made me less eager to stay.

Sapt sat down.

“I must write us an order to leave the city. Michael is Governor, you know, and we must be ready for problems. You must sign the order.”

“My dear colonel, I have not been trained as a forger!”

Sapt took a piece of paper from his pocket.

“There is the King’s signature,” he said. “And here,” he went on, after another search in his pocket, “is some tracing paper. If you cannot manage a ‘Rudolf’ in ten minutes, why--I can.”

“Your education has been wider than mine,” said I. “You write it.”

And this able hero made a very passable forgery.

“Now, Fritz,” said he, “the King goes to bed. He is upset. No one is to see him until nine o’clock tomorrow. You understand--no one?”

“I understand,” answered Fritz.

“Michael may come, and ask to see him at once. You will answer that only princes of the blood have that right.”

“That will annoy Michael,” laughed Fritz.

“You quite understand?” asked Sapt again. “If the door of this room is opened while we are away, you are not to be alive to tell us about it.”

“I need no teaching, colonel,” said Fritz, a little proudly.

“Here, wrap yourself in this big cloak,” Sapt continued to me, “and put on this flat cap. My orderly rides with me to the hunting-lodge tonight.”

“There is a problem,” I said. “The horse does not live that can carry me forty miles.”

“Oh, yes, he does--two of them: one here--one at the lodge. Now, are you ready?”

“I am ready,” said I.

Fritz held out his hand.

“In case,” said he; and we shook hands warmly.

“Damn your sentiment!” said Sapt in a low, rough voice. “Come along.”

He went, not to the door, but to a panel in the wall.

“In the old King’s time,” said he, “I knew this way well.”

I followed him, and we walked, as I would estimate, nearly two hundred yards along a narrow passage. Then we came to a strong oak door. Sapt unlocked it. We passed through, and found ourselves in a quiet street that ran along the back of the Palace gardens. A man was waiting for us with two horses. One was a magnificent bay, strong enough for any weight; the other a strong brown horse. Sapt signed to me to mount the bay. Without a word to the man, we mounted and rode away. The town was full of noise and fun, but we took quiet ways. My cloak was wrapped over half my face. The large flat cap hid every lock of my telling hair. By Sapt’s directions, I bent low on my saddle, and rode with such a round back as I hope never to show on a horse again. Down a long narrow lane we went, meeting some wanderers and some noisy drinkers. As we rode, we heard the Cathedral bells still ringing out their welcome to the King. It was half-past six, and still light. At last we came to the city wall and to a gate.

“Have your weapon ready,” whispered Sapt. “We must stop his mouth, if he talks.”

I put my hand on my revolver. Sapt called out to the doorkeeper. The stars fought for us! A little girl of fourteen came out quickly.

“Please, sir, father has gone to see the King.”

“He would have done better to stay here,” said Sapt to me, smiling.

“But he said I was not to open the gate, sir.”

“Did he, my dear?” said Sapt, getting off his horse. “Then give me the key.”

The key was in the child’s hand. Sapt gave her a crown.

“Here is an order from the King. Show it to your father. Orderly, open the gate!”

I jumped down. Between us we rolled back the great gate, led our horses out, and closed it again.

“I will be sorry for the doorkeeper if Michael finds out that he was not there. Now then, lad, for a canter. We must not go too fast while we are near the town.”

Once outside the city, however, we were in little danger, for everybody else was inside, making merry. As the evening fell we quickened our pace. My splendid horse bounded along under me as though I had been a feather. It was a fine night, and soon the moon appeared. We talked little on the way, and mostly about how far we had gone.

“I wonder what the duke’s despatches told him,” said I, once.

“Yes, I wonder!” answered Sapt.

We stopped for a drink of wine and to feed our horses, losing half an hour this way. I did not dare go into the inn, and stayed with the horses in the stable. Then we went on again, and had covered some twenty-five miles, when Sapt suddenly stopped.

“Listen!” he cried.

I listened. Far behind us, in the quiet of the evening--it was just half-past nine--we heard the beat of horses’ feet. The wind blowing strong behind us carried the sound. I looked at Sapt.

“Come on!” he cried, and urged his horse into a gallop. When we next stopped to listen, the sound of feet was not audible, and we slowed our pace. Then we heard them again. Sapt jumped down and put his ear to the ground.

“There are two,” he said. “They are only a mile behind. Thank God the road curves in and out, and the wind is our way.”

We galloped on. We seemed to be keeping our lead. We had entered the outskirts of the forest of Zenda. The trees closed in behind us as the track turned this way and that. They kept us from seeing our pursuers, and kept them from seeing us.

Another half-hour brought us to a place where the road divided. Sapt pulled on the reins.

“To the right is our road,” he said. “To the left, to the Castle. Each about eight miles. Get down.”

“But they will be on us!” I cried.

"Get down!" he said again, in a short, sharp voice. And I obeyed. The wood was thick right up to the edge of the road. We led our horses into the hiding place. We tied cloths over their eyes and stood beside them.

"You want to see who they are?" I whispered.

"Yes, and where they are going," he answered.

I saw that his gun was in his hand.

Nearer and nearer came the sound of feet. The moon now shone out clear and full. The road was white with its light. The ground was hard, and we had left no marks.

"Here they come!" whispered Sapt.

"It is the duke!"

"I thought so," he answered.

It was the duke. With him was a big, strong man whom I knew well. He would have good reason to know me later: Max Holf, brother to Johann the keeper, and personal servant to his Highness. They came up to us. The duke pulled up his horse. I saw Sapt's finger move slowly and gently towards the trigger. I believe he would have given ten years of his life for one shot. He could have shot Black Michael as easily as I could shoot a chicken in a farmyard. I put my hand on his arm. He nodded to show he was calm. He was always ready to give up what he wanted for duty.

"Which way?" asked Black Michael.

"To the Castle, your Highness," said his companion. "There we shall learn the truth."

For a moment the duke waited.

"I thought I heard feet," he said.

"I think not, your Highness."

"Why shouldn't we go to the lodge?"

"I fear a trap. If all is well, why go to the lodge? If not, it is a trap to catch us."

Suddenly the duke's horse gave a loud cry. In a second we wrapped our coats close round our horses' heads. Holding them like this, we pointed our guns at the duke and his servant. If they had found us, they would have been dead men, or our prisoners.

Michael waited a moment longer. Then he cried:

"To Zenda, then!" He put spurs to his horse and rode on fast.

Sapt raised his weapon after him. There was such a sad, longing look on his face that I had much trouble not to burst out laughing.

For ten minutes we stayed where we were.

"You see," said Sapt, "they have sent him news that all is well."

"What does that mean?" I asked.

"God knows," said Sapt, with a heavy frown. "But it has brought him from Strelsau in a rare puzzle."

Then we mounted and rode as fast as our tired horses could go. For those last eight miles we spoke no more. Our minds were full of fear. "All is well." What did it mean? Was all well with the King?

At last the lodge came in sight. We urged our horses to a last fast run and rode up to the gate. All was still and quiet. Not a soul came to meet us. We got off our horses in haste. Suddenly Sapt caught me by the arm.

"Look there!" he said, pointing to the ground.

I looked down. At my feet lay five or six silk cloths, torn and cut and ripped. I turned to him with a question.

"They are what I tied the old woman up with," he said. "Tie up the horses, and come along."

The handle of the door turned without resistance. We went into the room which had been the scene of last night's fight. It was still covered with what was left of our meal and with empty bottles.

"Come on," cried Sapt. His wonderful calm had at last almost broken.

We rushed down the passage towards the cellars. The door of the coal cellar stood wide open.

"They found the old woman," said I.

"You might have known that from the cloths," he said.

Then we came to the door of the wine cellar. It was shut. It looked in every way as it had looked when we left it that morning.

"Come, it is all right," said I.

A loud curse from Sapt rang out. His face turned pale, and he pointed again at the floor. From under the door a red mark had spread over the floor of the passage and dried there. Sapt sank against the opposite wall. I tried the door. It was locked.

"Where is Josef?" muttered Sapt.

"Where is the King?" I responded.

Sapt took out a small bottle and put it to his lips. I ran back to the dining room and seized a heavy iron bar from the fireplace. In my terror and excitement I hit the lock of the door again and again, and I fired a shot into it. It gave way, and the door swung open.

"Give me a light," said I. But Sapt still leaned against the wall.

He was, of course, more moved than I was, for he loved his master. He was not afraid for himself. No man ever saw him afraid. But to think what might lie in that dark cellar was enough to turn any man's face pale. I went myself and took a silver holder from the dining table and lit it. As I returned, I felt the hot wax drip on my bare hand as the candle moved to and fro. So I cannot afford to look down on Colonel Sapt for his agitation.

I came to the door of the cellar. The red mark, turning more and more to a dull brown, stretched inside. I walked two yards into the cellar and held the candle high above my head. I saw the full boxes of wine. I saw spiders crawling on the walls. I saw, too, a couple of empty bottles lying on the floor. And then, away in the corner, I saw the body of a man, lying flat on his back, with his arms stretched wide, and a red cut across his throat. I walked to him and knelt down beside him, and gave to God the soul of a faithful man. For it was the body of Josef, the little servant, killed while guarding the King.

I felt a hand on my shoulders. I turned and saw Sapt beside me, his eyes staring and full of terror.

"The King? My God! the King?" he whispered in a rough voice.

I threw the candle's light over every inch of the cellar.

"The King is not here," said I.

CHAPTER 7. His Majesty Sleeps in Strelsau

His Majesty Sleeps in Strelsau

I put my arm around Sapt's waist and helped him out of the cellar. I pulled the damaged door closed behind me. For ten minutes or more we sat silent in the dining-room. Then old Sapt rubbed his knuckles into his eyes. He gave one great gasp and was himself again. The clock on the mantelpiece struck one. He stamped his foot on the floor and said:

"They have got the King!"

"Yes," said I, "'all is well!' as Black Michael's message said. What a moment it must have been for him when the royal salutes fired at Strelsau this morning! I wonder when he got the message?"

"It must have been sent in the morning," said Sapt. "They must have sent it before news of your arrival at Strelsau reached Zenda--I suppose it came from Zenda."

"And he has carried it about all day!" I exclaimed. "Upon my honour, I am not the only man who has had a trying day! What did he think, Sapt?"

"What does that matter? What does he think, lad, now?"

I rose to my feet.

"We must get back," I said, "and wake every soldier in Strelsau. We ought to be chasing Michael before midday."

Old Sapt pulled out his pipe and carefully lit it from the candle which flickered on the table.

"The King may be murdered while we sit here!" I urged.

Sapt smoked on for a moment in silence.

"That cursed old woman!" he broke out. "She must have attracted their attention somehow. I see the game. They came up to take the King away by force, and--as I say--somehow they found him. If you had not gone to Strelsau, you and I and Fritz had been in heaven by now!"

"And the King?"

"Who knows where the King is now?" he asked.

"Come, let us be off!" said I; but he sat still. And suddenly he burst into one of his harsh laughs:

"By Jove, we have shaken up Black Michael!"

"Come, come!" I repeated impatiently.

"And we will shake him up a bit more," he added. A cunning smile broadened on his wrinkled, weather-beaten face. His teeth worked on an end of his grey moustache. "Ay, lad, we will go back to Strelsau. The King shall be in his capital again tomorrow."

"The King?"

"The crowned King!"

"You are mad!" I cried.

"If we go back and tell the trick we played, what would you give for our lives?"

"Just what they are worth," said I.

"And for the King's throne? Do you think that the nobles and the people will enjoy being fooled as you have fooled them? Do you think they will love a King who was too drunk to be crowned, and sent a servant to take his place?"

"He was drugged--and I am no servant."

"Mine will be Black Michael's version."

He rose, came to me, and laid his hand on my shoulder.

"Lad," he said, "if you play the man, you may save the King yet. Go back and keep his throne warm for him."

"But the duke knows--the villains he has employed know--"

"Ay, but they cannot speak!" roared Sapt in grim triumph.

"We have got them! How can they denounce you without denouncing themselves? This is not the King, because we took the King away by force and murdered his servant. Can they say that?"

The position flashed on me. Whether Michael knew me or not, he could not speak. Unless he produced the King, what could he do? And if he produced the King, where was he? For a moment I was carried away headlong; but in an instant the difficulties came strong upon me.

"I must be found out," I urged.

"Perhaps; but every hour is something. Above all, we must have a King in Strelsau, or the city will be Michael's in four-and-twenty hours. And what would the King's life be worth then--or his throne? Lad, you must do it!"

"Suppose they kill the King?"

"They will kill him, if you do not."

"Sapt, suppose they have killed the King?"

"Then, by heaven, you are as good an Elphberg as Black Michael, and you shall rule in Ruritania! But I do not believe they have; nor will they kill him if you are on the throne. Will they kill him, to put you in?"

It was a wild plan--wilder even and more hopeless than the trick we had already carried through; but as I listened to Sapt I saw the strong points in our game. And then I was a young man and I loved action. I was offered such a hand in such a game as perhaps never man played yet.

"I shall be found out," I said.

"Perhaps," said Sapt. "Come! to Strelsau! We shall be caught like rats in a trap if we stay here."

"Sapt," I cried, "I will try it!"

"Well played!" said he. "I hope they have left us the horses. I will go and see."

"We must bury that poor fellow," said I.

"No time," said Sapt.

"I will do it."

"Hang you!" he grinned. "I make you a King, and--Well, do it. Go and fetch him, while I look to the horses. He cannot lie very deep, but I doubt if he will care about that. Poor little Josef! He was an honest bit of a man."

He went out, and I went to the cellar. I raised poor Josef in my arms and carried him into the passage and thence towards the door of the house. Just inside I laid him down, remembering that I must find spades for our task. At this instant Sapt came up.

"The horses are all right; there is the own brother to the one that brought you here. But you may save yourself that job."

"I will not go before he is buried."

"Yes, you will."

"Not I, Colonel Sapt; not for all Ruritania."

"You fool!" said he. "Come here."

He drew me to the door. The moon was sinking, but about three hundred yards away, coming along the road from Zenda, I made out a party of men. There were seven or eight of them; four were on horseback and the rest were walking. I saw that they carried long tools on their shoulders, which I guessed to be spades and mattocks.

"They will save you the trouble," said Sapt. "Come along."

He was right. The approaching party must, beyond doubt, be Duke Michael's men, come to remove the traces of their evil work. I hesitated no longer, but an irresistible desire seized me.

Pointing to the corpse of poor little Josef, I said to Sapt:

"Colonel, we ought to strike a blow for him!"

"You would like to give him some company, eh! But it is too risky work, your Majesty."

"I must have a hit at them," said I.

Sapt wavered.

"Well," said he, "it is not business, you know; but you have been a good boy--and if we come to grief, why, hang me, it will save us lot of thinking! I will show you how to touch them."

He cautiously closed the open crack of the door.

Then we retreated through the house and made our way to the back entrance. Here our horses were standing. A carriage-drive swept all round the lodge.

"Revolver ready?" asked Sapt.

"No; steel for me," said I.

"Gad, you are thirsty tonight," laughed Sapt. "So be it."

We got on our horses, took out our swords, and waited without a sound for a minute or two. Then we heard the steps of men on the drive on the other side of the house. They stopped, and one cried:

"Now then, bring him out!"

"Now!" whispered Sapt.

We pushed our spurs into our horses and rushed at a gallop round the house. In a moment we were among the rough men. Sapt told me later that he killed a man, and I believe him. But I saw no more of him. With one cut, I split the head of a fellow on a brown horse, and he fell to the ground. Then I found myself facing a big man. I was half aware of another man to my right. It was too hot to stay. In one same action I pushed my spurs into my horse again and drove my sword deep into the big man's chest. His bullet flew past my ear—I could almost swear it touched it. I pulled at the sword, but it would not come out. I dropped it and galloped after Sapt. I now saw him about twenty yards ahead. I waved my hand to say goodbye, and dropped it a second later with a yell. A bullet had cut my finger and I felt the blood. Old Sapt turned round in his saddle. Someone fired again, but they had no rifles, and we were out of range. Sapt began to laugh.

"That is one to me and two to you, with good luck," he said. "Little Josef will have company."

"Yes, they will be a _parti carrée_," said I. My blood was up, and I was glad to have killed them.

"Well, a pleasant night's work to the rest!" he said. "I wonder if they noticed you?"

"The big fellow did. As I stuck him I heard him cry, 'The King!'"

"Good! good! Oh, we will give Black Michael some work before we are done!"

We stopped for a moment and made a bandage for my wounded finger. It was bleeding freely and ached badly, and the bone was much bruised. Then we rode on, asking our good horses for all that was in them. The excitement of the fight and of our great plan died away, and we rode in gloomy silence. Day broke clear and cold. We found a farmer just up, and made him give us food for ourselves and our horses. I pretended to have a toothache and covered my face closely. Then we went on again, until Strelsau lay before us. It was eight o'clock or nearing nine, and the gates were all open. They always were open, except when the duke's whim or plots shut them. We rode in by the same way as we had come out the evening before, all four of us—the men and the horses—tired and worn out. The streets were even quieter than when we had gone. Everyone was sleeping off last night's party, and we met hardly a soul until we reached the little gate of the Palace. There Sapt's old groom was waiting for us.

"Is all well, sir?" he asked.

"All is well," said Sapt. The man came to me and took my hand to kiss it.

"The King is hurt!" he cried.

"It is nothing," said I, as I got off my horse. "I caught my finger in the door."

"Remember—silence!" said Sapt. "Ah! but, my good Freyler, I do not need to tell you that!"

The old fellow shrugged his shoulders.

"All young men like to ride abroad now and again. Why not the King?" he said. Sapt's laugh left his opinion of my reasons undisturbed.

"You should always trust a man," said Sapt, putting the key in the lock, "just as far as you must."

We went in and reached the dressing-room. We threw open the door and saw Fritz von Tarlenheim stretched on the sofa, fully dressed. He seemed to have been sleeping, but our entry woke him. He jumped to his feet, gave one glance at me, and with a joyful cry threw himself on his knees before me.

"Thank God, sire! Thank God, you are safe!" he cried, stretching his hand up to catch hold of mine.

I confess that I was moved. This King, whatever his faults, made people love him. For a moment I could not bear to speak or break the poor fellow's illusion. But tough old Sapt had no such feeling. He slapped his hand on his thigh with delight.

"Bravo, lad!" he cried. "We shall do!"

Fritz looked up in confusion. I held out my hand.

"You are wounded, sire!" he exclaimed.

"It is only a scratch," said I, "but—" I paused.

He rose to his feet with a confused air. Holding my hand, he looked me up and down, and down and up. Then suddenly he dropped my hand and stepped back.

"Where is the King? Where is the King?" he cried.

"Hush, you fool!" hissed Sapt. "Not so loud! Here is the King!"

A knock sounded on the door. Sapt seized me by the hand.

"Here, quick, to the bedroom! Off with your cap and boots. Get into bed. Cover everything up."

I did as I was told. A moment later Sapt looked in, nodded, grinned, and brought in a very smart and respectful young gentleman. He came up to my bedside, bowing again and again, and told me that he was of the household of the Princess Flavia. He said that her Royal Highness had sent him especially to ask how the King's health was after the tiredness which his Majesty had gone through yesterday.

"My best thanks, sir, to my cousin," said I. "And tell her Royal Highness that I was never better in my life."

"The King," added old Sapt (who, I began to find, loved a good lie for its own sake), "has slept without a break all night."

The young gentleman (he reminded me of "Osric" in Hamlet) bowed himself out again. The farce was over, and Fritz von Tarlenheim's pale face brought us back to reality—though, in truth, the farce had to be reality for us now.

"Is the King dead?" he whispered.

"Please God, no," said I. "But he is in the hands of Black Michael!"

CHAPTER 8. A Fair Cousin and a Dark Brother

A Fair Cousin and a Dark Brother

A real king's life is perhaps a hard one. But a pretended king's is, I promise you, much harder. On the next day, Sapt taught me my duties--what I ought to do and what I ought to know--for three hours. Then I ate breakfast quickly, with Sapt still opposite me. He told me that the King always drank white wine in the morning. He also told me that the King was known to hate all strongly tasting dishes. Then came the Chancellor, for another three hours. To him I had to explain that the hurt to my finger (we used that bullet to our advantage) stopped me from writing. This caused a great deal of trouble, with much searching for past examples and so on. It ended with my "making my mark," and the Chancellor confirming it with far too many serious promises. Then the French ambassador was introduced, to present his papers. Here my lack of knowledge did not matter. The King would have been just as new to the business. We worked through the whole group of ambassadors in the next few days. A death of the Crown made all this trouble necessary.

Then, at last, I was left alone. I called my new servant. We had chosen a young man who had never known the King, to take the place of poor Josef. I had a brandy-and-soda brought to me. I said to Sapt that I trusted I might now have a rest. Fritz von Tarlenheim was standing by.

"By heaven!" he cried, "we waste time. Aren't we going to throw Black Michael in prison?"

"Gently, my son, gently," said Sapt, moving his eyebrows together. "It would be a pleasure, but it might cost us dearly. Would Michael fall and leave the King alive?"

"And," I suggested, "while the King is here in Strelsau, on his throne, what complaint has he against his dear brother Michael?"

"Are we to do nothing, then?"

"We're to do nothing stupid," said Sapt in a low, rough voice.

"In fact, Fritz," said I, "I am reminded of a situation in one of our English plays--The Critic--have you heard of it? Or, if you like, of two men, each pointing a gun at the other. For I can't expose Michael without exposing myself--"

"And the King," put in Sapt.

"And, hang me if Michael won't expose himself, if he tries to expose me!"

"It's very pretty," said old Sapt.

"If I'm found out," I went on, "I will tell the whole truth, and fight it out with the duke. But at present I'm waiting for a move from him."

"He'll kill the King," said Fritz.

"Not he," said Sapt.

"Half of the Six are in Strelsau," said Fritz.

"Only half? You're sure?" asked Sapt with strong interest.

"Yes--only half."

"Then the King's alive, for the other three are guarding him!" cried Sapt.

"Yes--you're right!" said Fritz, his face brightening. "If the King were dead and buried, they'd all be here with Michael. You know Michael's back, colonel?"

"I know, curse him!"

"Gentlemen, gentlemen," said I, "who are the Six?"

"I think you'll meet them soon," said Sapt. "They are six gentlemen whom Michael keeps in his household. They belong to him completely. There are three Ruritanians. Then there's a Frenchman, a Belgian, and one of your countrymen."

"They'd all cut a throat if Michael told them," said Fritz.

"Perhaps they'll cut mine," I suggested.

"Nothing more likely," agreed Sapt. "Who are here, Fritz?"

"De Gautet, Bersonin, and Detchard."

"The foreigners! It's as plain as a pikestaff. He's brought them, and left the Ruritanians with the King. That's because he wants to involve the Ruritanians as deeply as he can."

"They were none of them among our friends at the lodge, then?" I asked.

"I wish they had been," said Sapt with a sigh. "They had been, not six, but four, by now."

I had already developed one quality of royalty--a feeling that I need not reveal all my mind or my secret plans even to my close friends. I had fully decided on my course of action. I meant to make myself as popular as I could, and at the same time to show no dislike to Michael. By these means I hoped to reduce the hostility of his supporters. I also hoped to make it appear, if an open conflict came about, that he was ungrateful and not oppressed.

Yet an open conflict was not what I hoped for.

The King's interest demanded secrecy. And while secrecy lasted, I had a fine game to play in Strelsau. Michael should not grow stronger for delay!

I ordered my horse. Attended by Fritz von Tarlenheim, I rode in the grand new avenue of the Royal Park. I returned all the salutes which I received with careful politeness. Then I rode through a few of the streets. I stopped and bought flowers from a pretty girl, paying her with a piece of gold. Then, having attracted the desired amount of attention (for I had a trail of half a thousand people after me), I rode to the residence of the Princess Flavia. I asked if she would receive me. This step created much interest, and was met with shouts of approval. The princess was very popular. The Chancellor himself had not hesitated to hint to me that the more I pressed my suit, and the more quickly I brought it to a happy end, the stronger I should be in the love of my subjects. The Chancellor, of course, did not understand the difficulties which lay in the way of following his loyal and excellent advice. However, I thought I could do no harm by calling. Fritz supported me in this view with a warmth that surprised me. Then he confessed that he also had his reasons for liking a visit to the princess's house. That reason was no other than a great desire to see the princess's lady-in-waiting and close friend, the Countess Helga von Strofzin.

Etiquette seconded Fritz's hopes. While I was shown into the princess's room, he remained with the countess in the outer room. In spite of the people and servants who were hanging about, I doubt not that they managed a private talk. But I had no free time to think of them, for I was playing the most delicate move in all my difficult game. I had to keep the princess devoted to me--and yet not caring for me. I had to show love for her--and not feel it. I had to make love for another, and that to a girl who--princess or no princess--was the most beautiful I had ever seen. Well, I prepared myself for the task. It was made no easier by the charming shyness with which I was received. How I succeeded in carrying out my plan will appear later.

"You are gaining golden laurels," she said. "You are like the prince in Shakespeare who was transformed by becoming king. But I'm forgetting you are King, sire."

"I ask you to speak nothing but what your heart tells you--and to call me nothing but my name."

She looked at me for a moment.

"Then I'm glad and proud, Rudolf," said she. "Why, as I told you, your very face is changed."

I said thank you for the kind words, but I did not like the subject. So I said:

“My brother is back, I hear. He took a short trip, did he not?”

“Yes, he is here,” she said, with a small frown.

“He cannot stay away from Strelsau for long, it seems,” I said with a smile. “Well, we are all happy to see him. The closer he is, the better.”

The princess looked at me with a little light of fun in her eyes.

“Why, cousin? Is it that you can--?”

“See better what he is doing? Perhaps,” I said. “And why are you happy?”

“I did not say I was happy,” she answered.

“Some people say so for you.”

“There are many rude people,” she said, with a proud air that was very pleasing.

“Perhaps you mean that I am one?”

“Your Majesty could not be,” she said. She made a small bow that was not real respect. Then after a pause she added, with a playful smile: “Unless, that is--”

“Well, unless what?”

“Unless you tell me that I care not at all where the Duke of Strelsau is.”

Really, I wished that I had been the King.

“You do not care where cousin Michael--”

“Ah, cousin Michael! I call him the Duke of Strelsau.”

“You call him Michael when you meet him?”

“Yes--by the orders of your father.”

“I see. And now by mine?”

“If those are your orders.”

“Oh, surely! We must all be pleasant to our dear Michael.”

“You order me to welcome his friends, too, I suppose?”

“The Six?”

“You call them that, too?”

“To be in the fashion, I do. But I order you to welcome no one unless you like.”

“Except yourself?”

“I beg for myself. I could not order.”

As I spoke, there came a cheer from the street. The princess ran to the window.

“It is he!” she cried. “It is--the Duke of Strelsau!”

I smiled, but said nothing. She returned to her seat. For a few moments we sat in silence. The noise outside died down. But I heard the sound of feet in the ante-room. I began to talk on general subjects. This went on for some minutes. I wondered what had happened to Michael. But it did not seem to be for me to interfere. All at once, to my great surprise, Flavia held her hands together. She asked in a troubled voice:

“Are you wise to make him angry?”

“What? Who? How am I making him angry?”

“Why, by keeping him waiting.”

“My dear cousin, I do not want to keep him--”

“Well, then, is he to come in?”

“Of course, if you wish it.”

She looked at me with interest.

“How funny you are,” she said. “Of course no one could be announced while I was with you.”

Here was a lovely quality of royalty!

“An excellent rule!” I cried. “But I had quite forgotten it. And if I were alone with someone else, could you not be announced?”

“You know as well as I do. I could be, because I am of the Blood.” And she still looked puzzled.

“I never could remember all these silly rules,” I said, rather weakly. I silently cursed Fritz for not telling me. “But I will repair my fault.”

I jumped up, threw open the door, and went into the ante-room. Michael was sitting at a table, with a heavy frown on his face. Everyone else was standing. The only exception was that rude young dog Fritz. He was sitting lazily in an armchair, and flirting with the Countess Helga. He jumped up as I entered, with a quick respect that made his earlier ease seem false. I had no difficulty in understanding that the duke might not like young Fritz.

I held out my hand. Michael took it, and I embraced him. Then I drew him with me into the inner room.

“Brother,” I said, “if I had known you were here, you should not have waited a moment before I asked the princess to let me bring you to her.”

He thanked me, but coldly. The man had many qualities, but he could not hide his feelings. A mere stranger could have seen that he hated me. He hated worse to see me with Princess Flavia. Yet I am sure that he tried to hide both feelings. Further, he tried to make me believe that he believed I was truly the King. I did not know, of course. But the King was not a false man. He was at once cleverer and bolder than I (and I began to think something of myself in that role). So Michael could not believe that. And, if he did not, how he must have hated paying me respect! How he must have hated hearing my “Michael” and my “Flavia!”

“Your hand is hurt, sire,” he observed, with concern.

“Yes, I was playing a game with a mongrel dog” (I meant to stir him), “and you know, brother, such have uncertain tempers.”

He smiled sourly, and his dark eyes rested on me for a moment.

“But is there no danger from the bite?” cried Flavia anxiously.

“None from this,” I said. “If I gave him a chance to bite deeper, it would be different, cousin.”

“But surely he has been destroyed?” said she.

“Not yet. We are waiting to see if his bite is harmful.”

“And if it is?” asked Michael, with his sour smile.

“He will be knocked on the head, brother,” I said.

“You will not play with him any more?” urged Flavia.

“Perhaps I shall.”

“He might bite again.”

“Doubtless he will try,” I said, smiling.

Then I feared Michael would say something which I must appear to resent. For, though I might show him my hate, I must seem to be full of favour. So I began to praise him for the magnificent condition of his regiment. I praised their loyal greeting to me on the day of my coronation. Thence I passed to a rapturous description of the hunting-lodge which he had lent me. But he rose suddenly to his feet. His temper was failing him, and, with an excuse, he said farewell. However, as he reached the door he stopped, saying:

“Three friends of mine are very anxious to have the honour of being presented to you, sire. They are here in the ante-chamber.”

I joined him directly, passing my arm through his. The look on his face was honey to me. We entered the ante-chamber in brotherly fashion. Michael beckoned, and three men came forward.

“These gentlemen,” said Michael, with a stately courtesy which, to do him justice, he could assume with perfect grace and ease, “are the loyalest and most devoted of your Majesty’s servants, and are my very faithful and attached friends.”

“On the last ground as much as the first,” said I, “I am very pleased to see them.”

They came one by one and kissed my hand. De Gautet came first. He was a tall, thin man. His hair stood straight up, and his moustache was stiff with wax. Bersonin, the Belgian, came next. He was a heavy man of middle height with a bald head, though he was not much past thirty. Last came the Englishman, Detchard. He had a narrow face, short fair hair, and skin darkened by the sun. He was well built, broad in the shoulder and narrow in the hips. I judged him a good fighter, but not an honest man. I spoke to him in English, with a slight foreign accent. I swear the fellow smiled, though he hid the smile at once.

“So Mr. Detchard is in the secret,” I thought.

After I got rid of my dear brother and his friends, I returned to say goodbye to my cousin. She was standing at the door. I said goodbye and took her hand in mine.

“Rudolf,” she said, very quietly, “be careful, please.”

“Of what?”

“You know—I cannot say. But think what your life means to—”

“Well, to—?”

“To Ruritania.”

Was I right to play the part, or wrong to play the part? I do not know. Evil lay both ways, and I did not dare tell her the truth.

“Only to Ruritania?” I asked softly.

A sudden red spread over her perfect face.

“To your friends, too,” she said.

“Friends?”

“And to your cousin,” she whispered, “and loving servant.”

I could not speak. I kissed her hand and went out, cursing myself.

Outside I found Master Fritz. He did not care at all about the footmen. He was playing cat’s-cradle with the Countess Helga.

“Hang it!” he said. “We cannot always be plotting. Love claims his share.”

“I think you are right,” said I. Fritz had been by my side. Now he dropped behind me with respect.

CHAPTER 9. A New Use for a Tea-table

A New Use for a Tea-table

If I were to tell the ordinary events of my daily life at this time, they might teach people who do not know the inside of great houses. If I told some of the secrets I learnt, they might interest the leaders of Europe. I plan to do neither of these things. I would be between Scylla and Charybdis, and I feel I should keep myself only to the hidden drama played under the surface of Ruritanian politics. I need only say that the secret of my false role was never found out. I made mistakes. I had bad moments. I needed all the skill and kindness I had to smooth over some clear losses of memory and failures to remember old friends. But I escaped. And I owe my escape, as I have said before, most of all to the boldness of the plan. I believe that, with the necessary physical similarity, it was much easier to pretend to be King of Ruritania than it would have been to pretend to be my next-door neighbor. One day Sapt came into my room. He threw me a letter, saying:

“That is for you--a woman's hand, I think. But I have some news for you first.”

“What is that?”

“The King is at the Castle of Zenda,” said he.

“How do you know?”

“Because the other half of Michael's Six are there. I had questions asked, and they are all there--Lauengram, Krafstein, and young Rupert Hentzau: three bad men, too, on my word, as fine as any who live in Ruritania.”

“Well?”

“Well, Fritz wants you to march to the Castle with horse, foot, and big guns.”

“And drag the water around the castle?” I asked.

“That would be about it,” grinned Sapt, “and we would not find the King's body then.”

“You think it is certain he is there?”

“Very likely. Besides the fact that those three are there, the bridge is kept up, and no one goes in without an order from young Hentzau or Black Michael himself. We must stop Fritz.”

“I will go to Zenda,” said I.

“You are mad.”

“Some day.”

“Oh, perhaps. You will very likely stay there though, if you do.”

“That may be, my friend,” said I without care.

“His Majesty looks angry,” observed Sapt. “How is the love affair?”

“Damn you, hold your tongue!” I said.

He looked at me for a moment, then he lit his pipe. It was quite true that I was in a bad mood, and I went on in a contrary way:

“Wherever I go, I am followed by half a dozen men.”

“I know you are; I send them,” he replied calmly.

“What for?”

“Well,” said Sapt, smoking away, “it would not be exactly inconvenient for Black Michael if you disappeared. With you gone, the old game that we stopped would be played--or he would have a try at it.”

“I can take care of myself.”

“De Gautet, Bersonin, and Detchard are in Strelsau; and any one of them, boy, would cut your throat as readily--as readily as I would Black Michael's, and much more falsely. What is the letter?”

I opened it and read it aloud:

“If the King desires to know what it deeply concerns the King to know, let him do as this letter asks him. At the end of the New Avenue there stands a house in large grounds. The house has a porch, with a statue of a nymph on it. A wall encloses the garden; there is a gate in the wall at the back. At twelve o'clock tonight, if the King enters alone by that gate, turns to the right, and walks twenty yards, he will find a summerhouse, reached by a flight of six steps. If he goes up and enters, he will find someone who will tell him what touches most dearly his life and his throne. This is written by a faithful friend. He must be alone. If he ignores the invitation his life will be in danger. Let him show this to no one, or he will ruin a woman who loves him: Black Michael does not forgive.”

“No,” observed Sapt, as I ended, “but he can dictate a very pretty letter.”

I had arrived at the same conclusion, and was about to throw the letter away, when I saw there was more writing on the other side.

“Hallo! there is some more.”

“If you hesitate,” the writer continued, “consult Colonel Sapt--”

“Eh,” exclaimed that gentleman, truly surprised. “Does she take me for a greater fool than you?”

I waved to him to be silent.

“Ask him what woman would do most to prevent the duke from marrying his cousin, and therefore most to prevent him becoming king? And ask if her name begins with--A?”

I sprang to my feet. Sapt laid down his pipe.

“Antoinette de Mauban, by heaven!” I cried.

“How do you know?” asked Sapt.

I told him what I knew of the lady, and how I knew it. He nodded.

“It is true so far that she has had a great quarrel with Michael,” said he, thoughtfully.

“If she would, she could be useful,” I said.

“I believe, though, that Michael wrote that letter.”

“So do I, but I mean to know for certain. I will go, Sapt.”

“No, I will go,” said he.

“You may go as far as the gate.”

“I will go to the summer-house.”

“I will be hanged if you will!”

I rose and leant my back against the fireplace.

“Sapt, I believe in that woman, and I will go.”

“I do not believe in any woman,” said Sapt, “and you will not go.”

“I either go to the summer-house or go back to England,” said I.

Sapt began to know exactly how far he could lead or drive, and when he must follow.

“We are playing against time,” I added. “Every day we leave the King where he is there is fresh risk. Every day I pretend to be him like this, there is fresh risk. Sapt, we must play high; we must force the game.”

“So be it,” he said, with a sigh.

To cut the story short, at half-past eleven that night Sapt and I mounted our horses. Fritz was again left on guard, and we did not tell him where we were going. It was a very dark night. I wore no sword, but I carried a revolver, a long knife, and a bull's-eye lantern. We arrived outside the gate. I got off my horse. Sapt held out his hand.

“I will wait here,” he said. “If I hear a shot, I will--”

“Stay where you are; it is the King's only chance. You must not come to harm too.”

“You are right, boy. Good luck!”

I pushed the small gate. It opened, and I found myself in a wild garden with low plants. A path covered with grass was there. I turned right as I had been told, and I followed it carefully. My lamp was closed, and the gun was in my hand. I heard no sound. Soon a large dark shape appeared from the darkness ahead of me. It was the summer-house. I reached the steps and went up. I found a weak, broken wooden door in front of me, which hung loose. I pushed it open and walked in. A woman ran to me and took my hand.

“Shut the door,” she whispered.

I did so and turned the light of my lamp on her. She was in evening dress, dressed very expensively, and her dark striking beauty was shown fully in the bright light of the lamp. The summer-house was a bare little room. It had only a couple of chairs and a small iron table, like one sees in a tea garden or an open-air restaurant.

“Don’t talk,” she said. “We have no time. Listen! I know you, Mr. Rassendyll. I wrote that letter at the duke’s orders.”

“So I thought,” said I.

“In twenty minutes three men will be here to kill you.”

“Three--the three?”

“Yes. You must be gone by then. If not, tonight you will be killed--”

“Or they will.”

“Listen, listen! When you are killed, your body will be taken to a poor part of the town. It will be found there. Michael will at once arrest all your friends--Colonel Sapt and Captain von Tarlenheim first--announce a state of siege in Strelsau, and send a messenger to Zenda. The other three will murder the King in the Castle, and the duke will announce either himself or the princess--himself, if he is strong enough. Anyhow, he will marry her, and become king in fact, and soon in name. Do you see?”

“It is a clever plan. But why, madame, do you--?”

“Say I am a Christian--or say I am jealous. My God! shall I see him marry her? Now go; but remember--this is what I have to tell you--that never, by night or by day, are you safe. Three men follow you as a guard. Is it not so? Well, three follow them; Michael’s three are never two hundred yards from you. Your life is not worth a moment if ever they find you alone. Now go. Stay, the gate will be guarded by now. Go down softly, go past the summer-house, on for a hundred yards, and you will find a ladder against the wall. Get over it, and run for your life.”

“And you?” I asked.

“I have my game to play too. If he finds out what I have done, we shall not meet again. If not, I may yet--But never mind. Go at once.”

“But what will you tell him?”

“That you never came--that you saw through the trick.”

I took her hand and kissed it.

“Madame,” said I, “you have served the King well tonight. Where is he in the Castle?”

She sank her voice to a fearful whisper. I listened eagerly.

“Across the drawbridge you come to a heavy door; behind that lies--Hark! What is that?”

There were steps outside.

“They are coming! They are too soon! Heavens! they are too soon!” and she turned pale as death.

“They seem to me,” said I, “to be just in time.”

“Close your lamp. See, there is a small gap in the door. Can you see them?”

I put my eye to the gap. On the lowest step I saw three dim figures. I cocked my gun. Antoinette quickly laid her hand on mine.

“You may kill one,” said she. “But what then?”

A voice came from outside--a voice that spoke perfect English.

“Mr. Rassendyll,” it said.

I made no answer.

“We want to talk to you. Will you promise not to shoot till we have done?”

“Have I the pleasure of addressing Mr. Detchard?” I said.

“Never mind names.”

“Then let mine alone.”

“All right, sire. I have an offer for you.”

I still had my eye to the gap. The three had climbed two steps more; three guns pointed full at the door.

“Will you let us in? We pledge our honour to observe the truce.”

“Don’t trust them,” whispered Antoinette.

“We can speak through the door,” said I.

“But you might open it and fire,” objected Detchard; “and though we should finish you, you might finish one of us. Will you give your honour not to fire while we talk?”

“Don’t trust them,” whispered Antoinette again.

A sudden idea struck me. I considered it for a moment. It seemed possible.

“I give my honour not to fire before you do,” said I; “but I won’t let you in. Stand outside and talk.”

“That is sensible,” he said.

The three climbed the last step, and stood just outside the door. I laid my ear to the gap. I could hear no words, but Detchard’s head was close to that of the taller of his companions (De Gautet, I guessed).

“H’m! Private communications,” thought I. Then I said aloud:

“Well, gentlemen, what is the offer?”

“A safe-conduct to the frontier, and fifty thousand pounds English.”

“No, no,” whispered Antoinette in the lowest of whispers. “They are treacherous.”

“That seems handsome,” said I, looking through the gap. They were all close together, just outside the door now.

I had probed the hearts of the ruffians, and I did not need Antoinette’s warning. They meant to “rush” me as soon as I was engaged in talk.

“Give me a minute to consider,” said I; and I thought I heard a laugh outside.

I turned to Antoinette.

“Stand up close to the wall, out of the line of fire from the door,” I whispered.

“What are you going to do?” she asked in fright.

“You will see,” said I.

I took up the little iron table. It was not very heavy for a man of my strength, and I held it by the legs. The top, sticking out in front of me, made a complete screen for my head and body. I fastened my closed lamp to my belt and put my gun in a handy pocket. Suddenly I saw the door move ever so slightly--perhaps it was the wind, perhaps it was a hand trying it outside.

I drew back as far as I could from the door, holding the table in the position that I have described. Then I called out:

“Gentlemen, I accept your offer, relying on your honour. If you will open the door--”

“Open it yourself,” said Detchard.

“It opens outwards,” said I. “Stand back a little, gentlemen, or I shall hit you when I open it.”

I went and fumbled with the latch. Then I stole back to my place on tiptoe.

“I can’t open it!” I cried. “The latch has caught.”

“Tut! I will open it!” cried Detchard. “Nonsense, Bersonin, why not? Are you afraid of one man?”

I smiled to myself. A moment later the door was thrown open. The light of a lamp showed me the three of them close together outside. Their guns were pointed at me. With a shout, I ran as fast as I could across the summerhouse and through the doorway. Three shots rang out and hit my shield. Another moment, and I jumped out. The table caught them fully and straight on. They and I and that brave table fell down the steps of the summerhouse to the ground below in a rolling, swearing, struggling mass. Antoinette de Mauban gave a loud cry, but I stood up, laughing loudly.

De Gautet and Bersonin lay like men who were knocked out. Detchard was under the table. But as I stood up, he pushed it away from him and fired again. I raised my gun and took a quick shot. I heard him swear, and then I ran like a rabbit, laughing as I went. I ran past the summerhouse and along by the wall. I heard steps behind me. I turned round and fired again just in case. The steps stopped.

"Please God," I said, "she told me the truth about the ladder!" The wall was high and had iron spikes on top.

Yes, there it was. I was up and over in a minute. I ran back and saw the horses. Then I heard a shot. It was Sapt. He had heard us. He was fighting and shouting at the locked gate. He was hitting it and firing into the keyhole like a crazy man. He had quite forgotten that he was not supposed to take part in the fight. I laughed again and said, as I hit him on the shoulder:

"Come home to bed, old friend. I've got the finest tea-table story that you ever heard!"

He jumped and cried: "You're safe!" and shook my hand hard. But a moment later he added:

"And what on earth are you laughing at?"

"Four gentlemen around a tea-table," I said, still laughing. It had been very funny to see the three dangerous men completely beaten and scattered with no more deadly weapon than an ordinary tea-table.

Also, you will notice that I had kept my word in an honest way, and had not fired until they did.

CHAPTER 10. A Great Chance for a Villain

A Great Chance for a Villain

It was the custom that the Prefect of Police should send me a report every afternoon. The report was about the condition of the capital and the feeling of the people. The document also included an account of the movements of any persons whom the police had received instructions to watch. Since I had been in Strelsau, Sapt had been in the habit of reading the report. He told me any items of interest which it might contain. On the day after my adventure in the summer-house, he came in as I was playing a hand of _écarté_ with Fritz von Tarlenheim.

“The report is rather full of interest this afternoon,” he observed, sitting down.

“Do you find,” I asked, “any mention of a certain fight?”

He shook his head with a smile.

“I find this first,” he said: “‘His Highness the Duke of Strelsau left the city (so far as it appears, suddenly), accompanied by several of his household. His destination is believed to be the Castle of Zenda. But the party travelled by road and not by train. MM De Gautet, Bersonin, and Detchard followed an hour later. The last-named carried his arm in a bandage. The cause of his wound is not known. But it is suspected that he has fought a duel, probably related to a love affair.’”

“That is partly true,” I observed. I was very well pleased to find that I had left my mark on the fellow.

“Then we come to this,” pursued Sapt: “‘Madame de Mauban, whose movements have been watched according to instructions, left by train at noon. She took a ticket for Dresden--’”

“It’s an old habit of hers,” said I.

“‘The Dresden train stops at Zenda.’ An acute fellow, this. And finally listen to this: ‘The state of feeling in the city is not satisfactory. The King is much criticized’ (you know, he’s told to be quite frank) ‘for taking no steps about his marriage. From enquiries among the entourage of the Princess Flavia, her Royal Highness is believed to be deeply offended by the remissness of his Majesty. The common people are coupling her name with that of the Duke of Strelsau. And the duke gains much popularity from the suggestion.’ I have caused the announcement that the King gives a ball tonight in honour of the princess to be widely spread. And the effect is good.”

“That is news to me,” said I.

“Oh, the preparations are all made!” laughed Fritz. “I’ve seen to that.”

Sapt turned to me and said, in a sharp, decisive voice:

“You must make love to her tonight, you know.”

“I think it is very likely I shall, if I see her alone,” said I. “Hang it, Sapt, you don’t suppose I find it difficult?”

Fritz whistled a bar or two. Then he said: “You’ll find it only too easy. Look here, I hate telling you this, but I must. The Countess Helga told me that the princess had become most attached to the King. Since the coronation, her feelings have undergone a marked development. It’s quite true that she is deeply wounded by the King’s apparent neglect.”

“Here’s a kettle of fish!” I groaned.

“Tut, tut!” said Sapt. “I suppose you’ve made pretty speeches to a girl before now? That’s all she wants.”

Fritz, himself a lover, understood better my distress. He laid his hand on my shoulder, but said nothing.

“I think, though,” pursued that cold-blooded old Sapt, “that you’d better make your offer tonight.”

“Good heavens!”

“Or, at any rate, go near it: and I shall send a ‘semi-official’ to the papers.”

“I’ll do nothing of the sort--no more will you!” said I. “I utterly refuse to take part in making a fool of the princess.”

Sapt looked at me with his small keen eyes. A slow cunning smile passed over his face.

“All right, lad, all right,” said he. “We mustn’t press you too hard. Soothe her down a bit, if you can, you know. Now for Michael!”

“Oh, damn Michael!” said I. “He’ll do tomorrow. Here, Fritz, come for a stroll in the garden.”

Sapt at once yielded. His rough manner covered a wonderful tact--and as I came to recognize more and more, a remarkable knowledge of human nature. Why did he urge me so little about the princess? Because he knew that her beauty and my ardour would carry me further than all his arguments--and that the less I thought about the thing, the more likely was I to do it. He must have seen the unhappiness he might bring on the princess; but that went for nothing with him. Can I say, confidently, that he was wrong? If the King were restored, the princess must turn to him, either knowing or not knowing the change. And if the King were not restored to us? It was a subject that we had never yet spoken of. But I had an idea that, in such a case, Sapt meant to seat me on the throne of Ruritania for the term of my life. He would have set Satan himself there sooner than that pupil of his, Black Michael.

The ball was a sumptuous affair. I opened it by dancing a quadrille with Flavia: then I waltzed with her. Curious eyes and eager whispers attended us. We went in to supper; and, half way through, I, half mad by then, for her glance had answered mine, and her quick breathing met my stammered sentences--I rose in my place before all the brilliant crowd, and taking the Red Rose that I wore, flung the ribbon with its jewelled badge round her neck. In a tumult of applause I sat down: I saw Sapt smiling over his wine, and Fritz frowning. The rest of the meal passed in silence; neither Flavia nor I could speak. Fritz touched me on the shoulder, and I rose, gave her my arm, and walked down the hall into a little room, where coffee was served to us. The gentlemen and ladies in attendance withdrew, and we were alone.

The little room had French windows opening on the gardens. The night was fine, cool, and fragrant. Flavia sat down, and I stood opposite her. I was struggling with myself: if she had not looked at me, I believe that even then I should have won my fight. But suddenly, involuntarily, she gave me one brief glance--a glance of question, hurriedly turned aside; a blush that the question had ever come spread over her cheek, and she caught her breath. Ah, if you had seen her! I forgot the King in Zenda. I forgot the King in Strelsau. She was a princess--and I an impostor. Do you think I remembered that? I threw myself on my knee and seized her hands in mine. I said nothing. Why should I? The soft sounds of the night set my wooing to a wordless melody, as I pressed my kisses on her lips.

She pushed me from her, crying suddenly:

“Ah! is it true? or is it only because you must?”

“It’s true!” I said, in low smothered tones--“true that I love you more than life--or truth--or honour!”

She did not take my words seriously. She treated them as a sweet but foolish game of love. She came close to me and whispered:

"Oh, if you were not the King! Then I could show you how I love you! How is it that I love you now, Rudolf?"

"Now?"

"Yes--just lately. I--I never did before."

Pure victory filled me. It was I--Rudolf Rassendyll--who had won her! I caught her round the waist.

"You did not love me before?" I asked.

She looked up into my face, smiling, as she whispered:

"It must have been your Crown. I felt it first on the Coronation Day."

"Never before?" I asked with excitement.

She laughed low.

"You speak as if you would be pleased to hear me say 'Yes' to that," she said.

"Would 'Yes' be true?"

"Yes," I just heard her breathe, and she went on in an instant: "Be careful, Rudolf; be careful, dear. He will be mad now."

"What, Michael? If Michael were the worst--"

"What worse is there?"

There was still a chance for me. Controlling myself with a great effort, I took my hands off her and stood a yard or two away. I remember now the sound of the wind in the elm trees outside.

"If I were not the King," I began, "if I were only a private gentleman--"

Before I could finish, her hand was in mine.

"If you were a prisoner in the prison of Strelsau, you would be my King," she said.

And under my breath I said, "God forgive me!" and, holding her hand in mine, I said again:

"If I were not the King--"

"Quiet, quiet!" she whispered. "I do not deserve it--I do not deserve to be doubted. Ah, Rudolf! does a woman who marries without love look on the man as I look on you?"

And she hid her face from me.

For more than a minute we stood there together. And I, even with my arm about her, called up what honor and conscience her beauty and the hard work I was in had left me.

"Flavia," I said, in a strange dry voice that seemed not my own, "I am not--"

As I spoke--as she raised her eyes to me--there was a heavy step on the small stones outside, and a man appeared at the window. A little cry burst from Flavia, as she sprang back from me. My half-finished sentence died on my lips. Sapt stood there, bowing low, but with a hard frown on his face.

"A thousand pardons, sire," said he, "but his Eminence the Cardinal has waited this quarter of an hour to offer his respectful farewell to your Majesty."

I met his eye full and square. And I read in it an angry warning. How long he had been listening I knew not, but he had come in upon us just in time.

"We must not keep his Eminence waiting," said I.

But Flavia, in whose love there lay no shame, with bright eyes and reddening face, held out her hand to Sapt. She said nothing, but no man could have missed her meaning, who had ever seen a woman in the great joy of love. A sour, yet sad, smile passed over the old soldier's face. And there was tenderness in his voice, as bending to kiss her hand, he said:

"In joy and sorrow, in good times and bad, God save your Royal Highness!"

He paused and added, glancing at me and drawing himself up to military straightness:

"But, before all comes the King--God save the King!"

And Flavia caught at my hand and kissed it, whispering:

"Amen! Good God, Amen!"

We went into the ballroom again. Forced to receive farewells, I was separated from Flavia. Everyone, when they left me, went to her. Sapt was out and in of the crowd, and where he had been, glances, smiles, and whispers were everywhere. I did not doubt that, true to his relentless purpose, he was spreading the news that he had learnt. To uphold the Crown and beat Black Michael--that was his one resolve. Flavia, myself--yes, and the real King in Zenda, were pieces in his game. And pawns have no business with passions. Not even at the walls of the Palace did he stop. For when at last I handed Flavia down the broad marble steps and into her carriage, there was a great crowd awaiting us, and we were welcomed with deafening cheers. What could I do? Had I spoken then, they would have refused to believe that I was not the King. They might have believed that the King had run mad. By Sapt's plans and my own uncontrolled passion I had been forced on, and the way back had closed behind me. And the passion still drove me in the same direction as the plans tempted me. I faced all Strelsau that night as the King and the accepted suitor of the Princess Flavia.

At last, at three in the morning, when the cold light of dawning day began to steal in, I was in my dressing-room, and Sapt alone was with me. I sat like a man dazed, staring into the fire. He puffed at his pipe. Fritz was gone to bed, having almost refused to speak to me. On the table by me lay a rose. It had been in Flavia's dress, and, as we parted, she had kissed it and given it to me.

Sapt moved his hand towards the rose, but, with a quick movement, I shut mine down upon it.

"That's mine," I said, "not yours--nor the King's either."

"We struck a good blow for the King tonight," said he.

I turned on him fiercely.

"What's to prevent me striking a blow for myself?" I said.

He nodded his head.

"I know what's in your mind," he said. "Yes, lad. But you're bound in honor."

"Have you left me any honor?"

"Oh, come, to play a little trick on a girl--"

"You can spare me that. Colonel Sapt, if you would not have me utterly a villain--if you would not have your King rot in Zenda, while Michael and I play for the great stake outside--You follow me?"

"Yes, I follow you."

"We must act, and quickly! You saw tonight--you heard--tonight--"

"I did," said he.

"Your cursed sharpness told you what I should do. Well, leave me here a week--and there's another problem for you. Do you find the answer?"

"Yes, I find it," he answered, frowning heavily. "But if you did that, you'd have to fight me first--and kill me."

"Well, and if I had--or a score of men? I tell you, I could raise all Strelsau on you in an hour, and choke you with your lies--yes, your mad lies--in your mouth."

"It's gospel truth," he said--"thanks to my advice you could."

"I could marry the princess, and send Michael and his brother together to--"

"I'm not denying it, lad," said he.

"Then, in God's name," I cried, stretching out my hands to him, "let us go to Zenda and crush this Michael and bring the King back to his own again." The old fellow stood and looked at me for full a minute.

"And the princess?" he said.

I bowed my head to meet my hands. I crushed the rose between my fingers and my lips.

I felt his hand on my shoulder. His voice sounded low and rough as he whispered in my ear:

"Before God, you are the finest Elphberg of them all. But I have eaten of the King's bread, and I am the King's servant. Come, we will go to Zenda!"

And I looked up and caught him by the hand. And the eyes of both of us were wet.

CHAPTER 11. Hunting a Very Big Boar

Hunting a Very Big Boar

The terrible temptation that was attacking me will now be understood. I would force Michael’s hand so that he must kill the King. I was in a position to stand against him and hold the crown more tightly. This was not for its own sake. It was because the King of Ruritania was to marry the Princess Flavia. What about Sapt and Fritz? Ah! But a man cannot be held to write down in cold blood the wild and black thoughts that storm his brain. This happens when an uncontrolled passion has broken a way for them. Yet, unless he sets up as a saint, he need not hate himself for them. He is better employed, as it humbly seems to me, in giving thanks that power to resist was given to him. He should not worry over wicked impulses that come unsought and force an unwilling welcome from the weakness of our nature.

It was a fine bright morning when I walked, alone, to the princess’s house. I carried a small bunch of flowers in my hand. Policy made excuses for love. Every attention that I paid her tied my own chains more tightly. But it also bound closer to me the people of the great city, who worshipped her. I found Fritz’s inamorata, the Countess Helga, gathering flowers in the garden for her mistress to wear. I persuaded her to take mine in their place. The girl was rosy with happiness. Fritz, in his turn, had not wasted his evening. No dark shadow hung over his wooing, except the hatred that the Duke of Strelsau was known to bear him.

“And that,” she said, with a mischievous smile, “your Majesty has made of no importance. Yes, I will take the flowers. Shall I tell you, sire, what is the first thing the princess does with them?”

We were talking on a broad terrace that ran along the back of the house. A window above our heads stood open.

“Madame!” cried the countess merrily, and Flavia herself looked out. I took off my hat and bowed. She wore a white gown, and her hair was loosely gathered in a knot. She kissed her hand to me, crying:

“Bring the King up, Helga; I’ll give him some coffee.”

The countess, with a gay glance, led the way, and took me into Flavia’s morning-room. And, left alone, we greeted one another as lovers do. Then the princess laid two letters before me. One was from Black Michael. It was a most courteous request that she would honour him by spending a day at his Castle of Zenda. This had been her custom once a year in the summer, when the place and its gardens were at the height of their great beauty. I threw the letter down in disgust, and Flavia laughed at me. Then, growing grave again, she pointed to the other sheet.

“I don’t know who that comes from,” she said. “Read it.”

I knew in a moment. There was no signature at all this time. But the handwriting was the same as that which had told me of the trap in the summer-house. It was Antoinette de Mauban’s.

“I have no cause to love you,” it ran, “but God forbid that you should fall into the power of the duke. Accept no invitations of his. Go nowhere without a large guard—a regiment is not too much to make you safe. Show this, if you can, to him who reigns in Strelsau.”

“Why doesn’t it say ‘the King’?” asked Flavia, leaning over my shoulder. The ripple of her hair played on my cheek. “Is it a hoax?”

“As you value life, and more than life, my queen,” I said, “obey it to the very letter. A regiment shall camp round your house today. See that you do not go out unless well guarded.”

“An order, sire?” she asked, a little rebellious.

“Yes, an order, madame—if you love me.”

“Ah!” she cried; and I could not but kiss her.

“You know who sent it?” she asked.

“I guess,” said I. “It is from a good friend—and I fear, an unhappy woman. You must be ill, Flavia, and unable to go to Zenda. Make your excuses as cold and formal as you like.”

“So you feel strong enough to anger Michael?” she said, with a proud smile.

“I’m strong enough for anything, while you are safe,” said I.

Soon I tore myself away from her. Then, without consulting Sapt, I took my way to the house of Marshal Strakencz. I had seen something of the old general, and I liked and trusted him. Sapt was less enthusiastic. But I had learnt by now that Sapt was best pleased when he could do everything. Jealousy played some part in his views. As things were now, I had more work than Sapt and Fritz could manage. They must come with me to Zenda. And I wanted a man to guard what I loved most in all the world. I wanted to set about my task of releasing the King with a quiet mind.

The Marshal received me with most loyal kindness. To some extent, I took him into my confidence. I charged him with the care of the princess. I looked him full and meaningfully in the face as I bade him let no one from her cousin the duke approach her. This was unless he himself were there and a dozen of his men with him.

“You may be right, sire,” said he, shaking his grey head sadly. “I have known better men than the duke do worse things than that for love.”

I could quite appreciate the remark, but I said:

“There’s something beside love, Marshal. Love’s for the heart. Is there nothing my brother might like for his head?”

“I pray that you wrong him, sire.”

“Marshal, I’m leaving Strelsau for a few days. Every evening I will send a courier to you. If for three days none comes, you will publish an order which I will give you. It deprives Duke Michael of the governorship of Strelsau and appoints you in his place. You will declare a state of siege. Then you will send word to Michael that you demand an audience of the King—You follow me?”

“Ay, sire.”

“—In twenty-four hours. If he does not produce the King” (I laid my hand on his knee), “then the King is dead, and you will proclaim the next heir. You know who that is?”

“The Princess Flavia.”

“And swear to me, on your faith and honour and by the fear of the living God, that you will stand by her to the death. Kill that reptile, and seat her where I sit now.”

“On my faith and honour, and by the fear of God, I swear it! And may Almighty God preserve your Majesty, for I think that you go on an errand of danger.”

“I hope that no life more precious than mine may be demanded,” said I, rising. Then I held out my hand to him.

"Marshal," I said, "in days to come, it may be—I do not know—that you will hear strange things of the man who speaks to you now. Let him be what he may, and who he may, what do you say of the way he has carried himself as King in Strelsau?"

The old man held my hand and spoke to me, man to man.

"I have known many of the Elphbergs," he said, "and I have seen you. And whatever may happen, you have carried yourself as a wise King and a strong man. Yes, and you have proved as good a gentleman and as strong a lover as any that have been of the House."

"Let that be my last word," I said, "when the time comes that another sits on the seat of power in Ruritania."

"God send a far day, and may I not see it!" he said.

I was much moved, and the Marshal's worn face moved. I sat down and wrote my order.

"I can hardly write yet," I said. "My finger is still stiff."

In fact, it was the first time that I had dared to write more than a signature. I had taken pains to learn the King's hand, but I was not yet perfect in it.

"Indeed, sire," he said, "it differs a little from your usual way of writing. It is bad, for it may lead to a suspicion of a false paper."

"Marshal," I said, with a laugh, "what use are the guns of Strelsau, if they cannot quiet a little suspicion?"

He smiled with a hard face, and took the paper.

"Colonel Sapt and Fritz von Tarlenheim go with me," I continued.

"You go to seek the duke?" he asked in a low tone.

"Yes, the duke, and someone else of whom I have need, and who is at Zenda," I replied.

"I wish I could go with you," he cried, pulling at his white hair on his lip. "I would like to strike a blow for you and your title."

"I leave you what is more than my life and more than my title," I said, "because you are the man I trust more than all others in Ruritania."

"I will deliver her to you safe and sound," he said, "and, failing that, I will make her ruler."

We parted, and I returned to the Palace and told Sapt and Fritz what I had done. Sapt had a few faults to find and a few complaints to make. This was merely what I expected, for Sapt liked to be consulted before, not informed after. But on the whole he approved of my plans, and his spirits rose high as the hour of action drew nearer and nearer. Fritz, too, was ready. Though he, poor fellow, risked more than Sapt did, for he was a lover, and his happiness hung in the scale. Yet how I wished I were him! The happy end which would bring him happiness and unite him to his lady, the success for which we were bound to hope and work and struggle, meant to me sadness more certain and greater than if I were sure to fail. He understood something of this. When we were alone (save for old Sapt, who was smoking at the other end of the room), he passed his arm through mine, saying:

"It is hard for you. Do not think I do not trust you. I know you have nothing but true thoughts in your heart."

But I turned away from him, glad that he could not see what my heart held. He could only see the acts that my hands were to do.

Yet even he did not understand, for he had not dared to lift his eyes to the Princess Flavia, as I had lifted mine.

Our plans were now all made, even as we went on to carry them out, and as they will later appear. The next morning we were to start on the hunting trip. I had made all arrangements for being away, and now there was only one thing left to do—the hardest, the most heart-breaking. As evening fell, I drove through the busy streets to Flavia's residence. I was recognized as I went and loudly cheered. I played my part, and made shift to look the happy lover. In spite of my low spirits, I was almost amused at the coldness and fine pride with which my sweet lover received me. She had heard that the King was leaving Strelsau on a hunting trip.

"I regret that we cannot please your Majesty here in Strelsau," she said, tapping her foot lightly on the floor. "I would have offered you more entertainment, but I was silly enough to think—"

"Well, what?" I asked, leaning over her.

"That just for a day or two after—after last night—you might be happy without much gaiety." And she turned away from me crossly, as she added, "I hope the wild pigs will be more interesting."

"I am going after a very big wild pig," I said. And because I could not help it, I began to play with her hair, but she moved her head away.

"Are you angry with me?" I asked, in pretended surprise, for I could not resist teasing her a little. I had never seen her angry, and every fresh side of her was a delight to me.

"What right have I to be angry? True, you said last night that every hour away from me was wasted. But a very big wild pig! That is a different thing."

"Perhaps the wild pig will hunt me," I suggested. "Perhaps, Flavia, he will catch me."

She made no answer.

"You are not touched even by that danger?"

Still she said nothing. And I, moving round, found her eyes full of tears.

"You cry for my danger?"

Then she spoke very low:

"This is like what you used to be. But not like the King—the King I—I have come to love!"

With a sudden great groan, I caught her to my heart.

"My dear!" I cried, forgetting everything but her. "Did you dream that I left you to go hunting?"

"What then, Rudolf? Ah! You are not going—?"

"Well, it is hunting. I go to seek Michael in his hiding place."

She had turned very pale.

"So, you see, sweet, I was not so poor a lover as you thought me. I shall not be long gone."

"You will write to me, Rudolf?"

I was weak, but I could not say a word to raise doubt in her.

"I will send you all my heart every day," I said.

"And you will run no danger?"

"None that I need not."

"And when will you be back? Ah, how long will it be!"

"When shall I be back?" I repeated.

"Yes, yes! Do not be long, dear, do not be long. I shall not sleep while you are away."

"I do not know when I shall be back," I said.

"Soon, Rudolf, soon?"

"God knows, my dear. But, if never—"

"Quiet, quiet!" and she pressed her lips to mine.

"If never," I whispered, "you must take my place. You will be the only one of the House then. You must rule, and not cry for me."

For a moment she drew herself up like a very queen.

“Yes, I will!” she said. “I will rule. I will do my part, even if all my life will be empty and my heart dead. Yet I will do it!”

She stopped, and leaned against me again. She cried softly.

“Come soon! Come soon!”

I was carried away. I cried out:

“As God lives, I—yes, I myself—will see you once more before I die!”

“What do you mean?” she said, with wondering eyes. But I had no answer for her. She looked at me with her wondering eyes.

I did not dare to ask her to forget. She would have found it an insult. I could not tell her then who I was and what I was. She was crying, and I only had to dry her tears.

“Shall a man not come back to the loveliest lady in all the wide world?” I said. “A thousand Michaels should not keep me from you!”

She held on to me, a little comforted.

“You will not let Michael hurt you?”

“No, my dear.”

“Or keep you from me?”

“No, my dear.”

“Nor anyone else?”

And again I answered:

“No, my dear.”

Yet there was one—not Michael—who, if he lived, must keep me from her. For his life I was going out to risk my own. And his shape—the thin, lively shape I had met in the woods of Zenda—the heavy, still mass I had left in the cellar of the hunting-lodge—seemed to rise before me, double-shaped. It seemed to come between us. It pushed itself in even where she lay, pale, tired out, fainting, in my arms. And yet she looked up at me with those eyes that held such love as I have never seen. They follow me now, and will follow me until the ground closes over me—and, who knows, perhaps beyond.

I Receive a Visitor and Bait a Hook

About five miles from Zenda--on the opposite side from the one where the Castle is located--there is a large area of wood. The ground rises there. In the middle of the land, on top of the hill, stands a fine modern house. It belongs to a distant relative of Fritz's, the Count Stanislas von Tarlenheim. Count Stanislas himself was a student and a person who liked to be alone. He rarely visited the house. At Fritz's request, he had very willingly and kindly offered me its hospitality for myself and my group. This, then, was our destination. We chose it apparently for the wild pig hunting (for the wood was carefully protected, and wild pigs, once common all over Ruritania, were still found there in large numbers). But really we chose it because it brought us close enough to strike at the Duke of Strelsau's more magnificent home on the other side of the town. A large group of servants, with horses and bags, started early in the morning. We followed at noon. We traveled by train for thirty miles. Then we got on our horses to ride the remaining distance to the house.

We were a fine-looking group. Besides Sapt and Fritz, ten gentlemen came with me. My two friends had chosen every one of them carefully and tested them just as carefully. All were deeply attached to the King himself. They were told part of the truth. The attempt on my life in the summer house was revealed to them. This encouraged their loyalty and stirred them against Michael. They were also told that a friend of the King's was suspected to be held by force inside the Castle of Zenda. His rescue was one of the goals of the journey. But, it was added, the King's main wish was to take certain steps against his dangerous brother. They could not at present be told more about the exact nature of these steps. It was enough that the King commanded their services. He would rely on their support when the occasion arose to call for it. Young, well bred, courageous, and faithful, they asked no more. They were ready to prove their dutiful obedience. They prayed for a fight as the best and most exciting way to show it.

So the scene moved from Strelsau to the house of Tarlenheim and Castle of Zenda, which looked down at us across the valley. I tried to move my thoughts too. I tried to forget my love and to bend all my energy to the task before me. It was to get the King out of the Castle alive. Force was useless. The chance lay in some trick. And I already had a hint of what we must do. But I was terribly held back by the public attention that followed my movements. Michael must know by now of my journey. And I knew Michael too well to suppose that his eyes would be fooled by the pretend wild pig hunt. He would understand very well what the real target was. That, however, had to be risked--that and all it might mean. For Sapt, no less than myself, recognized that the present state of things had become too hard to bear. And there was one thing I dared to count on--not, as I now know, without good reason. It was this--that Black Michael would not believe that I meant well by the King. He could not appreciate--I will not say an honest man, for the thoughts of my own heart have been revealed--but a man acting honestly. He saw my opportunity as I had seen it, as Sapt had seen it. He knew the princess--no (and I declare that a secret sort of pity for him came over me), in his way he loved her. He would think that Sapt and Fritz could be paid off, if the payment was large enough. Thinking this, would he kill the King, my rival and my danger? Yes, truly, he would, with as little regret as he would kill a rat. But he would kill Rudolf Rassendyll first, if he could. And nothing but the certainty of being completely ruined by the release of the King alive and his return to the throne would drive him to throw away the best card he held in reserve to block the supposed game of the rude impostor Rassendyll. Thinking about all this as I rode along, I took courage.

Michael knew of my coming, sure enough. I had not been in the house an hour when an imposing group of messengers arrived from him. He did not quite reach the rudeness of sending my would-be murderers. But he sent the other three of his famous Six--the three Ruritanian gentlemen--Lauengram, Krafstein, and Rupert Hentzau. A fine, strong trio they were, splendidly horsed and admirably equipped. Young Rupert, who looked a daredevil, and could not have been more than twenty-two or twenty-three, took the lead. He made us the neatest speech. In it, my devoted subject and loving brother Michael of Strelsau prayed me to forgive him for not paying his addresses in person. He also asked forgiveness for not putting his Castle at my disposal. The reason for both of these apparent failures was that he and several of his servants lay sick of scarlet fever. They were in a very sad, and also a very infectious state. So declared young Rupert with an insolent smile on his curling upper lip and a toss of his thick hair. He was a handsome villain. The gossip ran that many a lady had troubled her heart for him already.

"If my brother has scarlet fever," said I, "he is nearer my complexion than he is wont to be, my lord. I trust he does not suffer?"

"He is able to attend to his affairs, sire."

"I hope all beneath your roof are not sick. What of my good friends, De Gautet, Bersonin, and Detchard? I heard the last had suffered a hurt."

Lauengram and Krafstein looked glum and uneasy. But young Rupert's smile grew broader.

"He hopes soon to find a medicine for it, sire," he answered.

And I burst out laughing, for I knew what medicine Detchard longed for--it is called Revenge.

"You will dine with us, gentlemen?" I asked.

Young Rupert was profuse in apologies. They had urgent duties at the Castle.

"Then," said I, with a wave of my hand, "to our next meeting, gentlemen. May it make us better acquainted."

"We will pray your Majesty for an early opportunity," quoth Rupert airily. And he strode past Sapt with such jeering scorn on his face that I saw the old fellow clench his fist and scowl black as night.

For my part, if a man must needs be a bad man, I would have him a cheerful bad man. And I liked Rupert Hentzau better than his long-faced, close-eyed companions. It makes your sin no worse, as I conceive, to do it _à la mode_ and stylishly.

Now it was a strange thing that on this first night, instead of eating the excellent dinner my cooks had prepared for me, I had to leave my gentlemen to eat it alone, under Sapt’s care, and ride myself with Fritz to the town of Zenda and a certain little hotel that I knew of. There was little danger on this short trip. The evenings were long and light, and the road this side of Zenda was busy. So off we rode, with a servant behind us. I wrapped myself up in a big coat.

“Fritz,” said I, as we entered the town, “there is a very pretty girl at this hotel.”

“How do you know?” he asked.

“Because I have been there,” said I.

“Since--?” he began.

“No. Before,” said I.

“But they will recognize you?”

“Well, of course they will. Now, do not argue, my good fellow, but listen to me. We are two gentlemen of the King’s household, and one of us has a toothache. The other will order a private room and dinner, and also a bottle of the best wine for the sick man. And if he is as smart a fellow as I think he is, the pretty girl and no other will serve us.”

“What if she will not?” objected Fritz.

“My dear Fritz,” said I, “if she will not for you, she will for me.”

We were at the hotel. Nothing of me but my eyes could be seen as I walked in. The owner’s wife received us. Two minutes later, my little friend made her appearance. She was always, I fear, looking out for such guests as might prove amusing. Dinner and the wine were ordered. I sat down in the private room. A minute later Fritz came in.

“She is coming,” he said.

“If she were not, I should have to doubt the Countess Helga’s taste.”

She came in. I gave her time to set the wine down. I did not want it dropped. Fritz poured out a glass and gave it to me.

“Is the gentleman in great pain?” the girl asked, with kind feeling.

“The gentleman is no worse than when he saw you last,” said I, throwing away my coat.

She started, with a little cry. Then she cried:

“It was the King, then! I told mother so the moment I saw his picture. Oh, sir, forgive me!”

“Faith, you gave me nothing that hurt much,” said I.

“But the things we said!”

“I forgive them for the thing you did.”

“I must go and tell mother.”

“Stop,” said I, taking a more serious manner. “We are not here for fun tonight. Go and bring dinner, and not a word of the King being here.”

She came back in a few minutes, looking serious, yet very curious.

“Well, how is Johann?” I asked, beginning my dinner.

“Oh, that fellow, sir--my lord King, I mean!”

“‘Sir’ will do, please. How is he?”

“We hardly see him now, sir.”

“And why not?”

“I told him he came too often, sir,” said she, tossing her head.

“So he is angry and stays away?”

“Yes, sir.”

“But you could bring him back?” I suggested with a smile.

“Perhaps I could,” said she.

“I know your powers, you see,” said I, and she went red with pleasure.

“It is not only that, sir, that keeps him away. He is very busy at the Castle.”

“But there is no shooting on now.”

“No, sir; but he is in charge of the house.”

“Johann turned house servant?”

The little girl was full of talk.

“Well, there are no others,” said she. “There is not a woman there--not as a servant, I mean. They do say--but perhaps it is false, sir.”

“Let us have it for what it is worth,” said I.

“Indeed, I am ashamed to tell you, sir.”

“Oh, see, I am looking at the ceiling.”

“They do say there is a lady there, sir; but, except for her, there is not a woman in the place. And Johann has to wait on the gentlemen.”

“Poor Johann! He must be overworked. Yet I am sure he could find half an hour to come and see you.”

“It would depend on the time, sir, perhaps.”

“Do you love him?” I asked.

“Not I, sir.”

“And you wish to serve the King?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Then tell him to meet you at the second mile marker out of Zenda tomorrow evening at ten. Say you will be there and will walk home with him.”

“Do you mean him harm, sir?”

“Not if he will do as I tell him. But I think I have told you enough, my pretty girl. See that you do as I tell you. And, mind, no one is to know that the King has been here.”

I spoke a little strictly, for there is seldom harm in adding a little fear to a woman’s liking for you. I softened the effect by giving her a fine present. Then we dined, and, wrapping my coat about my face, with Fritz leading the way, we went downstairs to our horses again.

It was only half past eight, and hardly yet dark. The streets were full for such a quiet little place, and I could see that talk was everywhere. With the King on one side and the duke on the other, Zenda felt itself the centre of all Ruritania. We rode gently through the town, but set our horses to a sharper pace when we reached the open country.

“You want to catch this fellow Johann?” asked Fritz.

“Yes, and I think I have baited the hook right. Our little Delilah will bring our Samson. It is not enough, Fritz, to have no women in a house, though brother Michael shows some wisdom there. If you want safety, you must have none within fifty miles.”

“None nearer than Strelsau, for instance,” said poor Fritz, with a lovelorn sigh.

We reached the avenue of the chateau, and were soon at the house. As the hoofs of our horses sounded on the gravel, Sapt rushed out to meet us.

“Thank God, you are safe!” he cried. “Have you seen anything of them?”

“Of whom?” I asked, getting off my horse.

He drew us aside, so the servants might not hear.

“Lad,” he said to me, “you must not ride about here, unless with half a dozen of us. You know among our men a tall young fellow, Bernenstein by name?”

I knew him. He was a fine strong young man, almost of my height, and of light complexion.

“He lies in his room upstairs, with a bullet through his arm.”

“The devil he does!”

“After dinner he walked out alone, and went a mile or so into the wood. As he walked, he thought he saw three men among the trees. And one aimed a gun at him. He had no weapon, and he started at a run back towards the house. But one of them fired, and he was hit. He had much trouble to reach here before he fainted. By good luck, they feared to follow him nearer the house.”

He paused and added:

“Lad, the bullet was meant for you.”

“It is very likely,” said I, “and it is first blood to brother Michael.”

“I wonder which three it was,” said Fritz.

"Well, Sapt," I said, "I went out tonight for a real reason, as you will hear. But one thing is on my mind."

"What is that?" he asked.

"Why this," I answered. "Ruritania has given me very great honors. I will pay them back badly if I leave this country while one of those Six is still alive. And with God's help, I will not."

And Sapt shook my hand on that.

CHAPTER 13. An Improvement on Jacob’s Ladder

An Improvement on Jacob’s Ladder

On the morning of the day after I swore my oath against the Six, I gave some orders. Then I rested with more contentment than I had felt for some time. I was at work. Work cannot cure love, but it is like a drug for it. Sapt grew feverish. He was surprised to see me lying in an armchair in the sunshine. I was listening to one of my friends. He sang me love songs in a soft voice. They caused a pleasing sadness in me. I was doing this when young Rupert Hentzau came. He feared neither man nor devil. He rode through the demesne as if it were the park at Strelsau. Every tree there might hide a marksman, but he did not care. He cantered up to where I lay. He bowed with mocking respect. He asked to speak with me alone. He wanted to deliver a message from the Duke of Strelsau. I sent everyone away. Then he sat down by me and said:

“The King is in love, it seems?”

“Not with life, my lord,” I said, smiling.

“It is well,” he replied. “Come, we are alone, Rassendyll--”

I rose to a sitting position.

“What is the matter?” he asked.

“I was about to call one of my gentlemen to bring your horse, my lord. If you do not know how to address the King, my brother must find another messenger.”

“Why keep up the farce?” he asked. He carelessly dusted his boot with his glove.

“Because it is not finished yet. And for now I will choose my own name.”

“Oh, so be it! Yet I spoke in love for you. For indeed you are a man after my own heart.”

“Except for my poor honesty,” I said, “maybe I am. But I keep faith with men, and honour with women. Maybe I am, my lord.”

He darted a glance at me. It was a glance of anger.

“Is your mother dead?” I said.

“Ay, she’s dead.”

“She may thank God,” I said. I heard him curse me softly. “Well, what is the message?” I continued.

I had touched him on the raw. All the world knew he had broken his mother’s heart. He had flaunted his mistresses in her house. His airy manner was gone for the moment.

“The duke offers you more than I would,” he growled. “A halter for you, sire, was my suggestion. But he offers you safe-conduct across the frontier and a million crowns.”

“I prefer your offer, my lord, if I am bound to one.”

“You refuse?”

“Of course.”

“I told Michael you would.” The villain’s temper was restored. He gave me the sunniest of smiles. “The fact is, between ourselves,” he continued, “Michael doesn’t understand a gentleman.”

I began to laugh.

“And you?” I asked.

“I do,” he said. “Well, well, the halter be it.”

“I’m sorry you won’t live to see it,” I observed.

“Has his Majesty done me the honour to fasten a particular quarrel on me?”

“I would you were a few years older, though.”

“Oh, God gives years, but the devil gives increase,” he laughed. “I can hold my own.”

“How is your prisoner?” I asked.

“The K--?”

“Your prisoner.”

“I forgot your wishes, sire. Well, he is alive.”

He rose to his feet. I imitated him. Then, with a smile, he said:

“And the pretty princess? Faith, I’ll wager the next Elphberg will be red enough, for all that Black Michael will be called his father.”

I sprang a step towards him. I clenched my hand. He did not move an inch. His lip curled in insolent amusement.

“Go, while your skin’s whole!” I muttered. He had repaid me with interest my hit about his mother.

Then came the most audacious thing I have known in my life. My friends were some thirty yards away. Rupert called to a groom to bring him his horse. He dismissed the fellow with a crown. The horse stood near. I stood still. I suspected nothing. Rupert made as though to mount. Then he suddenly turned to me. His left hand rested in his belt. His right was outstretched. “Shake hands,” he said.

I bowed. I did as he had foreseen. I put my hands behind me. Quicker than thought, his left hand darted out at me. A small dagger flashed in the air. He struck me in the left shoulder. Had I not swerved, it would have been my heart. With a cry, I staggered back. Without touching the stirrup, he leapt upon his horse. He was off like an arrow. Cries and revolver shots pursued him. The last were as useless as the first. I sank into my chair, bleeding profusely. I watched the devil’s brat disappear down the long avenue. My friends surrounded me. Then I fainted.

I suppose that I was put to bed. I lay there, unconscious or half conscious, for many hours. It was night when I awoke to my full mind. I found Fritz beside me. I was weak and weary. But he told me to be of good cheer. He said my wound would soon heal. He said all had gone well meanwhile. Johann, the keeper, had fallen into the snare we had laid for him. He was even now in the house.

“And the queer thing is,” pursued Fritz, “that I fancy he’s not altogether sorry to find himself here. He seems to think that when Black Michael has brought off his coup, witnesses of how it was effected will not be at a premium. Saving, of course, the Six themselves.”

This idea argued a shrewdness in our captive. It led me to build hopes on his assistance. I ordered him to be brought in at once. Sapt conducted him. He set him in a chair by my bedside. He was sullen and afraid. But, to say truth, after young Rupert’s exploit, we also had our fears. He got as far as possible from Sapt’s formidable six-shooter. Sapt kept him as far as he could from me. Moreover, when he came in his hands were bound. But I would not suffer that.

I need not stay to recount the safeguards and rewards we promised the fellow. All of them were honourably observed and paid. So he lives now in prosperity (though where I may not mention). We were the more free because we soon learnt that he was rather a weak man than a wicked one. He had acted throughout this matter more from fear of the duke and of his own brother Max than for any love of what was done. But he had persuaded all of his loyalty. He was not in their secret counsels. Yet, by his knowledge of their dispositions within the Castle, he was able to lay bare before us the very heart of their devices. And here, in brief, is his story:

Below the ground level in the Castle, there were two small rooms cut out of the rock itself. You reached them by a set of stone steps at the end of the drawbridge. The outer room had no windows. It was always lit with candles. The inner room had one square window that looked out on the moat. In the outer room, three of the Six stayed day and night. Duke Michael's orders were these. If anyone attacked the outer room, the three men should defend its door as long as they could without danger to themselves. But if the door was in danger of being forced open, then Rupert Hentzau or Detchard (one of these two was always there) should leave the others to hold it as long as they could. He himself should go into the inner room. Then, without more delay, he should kill the King who lay there. The King was treated well, it is true, but he had no weapons. His arms were held in fine steel chains. These did not let him move his elbow more than three inches from his side. So before the outer door was broken open, the King would be dead. And his body? His body would be evidence as strong against them as the King himself.

"Nay, sir," said Johann, "his Highness has thought of that. While the two hold the outer room, the one who has killed the King unlocks the bars in the square window (they turn on a hinge). The window now gives no light, for its mouth is blocked by a great pipe of earthenware. This pipe is large enough to let a man's body pass through it. It goes into the moat and ends just above the surface of the water. So there is no visible gap between water and pipe. When the King is dead, his killer quickly ties a weight to the body. He drags it to the window and raises it with a pulley (Detchard has provided one, in case the weight should prove too great) until it is level with the mouth of the pipe. He puts the feet in the pipe and pushes the body down. Silently, without a splash or a sound, it falls into the water and from there to the bottom of the moat, which is twenty feet deep thereabouts. This done, the killer calls out loudly, 'All's well!' and slides down the pipe himself. The others, if they can and the attack is not too heavy, run to the inner room. They seek a moment's delay, bar the door, and in their turn slide down. And though the King does not rise from the bottom, they rise and swim round to the other side. There men wait for them with ropes, to pull them out, and horses. And here, if things go badly, the duke will join them and seek safety by riding away. But if all goes well, they will return to the Castle and have their enemies in a trap. That, sir, is his Highness's plan for dealing with the King in case of need. But it is not to be used until the last. For, as we all know, he does not mean to kill the King unless he can, before or soon after, kill you also, sir. Now, sir, I have spoken the truth, as God is my witness. And I pray you to protect me from the vengeance of Duke Michael. For if, after he knows what I have done, I fall into his hands, I shall pray for one thing out of all the world--a quick death, and that I shall not get from him!"

The fellow's story was told in a rough way, but our questions added to his account. What he had told us was about an armed attack. But if people became suspicious, and overwhelming force came--such as I, the King, could bring--then the idea of resistance would be given up. The King would be quietly killed and slid down the pipe. And here comes a clever touch. One of the Six would take his place in the cell. When the searchers entered, he would loudly demand to be released and to have his case heard. Michael would be called, and he would admit to acting too quickly. But he would say the man had angered him by seeking the favour of a lady in the Castle (this was Antoinette de Mauban). He had shut him up there, as he believed he had the right to do as Lord of Zenda. But now, on receiving his apology, he was content to let him go. And so he would end the gossip which, to his Highness's annoyance, had arisen about a prisoner in Zenda. That gossip had given his visitors the trouble of this enquiry. The visitors, confused, would leave. And Michael could, when he wished, get rid of the King's body.

Sapt, Fritz, and I in my bed looked round at one another in horror and confusion at the cruelty and cunning of the plan. Whether I came in peace or in war, openly at the head of a corps or secretly by a quiet attack, the King would be dead before I could come near him. If Michael were stronger and beat my party, that would be the end. But if I were stronger, I should have no way to punish him. I should have no means of proving any guilt in him without also proving my own guilt. On the other hand, I should be left as King (ah! for a moment my pulse quickened), and it would be for the future to see the final struggle between him and me. He seemed to have made victory possible and ruin impossible. At the worst, he would stand as well as he had stood before I crossed his path--with only one man between him and the throne, and that man a false king. At best, there would be no one left to stand against him. I had begun to think that Black Michael was too fond of leaving the fighting to his friends. But now I admitted that the brains, if not the arms, of the conspiracy were his.

"Does the King know this?" I asked.

“My brother and I,” answered Johann, “put up the pipe. We did it under the orders of my Lord of Hentzau. He was on guard that day. The King asked my lord what it meant. ‘Faith,’ he answered, with his light laugh, ‘it is a new improvement on the ladder of Jacob. As you have read, sire, men pass from the earth to heaven by it. We thought it not right that your Majesty should go by the common route, in case you must go, sire. So we have made you a pretty private passage. There the common people cannot stare at you or trouble your passage. That, sire, is the meaning of that pipe.’ And he laughed and bowed. He asked the King’s leave to fill the King’s glass again--for the King was at supper. The King is a brave man, as are all of his House. But he grew red and then white as he looked on the pipe and at the merry devil who laughed at him. Ah, sir” (and the fellow shook with fear), “it is not easy to sleep quiet in the Castle of Zenda. All of them would as soon cut a man’s throat as play a game at cards. And my Lord Rupert would choose it sooner for a pastime than any other--yes, sooner than he would ruin a woman, though that he loves also.”

The man stopped, and I told Fritz to take him away and have him carefully guarded. And, turning to him, I added:

“If anyone asks you if there is a prisoner in Zenda, you may answer ‘Yes.’ But if any asks who the prisoner is, do not answer. For all my promises will not save you if any man here learns from you the truth about the prisoner of Zenda. I will kill you like a dog if the thing is so much as breathed within the house!”

Then, when he was gone, I looked at Sapt.

“It is a hard nut!” said I.

“So hard,” said he, shaking his grey head, “that as I think, this time next year is like to find you still King of Ruritania!” and he broke out into curses on Michael’s cunning.

I lay back on my pillows.

“There seems to me,” I observed, “to be two ways by which the King can come out of Zenda alive. One is by treachery in the duke’s followers.”

“You can leave that out,” said Sapt.

“I hope not,” I rejoined, “because the other I was about to mention is--by a miracle from heaven!”

CHAPTER 14. A Night Outside the Castle

A Night Outside the Castle

It would have surprised the good people of Ruritania to know of the talk above. The official reports said I had suffered a very bad and dangerous hurt from a spear wound by accident. I had received it during my sport. I made the public reports very serious. This created great public excitement. Three things happened because of this. First, I seriously made the medical men of Strelsau angry by refusing to call any of them to my bed. I called only a young man, a friend of Fritz’s, whom we could trust. Second, I received word from Marshal Strakencz. He said my orders seemed to have no more weight than his. He also said that the Princess Flavia was leaving for Tarlenheim under his unwilling guard. I tried not to be glad and proud at this news. Third, my brother, the Duke of Strelsau, was too well informed to believe the account of how my sickness began. But the reports and my seeming inactivity persuaded him that I was truly unable to act. He also believed my life was in some danger. I learned this from the man Johann. I was forced to trust him and send him back to Zenda. By the way, Rupert Hentzau had him soundly beaten there. This was because he dared to damage the morals of Zenda by staying out all night in the pursuit of love. Johann deeply resented this from Rupert. The duke’s approval of it did more to tie the keeper to my side than all my promises.

I cannot dwell on Flavia’s arrival. Her joy at finding me up and well, instead of on my back and fighting with death, makes a picture that even now dances before my eyes till they grow too dim to see it. Her reproaches that I had not trusted even her must excuse the means I took to quiet them. In truth, to have her with me once more was like a taste of heaven to a damned soul. It was the sweeter for the inevitable doom that was to follow. I rejoiced in being able to waste two whole days with her. And when I had wasted two days, the Duke of Strelsau arranged a hunting party.

The stroke was near now. Sapt and I had had anxious consultations. We had resolved that we must risk a blow. Our resolution was clinched by Johann’s news. The King grew peaked, pale, and ill. His health was breaking down under his rigorous confinement. Now a man—be he king or no king—may as well die swiftly and as becomes a gentleman, from bullet or thrust, as rot his life out in a cellar! That thought made prompt action advisable in the interests of the King. From my own point of view, it grew more and more necessary. Strakencz urged on me the need of a speedy marriage. My own inclinations seconded him with such terrible insistence that I feared for my resolution. I do not believe that I should have done the deed I dreamt of. But I might have come to flight, and my flight would have ruined the cause. And—yes, I am no saint (ask my little sister-in-law), and worse still might have happened.

It is perhaps as strange a thing as has ever been in the history of a country. The King’s brother and the King’s personator, in a time of profound outward peace, near a placid, undisturbed country town, under semblance of amity, should wage a desperate war for the person and life of the King. Yet such was the struggle that began now between Zenda and Tarlenheim. When I look back on the time, I seem to myself to have been half mad. Sapt has told me that I suffered no interference and listened to no remonstrances. And if ever a King of Ruritania ruled like a despot, I was, in those days, the man. Look where I would, I saw nothing that made life sweet to me. I took my life in my hand and carried it carelessly as a man dangles an old glove. At first they strove to guard me, to keep me safe, to persuade me not to expose myself. But when they saw how I was set, there grew up among them—whether they knew the truth or not—a feeling that Fate ruled the issue. They felt that I must be left to play my game with Michael my own way.

Late next night I rose from table, where Flavia had sat by me. I conducted her to the door of her apartments. There I kissed her hand, and bade her sleep sound and wake to happy days. Then I changed my clothes and went out. Sapt and Fritz were waiting for me with six men and the horses. Over his saddle Sapt carried a long coil of rope. Both were heavily armed. I had with me a short stout cudgel and a long knife. Making a circuit, we avoided the town. In an hour we found ourselves slowly mounting the hill that led to the Castle of Zenda. The night was dark and very stormy. Gusts of wind and spits of rain caught us as we breasted the incline. The great trees moaned and sighed. When we came to a thick clump, about a quarter of a mile from the Castle, we bade our six friends hide there with the horses. Sapt had a whistle, and they could rejoin us in a few moments if danger came. But, up to now, we had met no one. I hoped that Michael was still off his guard, believing me to be safe in bed. However that might be, we gained the top of the hill without accident. We found ourselves on the edge of the moat where it sweeps under the road, separating the Old Castle from it. A tree stood on the edge of the bank. Sapt, silently and diligently, set to make fast the rope. I stripped off my boots, took a pull at a flask of brandy, loosened the knife in its sheath, and took the cudgel between my teeth. Then I shook hands with my friends, not heeding a last look of entreaty from Fritz, and laid hold of the rope. I was going to have a look at “Jacob’s Ladder.”

I lowered myself gently into the water. The night was wild, but the day had been warm and bright. The water was not cold. I began to swim round the great walls that stood high above me. I could see only three yards ahead. I had good hopes of not being seen, as I moved along close under the wet stone wall. There were lights from the new part of the Castle on the other side. Now and again I heard laughter and happy shouts. I thought I recognized young Rupert Hentzau's ringing voice. I pictured him with a red face from wine. I brought my thoughts back to the task at hand. I rested for a moment. If Johann's description was right, I must be near the window now. I moved very slowly. Out of the darkness ahead, a shape appeared. It was the pipe, curving from the window to the water. About four feet of its surface showed. It was as big round as two men. I was about to go to it when I saw something else. My heart stopped. The nose of a boat stuck out beyond the pipe on the other side. I listened closely. I heard a small movement, like a man changing his position. Who was the man guarding Michael's invention? Was he awake or asleep? I felt if my knife was ready. I moved my legs in the water to stay in place. As I did so, I found the bottom under my feet. The foundations of the Castle went out about fifteen inches, making a small shelf. I stood on it, out of the water from my chest up. Then I bent down and looked through the darkness under the pipe, where it curved and left a space.

There was a man in the boat. A rifle lay by him. I saw the shine of the barrel. Here was the guard! He sat very still. I listened. He breathed heavily, regularly, in a dull way. By heaven, he slept! I kneeled on the shelf and moved forward under the pipe until my face was within two feet of his. I saw he was a big man. It was Max Holf, the brother of Johann. My hand went to my belt, and I pulled out my knife. Of all the acts of my life, I like least to think of this one. I will not ask whether it was the act of a man or a traitor. I said to myself: "It is war, and the King's life is at stake." I raised myself from under the pipe and stood up by the boat, which was tied to the shelf. Holding my breath, I marked the spot and raised my arm. The big man moved. He opened his eyes, wide, wider. He gasped in terror at my face and grabbed at his rifle. I struck home. And I heard a group of voices singing a love song from the opposite bank.

I left him where he lay, a folded mass, and turned to "Jacob's Ladder." My time was short. This man's turn of watching might end soon, and a new guard would come. I leaned over the pipe and examined it. I looked from the end near the water to the highest point where it passed, or seemed to pass, through the wall. There was no break in it, no small opening. I dropped on my knees and tested the under side. My breath went quick and fast. On this lower side, where the pipe should have held close to the wall, there was a small light! That light must come from the cell of the King! I put my shoulder against the pipe and pushed with my strength. The small opening widened a very, very little. I stopped quickly. I had done enough to show that the pipe was not fixed in the wall at the lower side.

Then I heard a voice, a harsh, rough voice:

"Well, sire, if you have had enough of my company, I will leave you to rest. But I must fasten the little ornaments first."

It was Detchard! I caught the English accent at once.

"Do you have anything to ask, sire, before we part?"

The King's voice followed. It was his, though it was faint and hollow. It was different from the happy tones I had heard in the open spaces of the forest.

"Pray my brother," said the King, "to kill me. I am dying by small steps here."

"The duke does not want your death, sire, yet," said Detchard with a mean smile. "When he does, look out for your path to heaven!"

The King answered:

"So be it! And now, if your orders allow it, pray leave me."

"May you dream of paradise!" said the rough man.

The light disappeared. I heard the bolts of the door go into place. And then I heard the sobs of the King. He was alone, as he thought. Who dares laugh at him?

I did not dare to speak to him. The risk was too great that he would cry out in surprise. I dared do nothing that night. My task now was to get myself away in safety and to carry off the body of the dead man. To leave him there would tell too much. I untied the boat and got in. The wind was blowing hard now, and there was little danger of oars being heard. I rowed quickly round to where my friends waited. I had just reached the spot when a loud whistle sounded over the moat behind me.

"Hullo, Max!" I heard shouted.

I called Sapt in a low voice. The rope came down. I tied it round the dead body, and then went up it myself.

"Whistle too," I whispered, "for our men, and pull in the line. No talk now."

They pulled up the body. Just as it reached the road, three men on horseback came quickly round from the front of the Castle. We saw them. But we were on foot, so they did not notice us. But we heard our men coming up with a shout.

"The devil, but it's dark!" cried a ringing voice.

It was young Rupert. A moment later, shots rang out. Our people had met them. I started forward at a run, with Sapt and Fritz following me.

"Strike, strike!" cried Rupert again. A loud groan followed, which told that he himself was not behind-hand.

"I'm done, Rupert!" cried a voice. "They're three to one. Save yourself!"

I ran on, holding my club in my hand. Suddenly a horse came towards me. A man was on it, leaning over his shoulder.

"Are you finished too, Krafstein?" he cried.

There was no answer.

I jumped to the horse's head. It was Rupert Hentzau.

"At last!" I cried.

For we seemed to have him. He had only his sword in his hand. My men were close upon him. Sapt and Fritz were running up. I had gone ahead of them. But if they got close enough to fire, he must die or surrender.

"At last!" I cried.

“It’s the play-actor!” he cried. He hit my stick with his sword. He cut it clean in two. I decided that being careful was better than dying. I ducked my head and ran for my life. I am ashamed to say it. Rupert Hentzau was full of wild energy. He kicked his horse and rode away. I turned to look. I saw him ride at full speed to the edge of the water around the Castle. He jumped in. The shots from our group fell thick around him like rain. One flash of light from the moon would have let us shoot him. But in the darkness, he reached the corner of the Castle and disappeared from our sight.

“Bad luck to him!” Sapt said with a grin.

“It is a sad thing,” I said, “that he is a bad man. Who have we got?”

We had Lauengram and Krafstein. They lay dead. We could hide no longer. We threw them, with Max, into the water around the Castle. We gathered close together and rode off down the hill. In the middle of us went the bodies of three brave gentlemen. So we traveled home. We were heavy at heart for the death of our friends. We felt very worried about the King. We were deeply hurt that young Rupert had won yet another round against us.

For my own part, I was angry and upset that I had killed no man in open fight. I had only stabbed a bad man in his sleep. And I did not like to hear Rupert call me a play-actor.

CHAPTER 15. I Talk with a Tempter

I Talk with a Tempter

Ruritania is not England. If it were, the quarrel between Duke Michael and me could not have gone on, with all its strange events, without more public attention. Duels were common among the upper classes. Private quarrels between great men spread to their friends and servants. After the fight I have just described, such reports began to spread that I felt I must be on my guard. The death of the gentlemen could not be hidden from their families. I made a strict order. It said that duelling had reached a level never seen before (the Chancellor wrote the document for me, and he did it very well). It also said that duelling was forbidden except in the most serious cases. I sent a public and formal apology to Michael. He sent back a respectful and polite reply. Our one point of agreement was this, and it lay under all our differences and forced an unwilling harmony between our actions: neither of us could afford to show our cards. He was a "play-actor" as much as I was. We hated one another, but we worked together to fool public opinion. Sadly, the need for hiding things meant we had to wait. The King might die in his prison, or even be taken off somewhere else. There was nothing we could do. For a little while I had to keep a truce. My only comfort was that Flavia most warmly approved of my order against duelling. When I said I was glad to have won her favour, she asked me, if her favour meant anything to me, to prohibit the practice altogether.

"Wait till we are married," said I, smiling.

The strangest result of the truce, and of the secrecy that required it, was this: the town of Zenda became in the daytime a sort of neutral zone, where both sides could safely go. I would not have trusted its protection much by night. One day I was riding down with Flavia and Sapt. I met an acquaintance. It had a funny side, but it was also embarrassing. As I rode along, I met a dignified-looking person driving in a carriage with two horses. He stopped his horses, got out, and came up to me, bowing low. I recognized the Head of the Strelsau Police.

"Your Majesty's order about duelling is receiving our best attention," he assured me.

If his best attention meant he was here in Zenda, I decided at once to do without it.

"Is that what brings you to Zenda, Prefect?" I asked.

"Why no, sire; I am here because I wished to oblige the British Ambassador."

"What's the British Ambassador doing _dans cette galère_?" said I, carelessly.

"A young countryman of his, sire--a man of some position--is missing. His friends have not heard from him for two months. There is reason to believe he was last seen in Zenda."

Flavia was paying little attention. I dared not look at Sapt.

"What reason?"

"A friend of his in Paris--a certain M. Featherly--has given us information. It makes it possible that he came here. The railway officials remember his name on some luggage."

"What was his name?"

"Rassendyll, sire," he answered. I saw that the name meant nothing to him. But he glanced at Flavia and lowered his voice as he went on: "It is thought that he may have followed a lady here. Has your Majesty heard of a certain Madame de Mauban?"

"Why, yes," said I. My eye moved towards the Castle without my wanting it to.

"She arrived in Ruritania about the same time as this Rassendyll."

I caught the Prefect's glance. He was looking at me with a question written large on his face.

"Sapt," said I, "I must speak a word to the Prefect. Will you ride on a few paces with the princess?" And I added to the Prefect: "Come, sir, what do you mean?"

He came close to me, and I bent down in the saddle.

"If he were in love with the lady?" he whispered. "Nothing has been heard of him for two months." This time it was the Prefect's eye that moved towards the Castle.

"Yes, the lady is there," I said quietly. "But I don't suppose Mr. Rassendyll--is that the name?--is."

"The duke," he whispered, "does not like rivals, sire."

"You're right there," said I, with all sincerity. "But surely you are suggesting a very serious charge?"

He spread his hands out in apology. I whispered in his ear:

"This is a serious matter. Go back to Strelsau--"

"But, sire, if I have a clue here?"

"Go back to Strelsau," I repeated. "Tell the Ambassador that you have a clue, but that you must be left alone for a week or two. Meanwhile, I'll take charge of looking into the matter."

"The Ambassador is very pressing, sir."

"You must quiet him. Come, sir; you see that if your suspicions are correct, it is an affair in which we must move with care. We can have no scandal. Mind you return tonight."

He promised to obey me. I rode on to rejoin my companions, a little easier in my mind. Enquiries about me must be stopped at all costs for a week or two. This clever official had come surprisingly near the truth. His impression might be useful some day. But if he acted on it now, it might mean the worse for the King. I cursed George Featherly heartily for not holding his tongue.

"Well," asked Flavia, "have you finished your business?"

"Most satisfactorily," said I. "Come, shall we turn round? We are almost stepping on my brother's territory."

We were, in fact, at the far end of the town, just where the hills begin to rise towards the Castle. We looked up, admiring the massive beauty of the old walls. We saw a procession winding slowly down the hill. On it came.

"Let us go back," said Sapt.

"I should like to stay," said Flavia. I pulled my horse in beside hers.

We could see the approaching party now. First came two servants on horseback in black uniforms, with only a silver badge to break the black. Then came a car pulled by four horses. On it, under a heavy cloth, lay a coffin. Behind it rode a man in plain black clothes, carrying his hat in his hand. Sapt took off his hat, and we stood waiting. Flavia stayed by me and put her hand on my arm.

"It is one of the gentlemen killed in the quarrel, I expect," she said.

I signalled to a groom.

"Ride and ask whom they escort," I ordered.

He rode up to the servants, and I saw him pass on to the gentleman who rode behind.

"It's Rupert of Hentzau," whispered Sapt.

Rupert it was, and right after that, he waved to the group to stop. Then Rupert rode quickly up to me. He wore a tight-buttoned coat and trousers. He looked sad, and he bowed with deep respect. But suddenly he smiled, and I smiled too. Old Sapt’s hand was inside his left chest pocket. Rupert and I both guessed what was in that hand inside the pocket.

“Your Majesty asks who we are taking,” said Rupert. “It is my dear friend, Albert of Lauengram.”

“Sir,” said I, “no one feels more sorry about this bad business than I do. My order, which I mean to have followed, is proof of it.”

“Poor man!” said Flavia softly, and I saw Rupert’s eyes flash at her. Then I grew red. If I had my way, Rupert Hentzau should not have dirtied her with even a glance. But he did it, and he dared to let his liking show in his look.

“Your Majesty’s words are kind,” he said. “I am sad for my friend. But, sire, others must soon lie as he lies now.”

“It is a thing we all do well to remember, my lord,” I answered.

“Even kings, sire,” said Rupert, in a teaching tone. And old Sapt swore softly beside me.

“It is true,” said I. “How is my brother, my lord?”

“He is better, sire.”

“I am glad.”

“He hopes to leave soon for Strelsau, when his health is safe.”

“He is only getting better, then?”

“There are still one or two small troubles,” answered the rude fellow, in the mildest tone in the world.

“Express my strong hope,” said Flavia, “that they may soon stop troubling him.”

“Your Royal Highness’s wish is, humbly, my own,” said Rupert, with a bold look that made Flavia’s cheek turn red.

I bowed. Rupert bowed lower, backed his horse, and signed to his group to go on. On a sudden urge, I rode after him. He turned quickly, fearing that even with the dead there and before a lady’s eyes, I meant him harm.

“You fought as a brave man the other night,” I said. “Come, you are young, sir. If you will hand your prisoner to me alive, you shall come to no harm.”

He looked at me with a mocking smile. But suddenly he rode nearer to me.

“I have no weapon,” he said. “And our old Sapt there could shoot me in a minute.”

“I am not afraid,” said I.

“No, curse you!” he answered. “Look here, I once made you a proposal from the duke.”

“I’ll hear nothing from Black Michael,” said I.

“Then hear one from me.” He lowered his voice to a whisper. “Attack the Castle boldly. Let Sapt and Tarlenheim lead.”

“Go on,” said I.

“Set the time with me.”

“I have such trust in you, my lord!”

“Tut! I’m talking business now. Sapt there and Fritz will fall. Black Michael will fall--”

“What!”

“--Black Michael will fall, like the dog he is. The prisoner, as you call him, will go by ‘Jacob’s Ladder’--ah, you know that!--to hell! Two men will be left--I, Rupert Hentzau, and you, the King of Ruritania.”

He paused, and then, in a voice that shook with eagerness, he added:

“Isn’t that a hand to play?--a throne and your princess! And for me, say a good income and your Majesty’s thanks.”

“Surely,” I exclaimed, “while you’re above ground, hell wants its master!”

“Well, think it over,” he said. “And, look you, it would take more than a scruple or two to keep me from that girl,” and his evil eye flashed again at her I loved.

“Get out of my reach!” said I. Yet in a moment I began to laugh at the very boldness of it.

“Would you turn against your master?” I asked.

He swore at Michael for being what the child of a legal, though morganatic, union should not be called. Then he said to me in an almost secret and seemingly friendly tone:

“He gets in my way, you know. He’s a jealous brute! Faith, I nearly put a knife into him last night. He came most cursedly _mal àpropos_!”

My temper was well under control now. I was learning something.

“A lady?” I asked carelessly.

“Ay, and a beauty,” he nodded. “But you’ve seen her.”

“Ah! was it at a tea-party, when some of your friends got on the wrong side of the table?”

“What can you expect of fools like Detchard and De Gautet? I wish I’d been there.”

“And the duke interferes?”

“Well,” said Rupert slowly, “that’s hardly a fair way of putting it, perhaps. I want to interfere.”

“And she prefers the duke?”

“Ay, the silly creature! Ah, well, you think about my plan,” and, with a bow, he pricked his horse and rode quickly after the body of his friend.

I went back to Flavia and Sapt, thinking about how strange the man was. I have known plenty of bad men, but Rupert Hentzau remains unique in my experience. And if there is another anywhere, let him be caught and hanged at once. So say I!

“He’s very handsome, isn’t he?” said Flavia.

Well, of course, she didn’t know him as I did. Yet I was upset, for I thought his bold looks would have made her angry. But my dear Flavia was a woman, and so--she was not upset. On the contrary, she thought young Rupert very handsome--as, beyond question, the ruffian was.

“And how sad he looked at his friend’s death!” said she.

“He’ll have better reason to be sad at his own,” observed Sapt, with a grim smile.

As for me, I grew sulky. It was perhaps unreasonable, for what better business had I to look at her with love than even Rupert’s lustful eyes? And sulky I remained till, as evening fell and we rode up to Tarlenheim, Sapt having fallen behind in case anyone should be following us, Flavia, riding close beside me, said softly, with a little half-ashamed laugh:

“Unless you smile, Rudolf, I cry. Why are you angry?”

“It was something that fellow said to me,” said I, but I was smiling as we reached the door and got off our horses.

There a servant handed me a note. It was unaddressed.

“Is it for me?” I asked.

“Yes, sire; a boy brought it.”

I tore it open:

Johann carries this for me. I warned you once. In the name of God, and if you are a man, rescue me from this den of murderers!--A. de M.

I handed it to Sapt. But all that the tough old soul said in reply to this pitiful appeal was:

“Whose fault brought her there?”

Nevertheless, not being faultless myself, I took leave to pity Antoinette de Mauban.

CHAPTER 16. A Desperate Plan

A Desperate Plan

I had ridden openly in Zenda, and I had talked there with Rupert Hentzau. So of course all pretence of illness was at an end. I saw the effect on the fort at Zenda. The men there stopped being seen outside. Any of my men who went near the Castle reported that the greatest watchfulness won there. Madame de Mauban's appeal touched me, but I seemed as unable to help her as I had proved unable to help the King. Michael defied me. He too had been seen outside the walls. He showed less care for appearances than he had shown before. But he did not take the trouble to send any excuse for his failure to wait on the King. Time ran on with no action, when every moment was pressing. I faced a new danger, which the stir about my disappearance brought on me. Also, great murmurs had arisen in Strelsau at my continued absence from the city. They would have been greater, but for the knowledge that Flavia was with me. For this reason I let her stay, though I hated to have her where danger was. Every day of our present sweet talk strained my endurance almost to breaking. As a final blow, nothing would satisfy my advisers, Strakencz and the Chancellor. The Chancellor came out from Strelsau to make an urgent appeal to me. They said I must appoint a day for the public solemnization of my betrothal. In Ruritania this ceremony is almost as binding and great a thing as the marriage itself. And this I was forced to do, with Flavia sitting by me. I set a date two weeks ahead, and I appointed the Cathedral in Strelsau as the place. This formal act was published far and wide. It caused great joy throughout the kingdom, and was the talk of all tongues. So I reckoned there were but two men who chafed at it. I mean Black Michael and myself. And but one who did not know of it. That one was the man whose name I bore, the King of Ruritania.

In truth, I heard something of the way the news was received in the Castle. After three days, the man Johann again found means to visit us. He was greedy for more money, though fearful for his life. He had been waiting on the duke when the tidings came. Black Michael's face had grown blacker still, and he had sworn savagely. Nor was he better pleased when young Rupert took oath that I meant to do as I said. Rupert turned to Madame de Mauban and wished her joy on a rival gone. Michael's hand stole towards his sword, said Johann. But Rupert did not care a bit. He rallied the duke on having made a better King than had reigned for years past in Ruritania. "And," said he, with a meaning bow to his exasperated master, "the devil sends the princess a finer man than heaven had marked out for her, by my soul, it does!" Then Michael harshly bade him hold his tongue, and leave them. But Rupert must needs first kiss madame's hand. He did it as though he loved her, while Michael glared at him.

This was the lighter side of the fellow's news. But more serious came behind. It was plain that if time pressed at Tarlenheim, it pressed none the less fiercely at Zenda. For the King was very sick. Johann had seen him, and he was wasted and hardly able to move. "There could be no thought of taking another for him now." They were so alarmed that they had sent for a physician from Strelsau. The physician was introduced into the King's cell. He came forth pale and trembling, and urgently prayed the duke to let him go back and meddle no more in the affair. But the duke would not, and held him there a prisoner. He told him his life was safe if the King lived while the duke desired and died when the duke desired, not otherwise. And, persuaded by the physician, they had allowed Madame de Mauban to visit the King. She gave him such attendance as his state needed, and as only a woman can give. Yet his life hung in the balance. And I was still strong and whole and free. Wherefore great gloom reigned at Zenda. And save when they quarrelled, to which they were very prone, they hardly spoke. But the deeper the depression of the rest, young Rupert went about Satan's work with a smile in his eye and a song on his lip. And he laughed "fit to burst," said Johann, because the duke always set Detchard to guard the King when Madame de Mauban was in the cell. This precaution was, indeed, not unwise in my careful brother. Thus Johann told his tale and seized his crowns. Yet he begged us to allow him to stay with us in Tarlenheim, and not venture his head again in the lion's den. But we had need of him there. Although I refused to force him, I prevailed on him by increased rewards to go back. He was to carry tidings to Madame de Mauban that I was working for her. And, if she could, she should speak one word of comfort to the King. For while suspense is bad for the sick, yet despair is worse still. And it might be that the King lay dying of mere hopelessness, for I could learn of no definite disease that afflicted him.

"And how do they guard the King now?" I asked, remembering that two of the Six were dead, and Max Holf also.

"Detchard and Bersonin watch by night, Rupert Hentzau and De Gautet by day, sir," he answered.

"Only two at a time?"

"Ay, sir; but the others rest in a room just above, and are within sound of a cry or a whistle."

"A room just above? I didn't know of that. Is there any communication between it and the room where they watch?"

"No, sir. You must go down a few stairs and through the door by the drawbridge, and so to where the King is lodged."

"And that door is locked?"

"Only the four lords have keys, sir."

I drew nearer to him.

"And have they keys of the grating?" I asked in a low whisper.

"I think, sir, only Detchard and Rupert."

"Where does the duke lodge?"

"In the chateau, on the first floor. His apartments are on the right as you go towards the drawbridge."

"And Madame de Mauban?"

"Just opposite, on the left. But her door is locked after she has entered."

"To keep her in?"

"Doubtless, sir."

"Perhaps for another reason?"

"It is possible."

"And the duke, I suppose, has the key?"

"Yes. And the drawbridge is drawn back at night, and of that, too, the duke holds the key, so that it cannot be run across the moat without application to him."

"And where do you sleep?"

"In the entrance hall of the chateau, with five servants."

"Armed?"

“They have pikes, sir, but no guns. The duke will not trust them with guns.”

Then at last I took the matter bravely in my hands. I had failed once at “Jacob’s Ladder.” I should fail again there. I must make the attack from the other side.

“I have promised you twenty thousand crowns,” said I. “You shall have fifty thousand if you will do what I ask of you tomorrow night. But, first, do those servants know who your prisoner is?”

“No, sir. They believe him to be some private enemy of the duke’s.”

“And they would not doubt that I am the King?”

“How should they?” he asked.

“Look to this, then. Tomorrow, at two in the morning exactly, open the front door of the chateau. Do not fail by an instant.”

“Shall you be there, sir?”

“Ask no questions. Do what I tell you. Say the hall is close, or what you will. That is all I ask of you.”

“And may I escape by the door, sir, when I have opened it?”

“Yes, as quick as your legs will carry you. One thing more. Carry this note to madame--oh, it is in French, you cannot read it--and charge her, for the sake of all our lives, not to fail in what it orders.”

The man was shaking but I had to trust to what he had of courage and to what he had of honesty. I dared not wait, for I feared that the King would die.

When the fellow was gone, I called Sapt and Fritz to me, and explained the plan that I had formed. Sapt shook his head over it.

“Why cannot you wait?” he asked.

“The King may die.”

“Michael will be forced to act before that.”

“Then,” said I, “the King may live.”

“Well, and if he does?”

“For two weeks?” I asked simply.

And Sapt bit his moustache.

Suddenly Fritz von Tarlenheim laid his hand on my shoulder.

“Let us go and make the attempt,” said he.

“I mean you to go--do not be afraid,” said I.

“Ay, but do you stay here, and take care of the princess.”

A light came into old Sapt’s eye.

“We should have Michael one way or the other then,” he laughed quietly; “whereas if you go and are killed with the King, what will become of those of us who are left?”

“They will serve Queen Flavia,” said I, “and I would to God I could be one of them.”

A pause followed. Old Sapt broke it by saying with sadness, yet with a humor he did not mean that set Fritz and me laughing:

“Why did old Rudolf the Third not marry your--great-grandmother, was it?”

“Come,” said I, “it is the King we are thinking about.”

“It is true,” said Fritz.

“Moreover,” I went on, “I have been a false person for the profit of another, but I will not be one for my own. And if the King is not alive and on his throne before the day of betrothal comes, I will tell the truth, come what may.”

“You shall go, lad,” said Sapt.

Here is the plan I had made. A strong party under Sapt’s command was to move quietly up to the door of the chateau. If discovered too early, they were to kill anyone who found them--with their swords, for I wanted no noise of firing. If all went well, they would be at the door when Johann opened it. They were to rush in and take control of the servants if their mere presence and the use of the King’s name were not enough. At the same moment--and on this the plan depended--a woman’s cry was to ring out loud and high from Antoinette de Mauban’s room. Again and again she was to cry: “Help, help! Michael, help!” and then to say the name of young Rupert Hentzau. Then, as we hoped, Michael, in anger, would rush out of his rooms opposite, and fall alive into the hands of Sapt. Still the cries would go on. And my men would let down the drawbridge. And it would be strange if Rupert, hearing his name used like this, did not come down from where he slept and try to cross. De Gautet might or might not come with him: that must be left to chance.

And when Rupert set his foot on the drawbridge? There was my part. For I was minded for another swim in the moat. And, so that I should not grow weary, I had resolved to take with me a small wooden ladder, on which I could rest my arms in the water--and my feet when I left it. I would set it against the wall just by the bridge. And when the bridge was across, I would creep on to it without being seen--and then if Rupert or De Gautet crossed in safety, it would be my bad luck, not my fault. They dead, two men only would remain. And for them we must trust to the confusion we had created and to a sudden rush. We should have the keys of the door that led to the all-important rooms. Perhaps they would rush out. If they stood by their orders, then the King’s life hung on the speed with which we could force the outer door. And I thanked God that not Rupert Hentzau watched, but Detchard. For though Detchard was a cool man, relentless, and no coward, he had neither the dash nor the recklessness of Rupert. Moreover, he, if any one of them, really loved Black Michael. And it might be that he would leave Bersonin to guard the King, and rush across the bridge to take part in the fight on the other side.

So I planned--desperately. And, so that our enemy might be better lulled to security, I gave orders that our residence should be brightly lighted from top to bottom, as though we were engaged in revelry. And so it should be kept all night, with music playing and people moving to and fro. Strakencz would be there, and he was to hide our departure, if he could, from Flavia. And if we came not again by the morning, he was to march, openly and in force to the Castle, and demand the person of the King. If Black Michael were not there, as I did not think he would be, the Marshal would take Flavia with him, as swiftly as he could, to Strelsau. And there he would proclaim Black Michael’s treachery and the probable death of the King, and gather all that was honest and true round the banner of the princess. And, to say truth, this was what I thought most likely to happen. For I had great doubts whether either the King or Black Michael or I had more than a day to live. Well, if Black Michael died, and if I, the play-actor, killed Rupert Hentzau with my own hand, and then died myself, it might be that Fate would deal as lightly with Ruritania as could be hoped, even though she demanded the life of the King. And to her dealing thus with me, I was in no temper to make objection.

It was late when we rose from the meeting. I went to the princess’s rooms. She was quiet and thoughtful that evening. Yet when I left her, she threw her arms around me. For a moment she became shy and bright as she slipped a ring on my finger. I was wearing the King’s ring. But I also had on my little finger a plain gold band. It had the saying of our family cut into it: “Nil Quae Feci.” I took it off and put it on her. I signed to her to let me go. She understood. She stood back and watched me with clouded eyes.

“Wear that ring, even though you wear another when you are queen,” I said.

“Whatever else I wear, I will wear this until I die and after,” she said. She kissed the ring.

CHAPTER 17. Young Rupert’s Midnight Diversions

The night came fine and clear. I had prayed for bad weather. That kind of weather had helped me on my earlier trip in the water around the castle. But Fortune was against me this time. Still, I thought I could avoid being seen from the windows of the chateau. Those windows looked out on the place where I would work. I would keep close under the wall and in the shadow. If they searched the water, my plan must fail. But I did not think they would. They had made “Jacob’s Ladder” safe against attack. Johann himself had helped to fix it closely to the stone on the under side. So now it could not be moved from below any more than from above. Only an attack with explosives or a long beating with picks could move it. The noise of either of these would make them impossible. So what harm could a man do in the water? I trusted that Black Michael asked himself this question. I trusted that he answered with confidence, “None.” Even if Johann meant treachery, he did not know my plan. He would surely expect to see me at the head of my friends. He would expect me in front of the main entrance to the chateau. There, I said to Sapt, was the real danger. “And there,” I added, “you shall be. Doesn’t that please you?”

But it did not please him. He would have liked very much to come with me. But I completely refused to take him. One man might escape notice. Two would more than double the risk. He hinted once more that my life was too valuable. I knew the secret thought he held to. I told him sternly to be silent. I assured him that I would not live through the night unless the King lived through it too.

At twelve o’clock, Sapt’s group left the chateau of Tarlenheim and turned off to the right. They rode by quiet roads and avoided the town of Zenda. If all went well, they would be in front of the Castle at about a quarter to two. They were to leave their horses half a mile away. Then they were to move quietly up to the entrance. They were to be ready for the door to open. If the door was not opened by two, they were to send Fritz von Tarlenheim round to the other side of the Castle. I would meet him there if I were alive. We would decide whether to attack the Castle or not. If I were not there, they were to return with all speed to Tarlenheim. They were to wake the Marshal and march in force to Zenda. For if I was not there, I should be dead. And I knew that the King would not be alive five minutes after I stopped breathing. I must now leave Sapt and his friends. I must tell how I myself went on this important night. I went out on the good horse that had carried me back from the hunting lodge to Strelsau on the night of the coronation. I carried a revolver in the saddle and my sword. I was covered with a large cloak. Under this I wore a warm, tight woollen jersey, a pair of knickerbockers, thick stockings, and light canvas shoes. I had rubbed myself all over with oil. I carried a large flask of whisky. The night was warm, but I might be in the water for a long while. It was necessary to take every care against cold. Cold weakens a man’s courage if he has to die. It also weakens his energy if others have to die. And finally, it gives him rheumatism, if it be God’s will that he lives. Also I tied a length of thin but strong cord around my body. I did not forget my ladder. I started after Sapt and took a shorter route. I went around the town to the left. I found myself on the edge of the forest at about half-past twelve. I tied my horse in a thick group of trees. I left the revolver in its pocket in the saddle. It would be no use to me. With the ladder in hand, I made my way to the edge of the water around the castle. Here I unwound my rope from around my waist. I tied it securely round the trunk of a tree on the bank. I let myself down. The Castle clock struck a quarter to one as I felt the water under me. I began to swim round the keep. I pushed the ladder before me and stayed close to the Castle wall. So moving through the water, I came to my old friend, “Jacob’s Ladder.” I felt the edge of the stone under me. I crouched down in the shadow of the great pipe. I tried to move it, but it was quite fixed. And I waited. I remember that my main feeling was not worry for the King. It was not longing for Flavia. It was a strong desire to smoke. And of course, I could not satisfy this craving.

The drawbridge was still in its place. I saw its light, thin frame above me, about ten yards to my right. I crouched with my back against the wall of the King’s cell. I made out a window two yards to my side of it and nearly on the same level. That, if Johann spoke true, must belong to the duke’s rooms. And on the other side, in about the same position, must be Madame de Mauban’s window. Women are careless, forgetful creatures. I prayed that she might not forget that she was to be the victim of a cruel attempt at two o’clock exactly. I was rather amused at the part I had given to my young friend Rupert Hentzau. But I owed him a blow. Even as I sat, my shoulder ached where he had struck at me. He did it with a boldness that seemed half to hide his treachery. He did it in the sight of all my friends, on the terrace at Tarlenheim.

Suddenly the duke’s window grew bright. The shutters were not closed. I could partly see inside as I carefully raised myself till I stood on tiptoe. In this position, I could see a yard or more inside the window. The light did not reach me. The window was thrown open and someone looked out. I saw Antoinette de Mauban’s graceful figure. Her face was in shadow, but the fine outline of her head was shown against the light behind. I longed to cry softly, “Remember!” but I dared not. And it was lucky, for a moment later a man came up and stood by her. He tried to put his arm round her waist. But with a quick motion she sprang away and leant against the shutter. Her side profile was towards me. I made out who the newcomer was: it was young Rupert. A low laugh from him made me sure, as he leant forward, stretching out his hand towards her.

“Gently, gently!” I murmured. “You’re too soon, my boy!”

His head was close to hers. I suppose he whispered to her. I saw her point to the water around the castle. I heard her say, in slow and clear tones:

“I would rather throw myself out of this window!”

He came close to the window and looked out.

“It looks cold,” he said. “Come, Antoinette, are you serious?”

She gave no answer that I could hear. He hit his hand on the window sill in a bad-tempered way. Then he went on in the voice of a spoiled child:

“Hang Black Michael! Is the princess not enough for him? Is he to have everything? What do you see in Black Michael?”

“If I told him what you say--” she began.

“Well, tell him,” said Rupert in an uncaring way. He caught her off her guard. He jumped forward and kissed her. He laughed and cried, “There is something to tell him!”

If I had kept my revolver with me, I would have felt a very strong wish to use it. Since I did not have it, I only added this new wrong to his account.

“Though, truly,” said Rupert, “he cares very little. He is crazy about the princess, you know. He talks of nothing but cutting the play actor’s throat.”

Did he indeed?

“And if I do it for him, what do you think he has promised me?”

The poor woman raised her hands above her head. She did this in prayer or in hopeless pain.

“But I hate waiting,” said Rupert. I saw that he was about to put his hand on her again. Then came the noise of a door opening in the room, and a harsh voice cried:

“What are you doing here, sir?”

Rupert turned his back to the window. He bowed low and said in his loud, happy voice: “Apologizing for your absence, sir. Could I leave the lady alone?”

The man who came in must be Black Michael. I saw him at once as he came toward the window. He caught young Rupert by the arm.

“The water around the castle would hold more than the King!” he said. He made a meaningful movement.

“Does your Highness threaten me?” asked Rupert.

“A threat is more warning than most men get from me.”

“Yet,” said Rupert, “Rudolf Rassendyll has been threatened many times, and yet he lives!”

“Am I at fault because my servants make mistakes?” asked Michael in a mocking way.

“Your Highness has run no risk of making mistakes!” said Rupert with a mocking smile.

He was telling the duke that he avoided danger as plainly as I have ever heard a man told. Black Michael had self-control. I dare say he frowned. It was a great pity to me that I could not see their faces better. But his voice was even and calm as he answered:

“Enough, enough! We must not fight, Rupert. Are Detchard and Bersonin at their posts?”

“They are, sir.”

“I need you no more.”

“No, I am not tired out,” said Rupert.

“Please, sir, leave us,” said Michael, with less patience. “In ten minutes the bridge will be pulled back. I do not think you want to swim to your bed.”

Rupert’s figure disappeared. I heard the door open and shut again. Michael and Antoinette de Mauban were left together. To my annoyance, the duke put his hand on the window and closed it. He stood talking to Antoinette for a moment or two. She shook her head, and he turned away with little patience. She left the window. The door sounded again, and Black Michael closed the shutters.

“De Gautet, De Gautet, man!” came a voice from the bridge. “Unless you want a bath before your bed, come along!”

It was Rupert’s voice, coming from the end of the bridge. A moment later he and De Gautet stepped out on the bridge. Rupert’s arm was through De Gautet’s. In the middle of the bridge he held his companion back and leaned over. I dropped behind the shelter of “Jacob’s Ladder.”

Then Master Rupert had a little fun. He took from De Gautet a bottle that he carried and put it to his lips.

“Hardly a drop!” he cried in displeasure, and threw it into the water around the castle.

It fell, as I judged from the sound and the circles on the water, within a yard of the pipe. Rupert took out his revolver and began to shoot at it. The first two shots missed the bottle but hit the pipe. The third broke the bottle. I hoped the young rough man would be satisfied. But he emptied the other barrels at the pipe. One shot skimmed over the pipe and whistled through my hair as I crouched on the other side.

“Watch the bridge!” a voice cried, to my relief.

Rupert and De Gautet cried, “A moment!” and ran across. The bridge was pulled back, and all became still. The clock struck a quarter past one. I rose, stretched myself, and yawned.

I think about ten minutes had passed when I heard a slight noise to my right. I looked over the pipe and saw a dark figure standing in the gateway that led to the bridge. It was a man. By the uncaring, easy way he stood, I guessed it was Rupert again. He held a sword in his hand and stood motionless for a minute or two. Wild thoughts ran through me. What mischief was the young evil man planning now? Then he laughed low to himself. Then he turned his face to the wall, took a step in my direction, and, to my surprise, began to climb down the wall. In an instant I saw that there must be steps in the wall. It was clear. They were cut into or fixed to the wall, about eighteen inches apart. Rupert set his foot on the lower one. Then he placed his sword between his teeth, turned round, and let himself into the water without a sound. If it had been a matter of my life only, I would have swum to meet him. I would have dearly loved to fight it out with him then and there, with steel, on a fine night, and no one to come between us. But there was the King! I held myself back, but I could not control my quick breathing. I watched him with the greatest eagerness.

He swam slowly and quietly across. There were more steps up on the other side, and he climbed them. When he set foot in the gateway, standing on the pulled-back bridge, he felt in his pocket and took something out. I heard him unlock the door. I could hear no noise of it closing behind him. He vanished from my sight.

I left my ladder. I saw I did not need it now. I swam to the side of the bridge and climbed halfway up the steps. There I hung with my sword in my hand, listening eagerly. The duke’s room had shutters and was dark. There was a light in the window on the opposite side of the bridge. Not a sound broke the silence until half past one rang from the great clock in the tower of the chateau.

There were other plans than mine at work in the Castle that night.

CHAPTER 18. The Forcing of the Trap

The Forcing of the Trap

The position where I stood did not seem very good for thinking. Yet for the next moment or two I thought deeply. I had, I told myself, won one point. Whatever Rupert Hentzau’s task was, and whatever evil he was doing, I had won one point. He was on the other side of the water from the King. It would not be my fault if he ever set foot on the same side again. I had three left to deal with: two on guard and De Gautet in his bed. Ah, if I had the keys! I would have risked everything and attacked Detchard and Bersonin before their friends could join them. But I had no power. I must wait until my friends came and led someone to cross the bridge--someone with the keys. And I waited, as it seemed, for half an hour, really for about five minutes, before the next act in the fast drama began.

All was still on the other side. The duke’s room stayed hidden behind its shutters. The light burnt steadily in Madame de Mauban’s window. Then I heard the faintest, faintest sound. It came from behind the door that led to the drawbridge on the other side of the water. It just reached my ear, yet I could not be mistaken about what it was. It was made by a key being turned very carefully and slowly. Who was turning it? And of what room was it the key? There jumped before my eyes the picture of young Rupert, with the key in one hand, his sword in the other, and an evil smile on his face. But I did not know what door it was, nor which of his favourite pursuits young Rupert was spending the hours of that night on.

I was soon to be enlightened, for the next moment--before my friends could be near the chateau door--before Johann the keeper would have thought to make himself brave for his task--there was a sudden crash from the room with the lighted window. It sounded as though someone had thrown down a lamp. The window went dark and black. At the same instant a cry rang out, shrill in the night: “Help, help! Michael, help!” and was followed by a shriek of utter terror.

I was tingling in every nerve. I stood on the topmost step, clinging to the threshold of the gate with my right hand and holding my sword in my left. Suddenly I saw that the gateway was broader than the bridge. There was a dark corner on the opposite side where a man could stand. I darted across and stood there. Placed like this, I commanded the path. No man could pass between the chateau and the old Castle until he had tried conclusions with me.

There was another shriek. Then a door was flung open and clanged against the wall, and I heard the handle of a door savagely twisted.

“Open the door! In God’s name, what’s the matter?” cried a voice--the voice of Black Michael himself.

He was answered by the very words I had written in my letter.

“Help, Michael--Hentzau!”

A fierce oath rang out from the duke, and with a loud thud he threw himself against the door. At the same moment I heard a window above my head open, and a voice cried: “What’s the matter?” and I heard a man’s hasty footsteps. I grasped my sword. If De Gautet came my way, the Six would be less by one more.

Then I heard the clash of crossed swords and a tramp of feet and--I cannot tell the thing as quickly as it happened, for all seemed to come at once. There was an angry cry from madame’s room, the cry of a wounded man. The window was flung open. Young Rupert stood there sword in hand. He turned his back, and I saw his body go forward to the lunge.

“Ah, Johann, there’s one for you! Come on, Michael!”

Johann was there, then--come to the rescue of the duke! How would he open the door for me? For I feared that Rupert had slain him.

“Help!” cried the duke’s voice, faint and husky.

I heard a step on the stairs above me. And I heard a stir down to my left, in the direction of the King’s cell. But, before anything happened on my side of the water, I saw five or six men round young Rupert in the embrasure of madame’s window. Three or four times he lunged with incomparable dash and dexterity. For an instant they fell back, leaving a ring round him. He leapt on the parapet of the window, laughing as he leapt, and waving his sword in his hand. He was drunk with blood, and he laughed again wildly as he flung himself headlong into the water.

What became of him then? I did not see. For as he leapt, De Gautet’s lean face looked out through the door by me. Without a second’s hesitation, I struck at him with all the strength God had given me. He fell dead in the doorway without a word or a groan. I dropped on my knees by him. Where were the keys? I found myself muttering: “The keys, man, the keys?” as though he had been yet alive and could listen. When I could not find them, I--God forgive me!--I believe I struck a dead man’s face.

At last I had them. There were but three. Seizing the largest, I felt the lock of the door that led to the cell. I fitted in the key. It was right. The lock turned. I drew the door close behind me and locked it as noiselessly as I could, putting the key in my pocket.

I found myself at the top of a flight of steep stone stairs. An oil lamp burnt dimly in the bracket. I took it down and held it in my hand. And I stood and listened.

“What in the devil can it be?” I heard a voice say.

It came from behind a door that faced me at the bottom of the stairs.

And another answered:

“Shall we kill him?”

I strained to hear the answer, and could have sobbed with relief when Detchard’s voice came grating and cold:

“Wait a bit. There’ll be trouble if we strike too soon.”

There was a moment’s silence. Then I heard the bolt of the door cautiously drawn back. Instantly I put out the light I held, replacing the lamp in the bracket.

“It’s dark--the lamp’s out. Have you a light?” said the other voice--Bersonin’s.

They had a light, I was sure. But they should not use it. The danger had come now. I ran down the steps and threw myself against the door. Bersonin had unlocked it, and it opened before me. The Belgian stood there with a sword in his hand. Detchard was sitting on a couch at the side of the room. Bersonin stepped back in surprise when he saw me. Detchard jumped to his sword. I rushed at the Belgian like a wild man. He gave way before me, and I pushed him up against the wall. He was not good with a sword, though he fought hard. In a moment he lay on the floor before me. I turned. Detchard was not there. He had obeyed his orders. He had not risked a fight with me. Instead, he had run straight to the door of the King's room. He opened it and shut it hard behind him. Even now he was at work inside.

And he would surely have killed the King, and perhaps me too, if not for one loyal man who gave his life for the King. For when I forced the door, this is what I saw: the King stood in the corner of the room. He was broken by his illness and could do nothing. His tied hands moved up and down with no purpose. He was laughing in a terrible way, half out of his mind. Detchard and the doctor were together in the middle of the room. The doctor had thrown himself on the killer and held his hands to his sides for a moment. Then Detchard pulled himself free from the weak hold. As I entered, he drove his sword through the poor man. Then he turned on me and cried:

"At last!"

We were sword to sword. By good luck, neither he nor Bersonin had been wearing their guns. I found them later, loaded and ready, on the shelf above the fireplace in the outer room. It was close by the door, ready to their hands. But my sudden rush in had cut them off from it. Yes, we were man to man. And we began to fight, without a word, seriously, and hard. Yet I remember little of it. I only remember that the man was as good as me with the sword—no, better, for he knew more tricks than I did. And he pushed me back against the bars that guarded the entrance to "Jacob's Ladder." And I saw a smile on his face, and he wounded me in the left arm.

I take no glory from that fight. I believe the man would have beaten me and killed me, and then done his bloody work. He was the most skilled swordsman I have ever met. But even as he pressed me hard, the half-mad, thin, pale creature in the corner jumped high with wild laughter, crying:

"It's cousin Rudolf! Cousin Rudolf! I'll help you, cousin Rudolf!"

And he caught up a chair in his hands. He could only just lift it from the ground and held it with no strength before him. He came towards us. Hope came to me. "Come on!" I cried. "Come on! Push it against his legs."

Detchard answered with a fierce thrust. He almost had me.

"Come on! Come on, man!" I cried. "Come and share the fun!"

And the King laughed with joy, and came on, pushing his chair before him.

With a curse Detchard jumped back. Before I knew what he was doing, he had turned his sword against the King. He made one fierce cut at the King, and the King, with a pitiful cry, fell where he stood. The heavy rough man turned to face me again. But his own hand had prepared his end. For as he turned, he stepped in the pool of blood that flowed from the dead doctor. He slipped. He fell. Like an arrow I was upon him. I caught him by the throat. Before he could recover himself, I drove my point through his neck. With a choked curse he fell across the body of his victim.

Was the King dead? That was my first thought. I rushed to where he lay. Yes, it seemed as if he were dead. He had a great cut across his forehead, and he lay still in a heap on the floor. I dropped on my knees beside him and put my ear down to hear if he breathed. But before I could, there was a loud sound from outside. I knew the sound: the bridge was being pushed out. A moment later it hit the wall on my side of the moat. I would be caught in a trap, and the King with me, if he still lived. He must take his chance, to live or die. I took my sword and went into the outer room. Who were pushing the bridge out—my men? If so, all was well. My eye fell on the guns, and I seized one. I paused to listen in the doorway of the outer room. To listen, I say? Yes, and to get my breath. I tore my shirt and twisted a strip of it round my bleeding arm. And I stood listening again. I would have given the world to hear Sapt's voice. For I was faint, spent, and weary. And that wild-cat Rupert Hentzau was still free in the Castle. Yet I could defend the narrow door at the top of the stairs better than the wider entrance to the room. So I dragged myself up the steps and stood behind it, listening.

What was the sound? Again it was a strange one for this place and time. An easy, mocking, merry laugh—the laugh of young Rupert Hentzau! I could scarcely believe that a sane man would laugh. Yet the laugh told me that my men had not come. For they must have shot Rupert by now, if they had come. And the clock struck half-past two! My God! The door had not been opened! They had gone to the bank! They had not found me! They had gone by now back to Tarlenheim, with the news of the King's death—and mine. Well, it would be true before they got there. Was not Rupert laughing in triumph?

For a moment I sank against the door, my nerve gone. Then I started up alert again, for Rupert cried with scorn:

"Well, the bridge is there! Come over it! And in God's name, let's see Black Michael. Keep back, you dogs! Michael, come and fight for her!"

If it were a fight with three sides, I might yet play my part. I turned the key in the door and looked out.

CHAPTER 19. Face to Face in the Forest

Face to Face in the Forest

For a moment I could see nothing. The light of lamps and burning sticks hit me right in the eyes from the other side of the bridge. But soon the scene grew clear. It was a strange scene. The bridge was in its place. At the far end stood a group of the duke’s servants. Two or three carried the lights which had blinded me. Three or four held long spears ready. They were pressed close together. Their weapons were pushed out in front of them. Their faces were pale and upset. To say it simply, they looked as very afraid as I have ever seen men look. They stared with fear at a man who stood in the middle of the bridge, sword in hand. Rupert Hentzau was in his trousers and shirt. The white cloth was marked with blood. But his easy, light pose told me that he himself was either not hurt at all or only lightly cut. There he stood, holding the bridge against them, and daring them to come on. Or rather, he was telling them to send Black Michael to him. And they, having no guns, moved back before the desperate man and did not dare to attack him. They spoke in low voices to one another. In the back row, I saw my friend Johann. He was leaning against the door frame and pressing a cloth to the blood which flowed from a wound in his cheek.

By a wonderful chance, I was in control. The cowards would not oppose me any more than they dared attack Rupert. I had only to raise my gun, and I would send him to his death with his sins on his head. He did not even know that I was there. I did nothing. Why, I hardly know to this day. I had killed one man in secret that night, and another by luck rather than skill. Perhaps it was that. Again, bad as the man was, I did not like being one of a crowd against him. Perhaps it was that. But stronger than either of these holding-back feelings came a wish to know and a deep interest which held me fixed, watching for the outcome of the scene.

“Michael, you dog! Michael! If you can stand, come on!” cried Rupert. He moved forward a step, and the group moved back a little before him. “Michael, you son of a dog! Come on!”

The answer to his mean words came in the wild cry of a woman:

“He is dead! My God, he is dead!”

“Dead!” shouted Rupert. “I struck better than I knew!” and he laughed in victory. Then he went on: “Down with your weapons there! I am your master now! Down with them, I say!”

I believe they would have done as he said. But as he spoke, new things happened. First, a distant sound arose, like shouts and hits from the other side of the large house. My heart jumped. It must be my men, come by a happy act against orders to look for me. The noise continued, but none of the rest seemed to notice it. Their attention was held by what now happened before their eyes. The group of servants opened, and a woman walked unsteadily onto the bridge. Antoinette de Mauban was in a loose white gown. Her dark hair streamed over her shoulders. Her face was as pale as death, and her eyes shone wildly in the light of the burning sticks. In her shaking hand she held a gun. As she walked unsteadily forward, she fired it at Rupert Hentzau. The ball missed him and struck the wood above my head.

“Faith, madame,” laughed Rupert, “if your eyes had been no more deadly than your shooting, I would not have been in this trouble tonight--nor Black Michael in hell!”

She took no notice of his words. With a wonderful effort, she calmed herself until she stood still and stiff. Then very slowly and carefully she began to raise her arm again, taking most careful aim.

He would be mad to risk it. He must rush at her, chancing the bullet, or move back towards me. I covered him with my weapon.

He did neither. Before she had got her aim, he bowed in his most graceful way. He cried “I can’t kill where I’ve kissed,” and before she or I could stop him, he laid his hand on the low wall of the bridge and lightly jumped into the water around the castle.

At that very moment I heard a rush of feet. A voice I knew--Sapt’s--cried: “God! it is the duke--dead!” Then I knew that the King needed me no more. I threw down my gun and sprang out on the bridge. There was a cry of wild wonder, “The King!” And then I, like Rupert of Hentzau, sword in hand, jumped over the low wall. I meant to finish my fight with him where I saw his curly head fifteen yards off in the water around the castle.

He swam swiftly and easily. I was tired and half unable to move well with my wounded arm. I could not gain on him. For a time I made no sound. But as we rounded the corner of the old tower I cried:

“Stop, Rupert, stop!”

I saw him look over his shoulder, but he swam on. He was under the bank now, searching, as I guessed, for a spot that he could climb. I knew there to be none. But there was my rope, which would still be hanging where I had left it. He would come to where it was before I could. Perhaps he would miss it. Perhaps he would find it. And if he drew it up after him, he would get a good start of me. I put out all my remaining strength and pressed on. At last I began to gain on him, for he was busy with his search and without thinking slowed his pace.

Ah, he had found it! A low shout of victory came from him. He took hold of it and began to pull himself up. I was near enough to hear him mutter: “How the devil comes this here?” I was at the rope, and he, hanging in mid air, saw me. But I could not reach him.

“Hullo! who is here?” he cried in a startled voice.

For a moment, I believe, he took me for the King. I dare say I was pale enough to make him think so. But an instant later he cried:

“Why it is the play-actor! How come you here, man?”

And so saying he gained the bank.

I took hold of the rope, but I paused. He stood on the bank, sword in hand, and he could cut my head open or run me through the heart as I came up. I let go the rope.

“Never mind,” said I; “but as I am here, I think I will stay.”

He smiled down on me.

“These women are the deuce--” he began. Then suddenly the great bell of the Castle started to ring furiously, and a loud shout reached us from the water around the castle.

Rupert smiled again and waved his hand to me.

“I should like a turn with you, but it is a little too hot!” said he, and he disappeared from above me.

In a second, without thinking of danger, I put my hand on the rope. I was up. I saw him thirty yards away, running like a deer towards the shelter of the forest. For once Rupert Hentzau had chosen to be careful. I put my feet on the ground and ran after him, calling to him to stop. He would not. He was not hurt and full of energy. He got further ahead at every step. But I forgot everything in the world except him and my need to catch him. I kept going. Soon the deep shadows of the forest of Zenda covered us both, the pursued and the pursuer.

It was three in the morning now, and day was coming. I was on a long straight grass road, and a hundred yards ahead ran young Rupert. His curly hair moved in the fresh wind. I was tired and breathing hard. He looked over his shoulder and waved his hand at me again. He was making fun of me, because he saw he was faster than me. I had to stop for breath. A moment later, Rupert turned sharply to the right and I lost sight of him.

I thought all was over, and in deep upset I sank on the ground. But I was up again at once, for a scream rang through the forest--a woman's scream. I used the last of my strength and ran on to the place where he had turned out of my sight. I turned also, and I saw him again. But alas! I could not touch him. He was lifting a girl down from her horse. It was surely her scream that I heard. She looked like a small farmer's or a peasant's daughter, and she carried a basket on her arm. Probably she was on her way to the early market at Zenda. Her horse was a strong, well shaped animal. Master Rupert lifted her down while she screamed--the sight of him scared her. But he treated her gently, laughed, kissed her, and gave her money. Then he jumped on the horse, sitting sideways like a woman. Then he waited for me. I, on my part, waited for him.

Presently he rode towards me, keeping his distance, however. He lifted up his hand, saying:

"What did you do in the Castle?"

"I killed three of your friends," said I.

"What! You got to the cells?"

"Yes."

"And the King?"

"He was hurt by Detchard before I killed Detchard, but I pray that he lives."

"You fool!" said Rupert, in a light voice.

"One thing more I did."

"And what is that?"

"I let you live. I was behind you on the bridge, with a gun in my hand."

"No? Truly, I was between two fires!"

"Get off your horse," I cried, "and fight like a man."

"Before a lady!" said he, pointing to the girl. "Shame on you, your Majesty!"

Then in my anger, hardly knowing what I did, I rushed at him. For a moment he seemed to hesitate. Then he pulled his horse in and stood waiting for me. On I went in my foolishness. I seized the bridle and I struck at him. He blocked it and thrust at me. I fell back a step and rushed at him again. This time I reached his face and cut his cheek open, and jumped back almost before he could strike me. He seemed almost stunned at the fierceness of my attack. Otherwise I think he must have killed me. I went down on my knee breathing hard, expecting him to ride at me. And so he would have done, and then and there, I do not doubt, one or both of us would have died. But at that moment there came a shout from behind us. Looking round, I saw, just at the turn of the road, a man on a horse. He was riding hard, and he carried a gun in his hand. It was Fritz von Tarlenheim, my faithful friend. Rupert saw him, and knew that the game was up. He stopped his rush at me and swung his leg over the saddle, but yet for just a moment he waited. Leaning forward, he tossed his hair off his forehead and smiled, and said: "_Au revoir_, Rudolf Rassendyll!"

Then, with his cheek streaming blood, but his lips laughing and his body swaying with ease and grace, he bowed to me. And he bowed to the farm-girl, who had come near in trembling wonder, and he waved his hand to Fritz, who was just within range and let fly a shot at him. The ball came close to doing its work, for it struck the sword he held. He dropped the sword with an oath, wringing his fingers, and clapped his heels hard on his horse's belly, and rode away at a gallop.

And I watched him go down the long road, riding as though he rode for his pleasure and singing as he went, for all there was that gash in his cheek.

Once again he turned to wave his hand, and then the gloom of thickets swallowed him and he was lost from our sight. Thus he vanished--reckless and wary, graceful and graceless, handsome, debonair, vile, and unconquered. And I threw my sword passionately on the ground and cried to Fritz to ride after him. But Fritz stopped his horse, and jumped down and ran to me, and went down on his knee, putting his arm about me. And indeed it was time, for the wound that Detchard had given me was broken open afresh, and my blood was staining the ground.

"Then give me the horse!" I cried, staggering to my feet and throwing his arms off me. And the strength of my anger carried me so far as where the horse stood, and then I fell flat beside it. And Fritz went down on his knee by me again.

"Fritz!" I said.

"Yes, friend--dear friend!" he said, gentle as a woman.

"Is the King alive?"

He took his handkerchief and wiped my lips, and bent and kissed me on the forehead.

"Thanks to the most gallant gentleman that lives," said he softly, "the King is alive!"

The little farm-girl stood by us, weeping for fear and wide-eyed for wonder. For she had seen me at Zenda. And was not I, pale, dripping, foul, and bloody as I was--yet was not I the King?

And when I heard that the King was alive, I tried to cry "Hurrah!" But I could not speak. I laid my head back in Fritz's arms and closed my eyes, and I groaned. And then, because I did not want Fritz to think wrong of me, I opened my eyes and tried to say "Hurrah!" again. But I could not. And being very tired, and now very cold, I pulled myself close up to Fritz, to get the warmth of him, and shut my eyes again and went to sleep.

CHAPTER 20. The Prisoner and the King

The Prisoner and the King

To fully understand what had happened in the Castle of Zenda, I must add to my own account of that night. I will briefly tell what I later learnt from Fritz and Madame de Mauban. Her story clearly explained how the cry I had planned as a trick and a false alarm had come, in terrible reality, before its time. At that moment it seemed to have ruined our hopes. But in the end it helped them. I believe a true love for the Duke of Strelsau drove the unhappy woman. The shining hopes of power over him also drove her. At his request, she had followed him from Paris to Ruritania. He was a man of strong feelings, but of stronger will. His cool head ruled both. He was happy to take all and give nothing. When she arrived, she soon found that she had a rival in the Princess Flavia. She became desperate. She would stop at nothing that might give her power over the duke, or keep it for her. As I say, he took and gave not. At the same time, Antoinette found herself caught in his bold plans. She was unwilling to leave him. Chains of shame and hope tied her to him. Yet she would not be a decoy. She would not lure me to death at his command. That is why she had written the letters of warning. I do not know if the lines she sent to Flavia came from good or bad feeling, from jealousy or from pity. But here also she served us well. When the duke went to Zenda, she went with him. Here for the first time she learnt the full measure of his cruelty. She was touched with compassion for the unfortunate King. From this time she was with us. Yet, from what she told me, I know that she still loved Michael, as women will. She trusted to gain his life from the King, if not his pardon, as the reward for her help. She did not want his triumph. She hated his crime. She hated even more fiercely what would be the prize of it: his marriage with his cousin, Princess Flavia.

At Zenda new forces came into play: the lust and daring of young Rupert. Perhaps her beauty caught him. Perhaps it was enough for him that she belonged to another man, and that she hated him. For many days there had been quarrels and ill will between him and the duke. The scene I had witnessed in the duke’s room was only one of many. Rupert’s proposals to me did not surprise her at all when I related them. She had of course been ignorant of them. She had herself warned Michael against Rupert, even when she was calling on me to deliver her from both of them. On this night, then, Rupert had determined to have his will. When she had gone to her room, he had made his entrance. He had furnished himself with a key to it. Her cries had brought the duke. There in the dark room, while she screamed, the men had fought. Rupert had wounded his master with a mortal blow. Then the servants rushed in. He had escaped through the window as I have described. The duke’s blood, spurting out, had stained his opponent’s shirt. But Rupert did not know that he had dealt Michael his death. He was eager to finish the encounter. How he meant to deal with the other three of the band, I know not. I dare say he did not think. The killing of Michael was not premeditated. Antoinette, left alone with the duke, had tried to stanch his wound. She was busy with this till he died. Then she heard Rupert’s taunts. She had come forth to avenge him. She had not seen me. She did not see me till I darted out of my ambush and leapt after Rupert into the moat.

The same moment found my friends on the scene. They had reached the chateau in due time and waited ready by the door. But Johann, swept with the rest to the rescue of the duke, did not open it. Nay, he took a part against Rupert. He put himself forward more bravely than any in his anxiety to avert suspicion. He had received a wound in the embrasure of the window. Sapt waited till nearly half-past two. Then, following my orders, he had sent Fritz to search the banks of the moat. I was not there. Fritz hastened back and told Sapt. Sapt was for following orders still. He wanted to ride at full speed back to Tarlenheim. But Fritz would not hear of abandoning me, let me have ordered what I would. On this they disputed some few minutes. Then Sapt, persuaded by Fritz, detached a party under Bernenstein. He sent them to gallop back to Tarlenheim and bring up the marshal. The rest fell to on the great door of the chateau. For several minutes it resisted them. Then Antoinette de Mauban fired at Rupert of Hentzau on the bridge. Just then they broke in, eight of them in all. The first door they came to was the door of Michael’s room. Michael lay dead across the threshold, with a sword-thrust through his breast. Sapt cried out at his death, as I had heard. They rushed on the servants. But these dropped their weapons in fear. Antoinette flung herself weeping at Sapt’s feet. All she cried was that I had been at the end of the bridge and leapt off. “What of the prisoner?” asked Sapt. But she shook her head. Then Sapt and Fritz crossed the bridge, with the gentlemen behind them. They went slowly, warily, and without noise. Fritz stumbled over the body of De Gautet in the way of the door. They felt him and found him dead.

Then they talked together. They listened closely for any sound from the cells below. But no sound came. They were very afraid that the King's guards had killed him. They feared the guards had pushed his body through the great pipe and escaped the same way. Yet they still had some hope, because I had been seen here. Fritz told me this later, in his friendship. They went back to Michael's body. They pushed aside Antoinette, who was praying by it. They found a key to the door I had locked, and opened the door. The staircase was dark. At first they would not use a torch, because they did not want to be more exposed to fire. But soon Fritz cried: "The door down there is open! See, there is light!" So they went on without fear, and found no one to oppose them. They came to the outer room and saw the Belgian, Bersonin, lying dead. They thanked God. Sapt said: "Yes, he has been here." Then they rushed into the King's cell. They found Detchard lying dead across the dead doctor. The King was on his back with his chair by him. Fritz cried: "He is dead!" Sapt drove everyone out of the room except Fritz. He knelt down by the King. He knew more about wounds and the signs of death than I did. He soon knew that the King was not dead. He also knew the King would not die if he was properly attended. They covered his face and carried him to Duke Michael's room. They laid him there. Antoinette rose from praying by the body of the duke. She went to bathe the King's head and dress his wounds until a doctor came. Sapt saw that I had been there. He had heard Antoinette's story. He sent Fritz to search the moat and then the forest. He dared send no one else. Fritz found my horse, and feared the worst. Then, as I have told, he found me. He was guided by the shout with which I had called on Rupert to stop and face me. I think no man has ever been more glad to find his own brother alive than Fritz was to come on me. In love and anxiety for me, he thought nothing of a thing so great as would have been the death of Rupert Hentzau. Yet, if Fritz had killed him, I should have felt it was wrong.

The enterprise of the King's rescue was this way brought to a good end. It lay on Colonel Sapt to secure secrecy about the King ever having been in need of rescue. Antoinette de Mauban and Johann the keeper were sworn to reveal nothing. Johann was, indeed, too much hurt to be talking just now. Fritz went out to find not the King, but the unnamed friend of the King. This friend had lain in Zenda and flashed for a moment before the dazed eyes of Duke Michael's servants on the drawbridge. The change had happened. The King had been wounded almost to death by the attacks of the gaolers who guarded his friend. At last he had overcome them. He rested now, wounded but alive, in Black Michael's own room in the Castle. He had been carried there from the cell, his face covered with a cloak. From there orders were issued. If his friend were found, he should be brought directly and privately to the King. Meanwhile messengers should ride at full speed to Tarlenheim. They should tell Marshall Strakencz to assure the princess of the King's safety. They should tell him to come himself with all speed to greet the King. The princess was ordered to remain at Tarlenheim. There she should await her cousin's coming or his further orders. Thus the King would come to his own again. He had done brave deeds. He had escaped, almost by a miracle, the treacherous attack of his unnatural brother.

This clever arrangement of my long-headed old friend succeeded in every way. It failed only where it met a force that often defeats the most cunning plans. I mean nothing else than the pleasure of a woman. Her cousin and sovereign sent what command he chose (or Colonel Sapt chose for him). Marshal Strakencz insisted as he would. But the Princess Flavia was in no way minded to rest at Tarlenheim while her lover lay wounded at Zenda. The Marshal rode out from Tarlenheim with a small suite on the way to Zenda. The princess's carriage followed immediately behind. In this order they passed through the town. The report was already rife there. It said the King had gone the night before to remonstrate with his brother, in all friendliness, because he held one of the King's friends in confinement in the Castle. It said the King had been most traitorously set upon. It said there had been a desperate conflict. It said the duke was slain with several of his gentlemen. It said the King, wounded as he was, had seized and held the Castle of Zenda. All of this talk made a mighty excitement, as may be supposed. The wires were set in motion. The tidings came to Strelsau only just after orders had been sent there. The orders were to parade the troops and overawe the dissatisfied quarters of the town with a display of force.

Thus the Princess Flavia came to Zenda. She drove up the hill. The Marshal rode by the wheel and still begged her to return in obedience to the King's orders. Fritz von Tarlenheim, with the prisoner of Zenda, came to the edge of the forest. I had revived from my swoon and walked, resting on Fritz's arm. I looked out from the cover of the trees and saw the princess. Suddenly I understood from a glance at my companion's face that we must not meet her. I sank on my knees behind a clump of bushes. But there was one whom we had forgotten. She had followed us and was not disposed to let slip the chance of earning a smile and maybe a crown or two. While we lay hidden, the little farm-girl came by us and ran to the princess. She curtseyed and cried:

"Madame, the King is here--in the bushes! May I guide you to him, madame?"

"Nonsense, child!" said old Strakencz. "The King lies wounded in the Castle."

"Yes, sir, he is wounded, I know. But he is there--with Count Fritz--and not at the Castle," she persisted.

"Is he in two places, or are there two Kings?" asked Flavia, bewildered. "And how should he be there?"

"He pursued a gentleman, madame, and they fought until Count Fritz came. And the other gentleman took my father's horse from me and rode away. But the King is here with Count Fritz. Why, madame, is there another man in Ruritania like the King?"

"No, my child," said Flavia softly (I was told it afterwards). She smiled and gave the girl money. "I will go and see this gentleman," and she rose to alight from the carriage.

But at this moment Sapt came riding from the Castle. He saw the princess and made the best of a bad job. He cried to her that the King was well tended and in no danger.

“In the Castle?” she asked.

“Where else, madame?” he said, and bowed.

“But this girl says he is over there—with Count Fritz.”

Sapt turned his eyes on the child and smiled with doubt.

“Every fine gentleman is a King to a girl like her,” he said.

“Why, he looks as much like the King as one pea looks like another, madame!” cried the girl. She was a little shaken but still would not change her mind.

Sapt turned around quickly. The old Marshal’s face asked silent questions. Flavia’s look said just as much. Doubt spread fast.

“I’ll ride myself and see this man,” said Sapt quickly.

“No, I’ll come myself,” said the princess.

“Then come alone,” he whispered.

She obeyed the strange hint in his face and asked the Marshal and the others to wait. She and Sapt came on foot toward where we lay. Sapt waved to the farm girl to keep her distance. When I saw them coming, I sat in a sad pile on the ground and hid my face in my hands. I could not look at her. Fritz went down by me and put his hand on my shoulder.

“Speak low, whatever you say,” I heard Sapt whisper as they came up. The next thing I heard was a low cry—half joy, half fear—from the princess:

“It is he! Are you hurt?”

She fell on the ground by me and gently pulled my hands away. But I kept my eyes on the ground.

“It is the King!” she said. “Please, Colonel Sapt, tell me what was the point of the joke you played on me?”

None of us answered. We three were silent before her. She paid no attention to them. She threw her arms around my neck and kissed me. Then Sapt spoke in a low rough whisper:

“It is not the King. Don’t kiss him. He is not the King.”

She pulled back for a moment. Then, with one arm still around my neck, she asked with great anger:

“Do I not know my love? Rudolf my love!”

“It is not the King,” said old Sapt again. And a sudden cry broke from kind Fritz.

It was that cry that told her this was no joke.

“He is the King!” she cried. “It is the King’s face—the King’s ring—my ring! It is my love!”

“Your love, madame,” said old Sapt, “but not the King. The King is there in the Castle. This gentleman—”

“Look at me, Rudolf! look at me!” she cried. She took my face between her hands. “Why do you let them hurt me? Tell me what it means!”

Then I spoke, looking into her eyes.

“God forgive me, madame!” I said. “I am not the King!”

I felt her hands hold my cheeks. She looked at me as no one had ever looked at a man’s face before. I was silent again. I saw wonder born, and doubt grow, and fear come to life as she looked. Very slowly her hands loosened. She turned to Sapt, to Fritz, and back to me. Then suddenly she fell forward and dropped into my arms. With a great cry of pain I held her to me and kissed her lips. Sapt put his hand on my arm. I looked up in his face. I laid her softly on the ground and stood up, looking at her. I cursed heaven that young Rupert’s sword had saved me for this sharper pain.

CHAPTER 21. If love were all!

If love were all!

It was night, and I was in the cell where the King had lain in the Castle of Zenda. The great pipe that Rupert of Hentzau had called “Jacob’s Ladder” was gone. The lights in the room across the water twinkled in the darkness. All was still. The noise and fighting were gone. I had spent the day hidden in the forest, from the time when Fritz had led me off, leaving Sapt with the princess. When dusk came, I was brought to the Castle, wrapped up, and put in the room where I now lay. Three men had died there—two of them by my hand—but I was not troubled by ghosts. I had thrown myself on a small bed by the window and was looking out on the black water. Johann, the guard, still pale from his wound, but not much hurt besides, had brought me supper. He told me that the King was doing well and that he had seen the princess. She and he, Sapt and Fritz, had been together for a long time. Marshal Strakencz was gone to Strelsau. Black Michael lay in his coffin, and Antoinette de Mauban watched by him. Had I not heard, from the chapel, priests singing mass for him?

Outside there were strange stories going around. Some said that the prisoner of Zenda was dead. Some said that he had vanished yet alive. Some said that he was a friend who had served the King well in some adventure in England. Others said that he had discovered the Duke’s plots, and had therefore been taken away by him. One or two smart fellows shook their heads and said only that they would say nothing. But they had suspicions that more was to be known than was known, if Colonel Sapt would tell all he knew.

So Johann talked until I sent him away and lay there alone, thinking, not of the future, but—as a man often does when stirring things have happened to him—going over the events of the past weeks, and wondering how strangely they had turned out. And above me, in the stillness of the night, I heard the flags flapping against their poles. For Black Michael’s flag hung there half-mast high, and above it the royal flag of Ruritania, floating for one night more over my head. Habit grows so quick that only by an effort did I remember that it floated no longer for me.

Presently Fritz von Tarlenheim came into the room. I was standing then by the window. The glass was opened, and I was idly touching the cement which clung to the stonework where “Jacob’s Ladder” had been. He told me briefly that the King wanted me, and together we crossed the drawbridge and entered the room that had been Black Michael’s.

The King was lying there in bed. Our doctor from Tarlenheim was with him, and whispered to me that my visit must be brief. The King held out his hand and shook mine. Fritz and the doctor moved back to the window.

I took the King’s ring from my finger and placed it on his.

“I have tried not to dishonour it, sire,” said I.

“I can’t talk much to you,” he said, in a weak voice. “I have had a great fight with Sapt and the Marshal—for we have told the Marshal everything. I wanted to take you to Strelsau and keep you with me, and tell everyone of what you had done. And you would have been my best and nearest friend, Cousin Rudolf. But they tell me I must not, and that the secret must be kept—if kept it can be.”

“They are right, sire. Let me go. My work here is done.”

“Yes, it is done, as no man but you could have done it. When they see me again, I shall have my beard on. I shall—yes, faith, I shall be wasted with sickness. They will not wonder that the King looks changed in face. Cousin, I shall try to let them find him changed in nothing else. You have shown me how to play the King.”

“Sire,” said I. “I can take no praise from you. It is by the narrowest grace of God that I was not a worse traitor than your brother.”

He turned inquiring eyes on me. But a sick man shrinks from puzzles, and he had no strength to question me. His glance fell on Flavia’s ring, which I wore. I thought he would question me about it. But, after touching it idly, he let his head fall on his pillow.

“I don’t know when I shall see you again,” he said faintly, almost without interest.

“If I can ever serve you again, sire,” I answered.

His eyelids closed. Fritz came with the doctor. I kissed the King’s hand, and let Fritz lead me away. I have never seen the King since.

Outside, Fritz turned, not to the right, back towards the drawbridge, but to the left, and without speaking led me upstairs, through a handsome corridor in the chateau.

“Where are we going?” I asked.

Looking away from me, Fritz answered:

“She has sent for you. When it is over, come back to the bridge. I’ll wait for you there.”

“What does she want?” said I, breathing quickly.

He shook his head.

“Does she know everything?”

“Yes, everything.”

He opened a door, and gently pushing me in, closed it behind me. I found myself in a drawing-room, small and richly furnished. At first I thought that I was alone, for the light that came from a pair of shaded candles on the mantelpiece was very dim. But presently I saw a woman’s figure standing by the window. I knew it was the princess, and I walked up to her, fell on one knee, and carried the hand that hung by her side to my lips. She neither moved nor spoke. I rose to my feet, and, piercing the gloom with my eager eyes, saw her pale face and the gleam of her hair, and before I knew, I spoke softly:

“Flavia!”

She trembled a little, and looked round. Then she darted to me, taking hold of me.

“Don’t stand, don’t stand! No, you mustn’t! You’re hurt! Sit down—here, here!”

She made me sit on a sofa, and put her hand on my forehead.

“How hot your head is,” she said, sinking on her knees by me. Then she laid her head against me, and I heard her murmur: “My darling, how hot your head is!”

Somehow love gives even to a dull man the knowledge of his lover’s heart. I had come to humble myself and pray pardon for my presumption. But what I said now was:

“I love you with all my heart and soul!”

For what troubled and shamed her? Not her love for me, but the fear that I had counterfeited the lover as I had acted the King, and taken her kisses with a smothered smile.

“With all my life and heart,” I said, as she held on to me. “Always, from the first moment I saw you in the Cathedral! There has been only one woman in the world for me—and there will be no other. But God forgive me for the wrong I have done to you!”

“They made you do it!” she said quickly. Then she raised her head and looked in my eyes. “It might have made no difference if I had known it. It was always you, never the King!”

“I meant to tell you,” I said. “I was going to on the night of the ball in Strelsau, when Sapt interrupted me. After that, I could not—I could not risk losing you before—before—I must! My love, for you I almost let the King die!”

“I know, I know! What are we to do now, Rudolf?”

I put my arm around her and held her up while I said:

“I am going away tonight.”

“Ah, no, no!” she cried. “Not tonight!”

“I must go tonight, before more people have seen me. And how could I stay, my love, except—?”

“If I could come with you!” she whispered very low.

“My God!” I said roughly. “Do not talk about that!” And I pushed her a little back from me.

“Why not? I love you. You are as good a gentleman as the King!”

Then I was false to all that I should have held to. I caught her in my arms and begged her, in words that I will not write, to come with me. I dared all Ruritania to take her from me. For a while she listened, with wondering, bright eyes. But as her eyes looked on me, I grew ashamed. My voice died away in broken sounds, and at last I was silent.

She pulled herself away from me and stood against the wall. I sat on the edge of the sofa, shaking in every limb. I knew what I had done—I hated it, but I would not take it back. So we rested a long time.

“I am mad!” I said in a low, angry voice.

“I love your madness, dear,” she answered.

Her face was away from me, but I caught the shine of a tear on her cheek. I held the sofa with my hand and kept myself there.

“Is love the only thing?” she asked, in low, sweet tones that seemed to bring a calm even to my hurt heart. “If love were the only thing, I would follow you—in old torn clothes, if need be—to the end of the world. For you hold my heart in the palm of your hand! But is love the only thing?”

I made no answer. It gives me shame now to think that I would not help her.

She came near me and laid her hand on my shoulder. I put my hand up and held hers.

“I know people write and talk as if it were. Perhaps, for some, Fate lets it be. Ah, if I were one of them! But if love had been the only thing, you would have let the King die in his cell.”

I kissed her hand.

“Honor binds a woman too, Rudolf. My honor lies in being true to my country and my House. I do not know why God has let me love you. But I know that I must stay.”

Still I said nothing. She paused a while, then went on:

“Your ring will always be on my finger, your heart in my heart, the touch of your lips on mine. But you must go and I must stay. Perhaps I must do what it kills me to think of doing.”

I knew what she meant, and a cold feeling ran through me. But I could not completely fail her. I rose and took her hand.

“Do what you will, or what you must,” I said. “I think God shows His purposes to such as you. My part is lighter. For your ring shall be on my finger and your heart in mine, and no touch except your lips will ever be on mine. So, may God comfort you, my love!”

Then we heard the sound of singing. The priests in the chapel were singing masses for the souls of those who lay dead. They seemed to sing a requiem over our buried joy. They seemed to pray forgiveness for our love that would not die. The soft, sweet, pitiful music rose and fell as we stood opposite one another, her hands in mine.

“My queen and my beauty!” I said.

“My lover and true knight!” she said. “Perhaps we shall never see one another again. Kiss me, my dear, and go!”

I kissed her as she asked me. But at the last she held on to me, whispering nothing but my name. She said it over and over again—and again—and again. Then I left her.

I walked quickly down to the bridge. Sapt and Fritz were waiting for me. Under their directions I changed my dress. I covered my face, as I had done more than once before. I mounted with them at the door of the Castle. We three rode through the night and on to the breaking day. We found ourselves at a little roadside station just over the border of Ruritania. The train was not quite due. I walked with them in a meadow by a little brook while we waited for it. They promised to send me all news. They gave me so much kindness—even old Sapt was touched to gentleness, while Fritz was half broken. I listened in a kind of dream to all they said. “Rudolf! Rudolf! Rudolf!” still rang in my ears—a burden of sorrow and of love. At last they saw that I could not pay attention to them. We walked up and down in silence, until Fritz touched me on the arm. I saw, a mile or more away, the blue smoke of the train. Then I held out a hand to each of them.

“We are all but half-men this morning,” I said, smiling. “But we have been men, eh, Sapt and Fritz, old friends? We have run a good course between us.”

“We have defeated traitors and set the King firm on his throne,” said Sapt.

Then Fritz von Tarlenheim suddenly, before I could see his purpose or stop him, uncovered his head and bent as he used to do, and kissed my hand. As I pulled it away, he said, trying to laugh:

“Heaven does not always make the right men kings!”

Old Sapt twisted his mouth as he shook my hand.

“The devil has his share in most things,” he said.

The people at the station looked with interest at the tall man with the covered face. But we did not notice their looks. I stood with my two friends and waited until the train came up to us. Then we shook hands again and said nothing. This time both of them took off their hats. It seemed strange, even from old Sapt. They stood still until the train carried me away from their sight. So people thought some great man travelled alone for pleasure from the little station that morning. But in truth it was only I, Rudolf Rassendyll, an English gentleman, a younger son of a good family. I was a man with no wealth, no position, and not much rank. They would have been sad to know that. Yet if they had known everything, they would have looked with even more interest. For whatever I might be now, I had been a King for three months. That is not a thing to be proud of, but it is at least an experience to have had. I would surely have thought more of it, but through the air came a sound. From the towers of Zenda, far away as we left, it came into my ears and into my heart. It was the cry of a woman's love: "Rudolf! Rudolf! Rudolf!"

Listen! I hear it now!

CHAPTER 22. Present, Past--and Future?

My return home is not very interesting. I went straight to the Tyrol and spent a quiet two weeks. I was mostly lying down, because I had caught a bad cold. I was also very weak from nervous strain, as weak as a baby. As soon as I reached my rooms, I sent a casual card to my brother. I told him I was in good health and would soon return. That would help with questions about where I was. Those questions were probably still troubling the Prefect of the Police of Strelsau. I let my moustache and small beard grow again. Hair grows fast on my face. They looked decent, though not thick, by the time I reached Paris and visited my friend George Featherly. My talk with him was mostly important for the many lies I told. I did not want to tell them, but I had to. He told me he had decided I had followed Madame de Mauban to Strelsau. I laughed at him without pity. The lady, it seemed, was back in Paris. But she was living very quietly. Gossip had no trouble explaining that. Did not all the world know of the disloyalty and death of Duke Michael? Still, George told Bertram Bertrand to take heart. "For," he said in a light way, "a live poet is better than a dead duke." Then he turned to me and asked:

"What have you done to your moustache?"

"To tell the truth," I answered, with a secretive look, "a man sometimes has reasons for wanting to change his appearance. But it is growing back very well."

"What? Then I was not so far off! If not the fair Antoinette, there was another woman?"

"There is always another woman," I said in a wise-sounding way.

But George was not satisfied until he had pulled out of me a completely made-up love affair. He was very proud of his smartness. The story had a small amount of scandal in it. It had kept me all this time in the peaceful Tyrol. In return for this story, George gave me a lot of what he called "inside information." He said only diplomats knew it. It was about the true course of events in Ruritania, the plots and secret plans. In his opinion, he told me with a meaningful nod, there was more to be said for Black Michael than the public supposed. He hinted at a well-founded suspicion. Many short news items had appeared about the mysterious prisoner of Zenda. George said this prisoner was not a man at all. Instead (here I had much trouble not to smile) it was a woman dressed as a man. He said the fight between the King and his brother over this made-up lady's favor was at the bottom of their fight.

"Perhaps it was Madame de Mauban herself," I suggested.

"No!" said George firmly. "Antoinette de Mauban was jealous of her. She turned against the duke and helped the King for that reason. And to confirm what I say, it is well known that the Princess Flavia is now very cold to the King. Before, she had been most loving."

At this point I changed the subject. I escaped from George's "inspired" false beliefs. But if diplomats never know anything more than they had found out in this case, they seem to me to be somewhat expensive costly things.

While in Paris I wrote to Antoinette, though I did not dare to visit her. I received in return a very moving letter. In it she assured me that the King's generosity and kindness, as well as her regard for me, bound her conscience to complete secrecy. She said she meant to settle in the country and withdraw entirely from society. I have never heard whether she carried out her plans. But as I have not met her, or heard news of her up to this time, it is likely that she did. There is no doubt that she was deeply attached to the Duke of Strelsau. Her conduct at the time of his death proved that no knowledge of the man's real character was enough to root her regard for him out of her heart.

I had one more battle left to fight. I knew it would be severe. It was bound to end in my complete defeat. Was I not back from the Tyrol without having made any study of its people, institutions, scenery, animals, plants, or other features? Had I not simply wasted my time in my usual silly, good-for-nothing way? That was how the matter would look to my sister-in-law. I was forced to admit that. And against a verdict based on such evidence, I had really no defense to offer. It may be supposed, then, that I presented myself in Park Lane in an ashamed, timid way. On the whole, my reception was not so alarming as I had feared. It turned out that I had done, not what Rose wished, but the next best thing. I had done what she said would happen. She had declared that I should make no notes, record no observations, gather no materials. My brother, on the other hand, had been weak enough to maintain that a serious resolve had at last moved me.

When I returned empty-handed, Rose was so busy winning over Burlesdon that she let me down quite easily. She devoted most of her complaints to my failure to tell my friends where I was.

"We've wasted a lot of time trying to find you," she said.

"I know you have," said I. "Half our ambassadors have led tired lives on my account. George Featherly told me so. But why should you have been anxious? I can take care of myself."

"Oh, it was not that," she cried with scorn, "but I wanted to tell you about Sir Jacob Borrodaile. You know, he has got an Embassy--at least, he will have in a month--and he wrote to say he hoped you would go with him."

"Where is he going to?"

"He is going to succeed Lord Topham at Strelsau," said she. "You could not have a nicer place, short of Paris."

"Strelsau! H'm!" said I, glancing at my brother.

"Oh, that does not matter!" exclaimed Rose with impatience. "Now, you will go, won't you?"

"I do not know that I care about it!"

"Oh, you are too annoying!"

"And I do not think I can go to Strelsau. My dear Rose, would it be--suitable?"

"Oh, nobody remembers that horrid old story now."

Upon this, I took out of my pocket a portrait of the King of Ruritania. It had been taken a month or two before he ascended the throne. She could not miss my point when I said, putting it into her hands:

"In case you have not seen, or not noticed, a picture of Rudolf V, there he is. Do you not think they might recall the story, if I appeared at the Court of Ruritania?"

My sister-in-law looked at the portrait, and then at me.

"Good gracious!" she said, and flung the photograph down on the table.

"What do you say, Bob?" I asked.

Burlesdon got up. He went to a corner of the room and searched in a pile of newspapers. Soon he came back with a copy of the Illustrated London News. He opened the paper and showed a double-page picture of the Coronation of Rudolf V at Strelsau. He laid the photograph and the picture side by side. I sat at the table facing them. As I looked, I became very interested. My eye moved from my own portrait to Sapt, to Strakencz, to the rich clothes of the Cardinal, to Black Michael’s face, to the grand figure of the princess by his side. I looked for a long time and with great interest. My brother’s hand on my shoulder woke me. He was looking down at me with a puzzled face.

“It’s a remarkable likeness, you see,” said I. “I really think I had better not go to Ruritania.”

Rose was half convinced, but she would not give up her position.

“It’s just an excuse,” she said crossly. “You do not want to do anything. Why, you might become an ambassador!”

“I do not think I want to be an ambassador,” said I.

“It’s more than you ever will be,” she replied.

That is very likely true, but it is not more than I have been.

The idea of being an ambassador could hardly amaze me. I had been a king!

So pretty Rose left us in anger. Burlesdon lit a cigarette and looked at me still with that curious gaze.

“That picture in the paper--” he said.

“Well, what of it? It shows that the King of Ruritania and your humble servant are as like as two peas.”

My brother shook his head.

“I suppose so,” he said. “But I should know you from the man in the photograph.”

“And not from the picture in the paper?”

“I should know the photograph from the picture. The picture is very like the photograph, but--”

“Well?”

“It’s more like you!” said my brother.

My brother is a good man and true. So, even though he is a married man and very fond of his wife, he should know any secret of mine. But this secret was not mine, and I could not tell it to him.

“I do not think it’s so much like me as the photograph,” said I boldly. “But, anyhow, Bob, I won’t go to Strelsau.”

“No, do not go to Strelsau, Rudolf,” said he.

And whether he suspects anything, or has a small sign of the truth, I do not know. If he has, he keeps it to himself, and he and I never speak of it. And we let Sir Jacob Borrodaile find another attaché.

Since all these events whose history I have set down happened, I have lived a very quiet life at a small house which I have taken in the country. The ordinary ambitions and aims of men in my position seem to me dull and unattractive. I have little wish for the rush of society, and none for the push of politics. Lady Burlesdon has no hope for me at all. My neighbours think me a lazy, dreamy, unsociable fellow. Yet I am a young man. And sometimes I have a wish--the superstitious would call it a feeling about the future--that my part in life is not yet altogether played. That, somehow and some day, I shall mix again in great affairs. I shall again make policies in a busy brain. I shall match my wits against my enemies’. I shall brace my muscles to fight a good fight and strike strong blows. Such is the web of my thoughts as, with gun or rod in hand, I wander through the woods or by the side of the stream. Whether the wish will be fulfilled, I cannot tell. Still less can I tell whether the scene that, led by memory, I lay for my new exploits will be the true one. For I love to see myself once again in the crowded streets of Strelsau, or beneath the frowning keep of the Castle of Zenda.

Thus led, my thoughts leave the future and turn back on the past. Shapes rise before me in long array--the wild first revel with the King, the rush with my brave tea-table, the night in the moat, the pursuit in the forest. My friends and my foes, the people who learnt to love and honour me, the desperate men who tried to kill me. And, from among these last, comes one who alone of all of them yet moves on earth. Though where I know not, yet he plans (as I do not doubt) wickedness. Yet he turns women’s hearts to softness and men’s to fear and hate. Where is young Rupert of Hentzau--the boy who came so near to beating me? When his name comes into my head, I feel my hand grip and the blood move quicker through my veins. And the hint of Fate--the feeling about the future--seems to grow stronger and more definite. It seems to whisper insistently in my ear that I have yet a hand to play with young Rupert. Therefore I exercise myself in arms, and seek to put off the day when the vigour of youth must leave me.

One break comes every year in my quiet life. Then I go to Dresden, and there I am met by my dear friend and companion, Fritz von Tarlenheim. Last time, his pretty wife Helga came, and a strong, crowing baby with her. And for a week Fritz and I are together, and I hear all of what happens in Strelsau. And in the evenings, as we walk and smoke together, we talk of Sapt, and of the King, and often of young Rupert. And, as the hours grow small, at last we speak of Flavia. For every year Fritz carries with him to Dresden a little box. In it lies a red rose, and round the stalk of the rose is a slip of paper with the words written: “Rudolf--Flavia--always.” And the like I send back by him. That message, and the wearing of the rings, are all that now bind me and the Queen of Ruritania. For--more noble, as I hold her, for the act--she has followed where her duty to her country and her House led her. She is the wife of the King, uniting his subjects to him by the love they bear to her. She gives peace and quiet days to thousands by her self-sacrifice. There are moments when I dare not think of it. But there are others when I rise in spirit to where she ever dwells. Then I can thank God that I love the noblest lady in the world, the most gracious and beautiful. And I can thank God that there was nothing in my love that made her fall short in her high duty.

Will I see her face again—the pale face and the beautiful hair? I know nothing about that. Fate gives no hint. My heart gives no feeling of what is coming. I do not know. In this world, perhaps—no, it is likely—never. And can it be that somewhere, in a way our flesh-bound minds cannot understand, she and I will be together again? With nothing to come between us, nothing to stop our love? That I do not know, and neither do wiser heads than mine. But if it is never—if I can never talk sweetly with her again, or look upon her face, or know her love from her—why, then, on this side of the grave, I will live as the man she loves should live. And for the other side, I must pray for a sleep without dreams.
