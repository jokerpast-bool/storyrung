CHAPTER 1. The Science of Deduction

The Science of Deduction

Sherlock Holmes took his bottle from the corner of the shelf above the fireplace. He took his needle from its clean case. With his long, white, nervous fingers he adjusted the thin needle. He rolled back his left shirt sleeve. For some time his eyes rested on his strong arm and wrist. They were covered with many small marks from the needle. Finally he pushed the sharp point in. He pressed down the small plunger. Then he sank back into the soft chair with a long sigh of satisfaction.

Three times a day for many months I had seen this. But time had not made me accept it. Instead, day by day I had become more angry at the sight. My sense of right and wrong grew inside me each night. I thought that I had lacked the courage to speak. Again and again I had promised myself that I would speak my mind on the subject. But there was something in the cool, calm manner of my friend. It made him the last man with whom one would want to take any kind of freedom. His great powers, his skilled manner, and my experience of his many unusual qualities all made me shy and slow to oppose him.

Yet on that afternoon, I suddenly felt that I could hold out no longer. It may have been the wine I had taken with my lunch. It may have been the added anger caused by his very slow manner.

"Which is it today?" I asked. "Morphine or cocaine?"

He raised his eyes slowly from the old black book which he had opened. "It is cocaine," he said. "A seven-per-cent solution. Would you like to try it?"

"No, indeed," I answered, sharply. "My body has not got over the Afghan campaign yet. I cannot afford to put any extra strain upon it."

He smiled at my strong feeling. "Perhaps you are right, Watson," he said. "I suppose that its influence is physically a bad one. I find it, however, so greatly stimulating and clearing to the mind that its secondary effect is a small matter."

"But consider!" I said, seriously. "Count the cost! Your brain may, as you say, be woken and excited. But it is a diseased and unhealthy process. It involves increased tissue change and may at last leave a permanent weakness. You know, too, what a black reaction comes upon you. Surely the game is hardly worth the candle. Why should you, for a mere passing pleasure, risk the loss of those great powers with which you have been given? Remember that I speak not only as one friend to another, but as a doctor to one for whose health he is to some extent responsible."

He did not seem offended. Instead, he put his finger-tips together and leaned his elbows on the arms of his chair, like one who enjoys conversation.

"My mind," he said, "rebels at stillness. Give me problems, give me work, give me the most difficult secret writing or the most complex analysis, and I am in my own proper air. I can do without artificial stimulants then. But I hate the dull routine of existence. I long for mental excitement. That is why I have chosen my own particular profession. Or rather created it, for I am the only one in the world."

"The only unofficial detective?" I said, raising my eyebrows.

"The only unofficial consulting detective," he answered. "I am the last and highest court of appeal in detection. When Gregson or Lestrade or Athelney Jones are out of their depth—which, by the way, is their normal state—the matter is laid before me. I examine the facts, as an expert, and give a specialist's opinion. I claim no credit in such cases. My name figures in no newspaper. The work itself, the pleasure of finding a field for my peculiar powers, is my highest reward. But you have yourself had some experience of my methods of work in the Jefferson Hope case."

"Yes, indeed," said I, warmly. "I was never so struck by anything in my life. I even put it in a small book with the somewhat strange title of 'A Study in Scarlet.'"

He shook his head sadly. "I glanced over it," said he. "Honestly, I cannot congratulate you upon it. Detection is, or ought to be, an exact science. It should be treated in the same cold and unemotional manner. You have attempted to color it with romance. That produces much the same effect as if you worked a love-story or an elopement into the fifth proposition of Euclid."

"But the romance was there," I argued. "I could not change the facts."

"Some facts should be held back, or at least a just sense of proportion should be observed in treating them. The only point in the case which deserved mention was the curious analytical reasoning from effects to causes by which I succeeded in unraveling it."

I was annoyed at this criticism of a work which had been specially designed to please him. I confess, too, that I was irritated by the self-love which seemed to demand that every line of my pamphlet should be devoted to his own special doings. More than once during the years that I had lived with him in Baker Street I had observed that a small vanity lay under my friend's quiet and teaching manner. I made no remark, however, but sat nursing my wounded leg. I had a Jezail bullet through it some time before. Though it did not prevent me from walking, it ached wearily at every change of the weather.

"My practice has extended recently to the Continent," said Holmes, after a while, filling up his old brier-root pipe. "I was consulted last week by François Le Villard. As you probably know, he has come rather to the front lately in the French detective service. He has all the Celtic power of quick intuition. But he is lacking in the wide range of exact knowledge which is essential to the higher developments of his art. The case was concerned with a will, and had some features of interest. I was able to refer him to two parallel cases. One was at Riga in 1857, and the other at St. Louis in 1871. They have suggested to him the true solution. Here is the letter which I had this morning acknowledging my assistance." As he spoke, he tossed over a crumpled sheet of foreign notepaper. I glanced my eyes down it. I caught many notes of praise, with stray "magnifiques," "coup-de-maîtres," and "tours-de-force." All testified to the ardent praise of the Frenchman.

"He speaks as a pupil to his master," said I.

"Oh, he rates my assistance too highly," said Sherlock Holmes, lightly. "He has considerable gifts himself. He possesses two out of the three qualities necessary for the ideal detective. He has the power of observation and that of deduction. He is only wanting in knowledge. And that may come in time. He is now translating my small works into French."

"Your works?"

“Oh, did you not know?” he cried, laughing. “Yes, I have written several papers on special subjects. Here, for example, is one called ‘On the Difference between the Ashes of Various Tobaccos.’ In it I list a hundred and forty kinds of cigar, cigarette, and pipe tobacco. There are coloured plates showing the difference in the ash. This point keeps coming up in criminal trials. Sometimes it is very important as a clue. For example, if you can say for sure that a murder was done by a man who was smoking an Indian lunkah, it clearly narrows your search. To a trained eye, the black ash of a Trichinopoly and the white fluff of bird’s-eye are as different as a cabbage and a potato.”

“You have an extraordinary genius for small details,” I remarked.

“I appreciate their importance. Here is my paper on tracing footsteps, with some remarks on using plaster of Paris to preserve impressions. Here, too, is a curious little work on how a trade affects the shape of the hand. It has lithotypes of the hands of slaters, sailors, corkcutters, compositors, weavers, and diamond-polishers. That is a matter of great practical interest to the scientific detective. It is especially useful in cases of unclaimed bodies, or in discovering the past of criminals. But I weary you with my hobby.”

“Not at all,” I answered, earnestly. “It interests me greatly. This is especially true since I have had the chance to observe your practical use of it. But you spoke just now of observation and deduction. Surely one implies the other to some extent.”

“Why, hardly,” he answered. He leaned back luxuriously in his arm-chair and sent up thick blue wreaths from his pipe. “For example, observation shows me that you have been to the Wigmore Street Post-Office this morning. But deduction lets me know that you sent a telegram while you were there.”

“Right!” said I. “Right on both points! But I confess that I do not see how you arrived at it. It was a sudden impulse on my part, and I have mentioned it to no one.”

“It is simplicity itself,” he remarked, chuckling at my surprise. “It is so absurdly simple that an explanation is superfluous. And yet it may help to define the limits of observation and of deduction. Observation tells me that you have a little reddish mould sticking to your instep. Just opposite the Wigmore Street Office they have taken up the pavement and thrown up some earth. It lies in such a way that it is difficult to avoid stepping in it when entering. The earth is of this peculiar reddish tint. As far as I know, it is found nowhere else in the neighbourhood. So much is observation. The rest is deduction.”

“How, then, did you deduce the telegram?”

“Why, of course I knew that you had not written a letter, since I sat opposite to you all morning. I also see in your open desk that you have a sheet of stamps and a thick bundle of post-cards. What could you go into the post-office for, then, but to send a wire? Remove all other factors, and the one that remains must be the truth.”

“In this case it certainly is so,” I replied, after a little thought. “The thing, however, is, as you say, of the simplest. Would you think me rude if I were to put your theories to a more severe test?”

“On the contrary,” he answered, “it would prevent me from taking a second dose of cocaine. I should be delighted to look into any problem which you might give me.”

“I have heard you say that it is difficult for a man to have any object in daily use without leaving the mark of his individuality on it. A trained observer might read that mark. Now, I have here a watch which has recently come into my possession. Would you be so kind as to give me your opinion about the character or habits of the late owner?”

I handed him the watch with some slight amusement in my heart. The test was, as I thought, an impossible one. I intended it as a lesson against the somewhat dogmatic tone he sometimes used. He balanced the watch in his hand, stared hard at the dial, opened the back, and examined the works. First he used his naked eyes, and then a powerful convex lens. I could hardly keep from smiling at his disappointed face when he finally snapped the case shut and handed it back.

“There are hardly any data,” he remarked. “The watch has been recently cleaned, which robs me of my most suggestive facts.”

“You are right,” I answered. “It was cleaned before being sent to me.” In my heart I accused my companion of making a very weak excuse to cover his failure. What data could he expect from an uncleaned watch?

“Though unsatisfactory, my research has not been entirely barren,” he observed. He stared up at the ceiling with dreamy, dull eyes. “Subject to your correction, I should judge that the watch belonged to your elder brother. He inherited it from your father.”

“You gather that, no doubt, from the H. W. on the back?”

“Quite so. The W. suggests your own name. The date of the watch is nearly fifty years back, and the initials are as old as the watch. So it was made for the last generation. Jewelry usually passes down to the eldest son, and he is most likely to have the same name as the father. Your father has, if I remember right, been dead many years. It has, therefore, been in the hands of your eldest brother.”

“Right, so far,” said I. “Anything else?”

“He was a man of untidy habits. He was very untidy and careless. He was left with good prospects, but he threw away his chances. He lived for some time in poverty, with occasional short intervals of prosperity. Finally he took to drink, and he died. That is all I can gather.”

I sprang from my chair and limped impatiently about the room with considerable bitterness in my heart.

“This is unworthy of you, Holmes,” I said. “I could not have believed that you would have descended to this. You have made inquiries into the history of my unhappy brother. Now you pretend to deduce this knowledge in some fanciful way. You cannot expect me to believe that you have read all this from his old watch! It is unkind, and, to speak plainly, it has a touch of charlatanism in it.”

“My dear doctor,” said he, kindly, “please accept my apologies. Viewing the matter as an abstract problem, I had forgotten how personal and painful it might be to you. I assure you, however, that I never even knew that you had a brother until you handed me the watch.”

“Then how in the name of all that is wonderful did you get these facts? They are absolutely correct in every particular.”

"Ah, that is good luck. I could only say what was most likely. I did not expect to be so exact."

"But it was not just a guess?"

"No, no. I never guess. It is a bad habit. It hurts clear thinking. Things seem strange to you only because you do not follow my thoughts. You do not watch the small facts that lead to big conclusions. For example, I began by saying that your brother was not careful. Look at the lower part of that watch-case. You will see it is not only bent in two places. It is also cut and marked all over. That comes from keeping other hard objects, like coins or keys, in the same pocket. It is no great thing to suppose that a man who treats a fifty-guinea watch so roughly must be a careless man. And it is not a far guess that a man who owns one thing of such value is fairly well provided for in other ways."

I nodded, to show that I followed his reasoning.

"In England, pawnbrokers usually scratch the ticket number with a pin-point inside the case when they take a watch. It is easier than a label. There is no risk of the number being lost or moved. I can see no less than four such numbers inside this case with my lens. So your brother was often short of money. And he also had times of good fortune. If not, he could not have bought the watch back. Finally, look at the inner plate, which holds the key-hole. Look at the thousands of marks all round the hole. They are marks where the key has slipped. What sober man's key could have cut those lines? But you will never see a drunkard's watch without them. He winds it at night, and he leaves these marks of his unsteady hand. Where is the mystery in all this?"

"It is as clear as daylight," I answered. "I am sorry for the wrong I did you. I should have had more faith in your wonderful power. May I ask whether you have any professional case at present?"

"None. That is why I take cocaine. I cannot live without brain-work. What else is there to live for? Stand at the window here. Was there ever such a dull, sad, useless world? See how the yellow fog moves down the street and drifts across the brown houses. What could be more flat and material? What is the use of having powers, doctor, when one has no field in which to use them? Crime is ordinary, existence is ordinary, and nothing but ordinary qualities has any purpose on earth."

I had opened my mouth to reply to this speech when our landlady entered with a crisp knock. She carried a card on a brass tray.

"A young lady for you, sir," she said to my companion.

"Miss Mary Morstan," he read. "Hum! I do not remember the name. Ask the young lady to come up, Mrs. Hudson. Don't go, doctor. I would prefer that you stay."

CHAPTER 2. The Statement of the Case

The Statement of the Case

Miss Morstan entered the room with a firm step. Her manner seemed calm on the outside. She was a blonde young lady. She was small and dainty. She wore well gloved hands and dressed in the most perfect taste. However, there was a plainness and simplicity about her costume. It suggested limited means. The dress was a sombre greyish beige. It was untrimmed and unbraided. She wore a small turban of the same dull hue. Only a suspicion of white feather in the side relieved it. Her face had neither regularity of feature nor beauty of complexion. But her expression was sweet and amiable. Her large blue eyes were singularly spiritual and sympathetic. I have known women over many nations and three separate continents. I have never looked upon a face which gave a clearer promise of a refined and sensitive nature. I could not but observe one thing. She took the seat which Sherlock Holmes placed for her. Her lip trembled. Her hand quivered. She showed every sign of intense inward agitation.

“I have come to you, Mr. Holmes,” she said. “You once enabled my employer, Mrs. Cecil Forrester, to unravel a little domestic complication. She was much impressed by your kindness and skill.”

“Mrs. Cecil Forrester,” he repeated thoughtfully. “I believe that I was of some slight service to her. The case, however, as I remember it, was a very simple one.”

“She did not think so. But at least you cannot say the same of mine. I can hardly imagine anything more strange, more utterly inexplicable, than the situation in which I find myself.”

Holmes rubbed his hands. His eyes glistened. He leaned forward in his chair. His clear-cut, hawklike features showed an expression of extraordinary concentration. “State your case,” said he, in brisk, business tones.

I felt that my position was an embarrassing one. “You will, I am sure, excuse me,” I said, rising from my chair.

To my surprise, the young lady held up her gloved hand to detain me. “If your friend,” she said, “would be good enough to stop, he might be of inestimable service to me.”

I relapsed into my chair.

“Briefly,” she continued, “the facts are these. My father was an officer in an Indian regiment. He sent me home when I was quite a child. My mother was dead. I had no relative in England. I was placed, however, in a comfortable boarding establishment at Edinburgh. There I remained until I was seventeen years of age. In the year 1878 my father was senior captain of his regiment. He obtained twelve months’ leave and came home. He telegraphed to me from London that he had arrived all safe. He directed me to come down at once. He gave the Langham Hotel as his address. His message, as I remember, was full of kindness and love. On reaching London I drove to the Langham. I was informed that Captain Morstan was staying there. But he had gone out the night before and had not yet returned. I waited all day without news of him. That night, on the advice of the manager of the hotel, I communicated with the police. Next morning we advertised in all the papers. Our inquiries led to no result. From that day to this no word has ever been heard of my unfortunate father. He came home with his heart full of hope. He came to find some peace, some comfort, and instead—” She put her hand to her throat. A choking sob cut short the sentence.

“The date?” asked Holmes, opening his note-book.

“He disappeared upon the 3rd of December, 1878,—nearly ten years ago.”

“His luggage?”

“Remained at the hotel. There was nothing in it to suggest a clue. There were some clothes, some books, and a considerable number of curiosities from the Andaman Islands. He had been one of the officers in charge of the convict-guard there.”

“Had he any friends in town?”

“Only one that we know of. Major Sholto, of his own regiment, the 34th Bombay Infantry. The major had retired some little time before. He lived at Upper Norwood. We communicated with him, of course. But he did not even know that his brother officer was in England.”

“A singular case,” remarked Holmes.

“I have not yet described to you the most singular part. About six years ago—to be exact, upon the 4th of May, 1882—an advertisement appeared in the _Times_. It asked for the address of Miss Mary Morstan. It stated that it would be to her advantage to come forward. There was no name or address appended. I had at that time just entered the family of Mrs. Cecil Forrester in the capacity of governess. By her advice I published my address in the advertisement column. The same day there arrived through the post a small card-board box addressed to me. I found it to contain a very large and lustrous pearl. No word of writing was enclosed. Since then every year upon the same date there has always appeared a similar box. It contained a similar pearl, without any clue as to the sender. An expert has pronounced them to be of a rare variety and of considerable value. You can see for yourselves that they are very handsome.” She opened a flat box as she spoke. She showed me six of the finest pearls that I had ever seen.

“Your statement is most interesting,” said Sherlock Holmes. “Has anything else occurred to you?”

“Yes, and no later than to-day. That is why I have come to you. This morning I received this letter. You will perhaps read it for yourself.”

“Thank you,” said Holmes. “The envelope too, please. Postmark, London, S.W. Date, July 7. Hum! Man’s thumb-mark on corner,—probably postman. Best quality paper. Envelopes at sixpence a packet. Particular man in his stationery. No address. ‘Be at the third pillar from the left outside the Lyceum Theatre to-night at seven o’clock. If you are distrustful, bring two friends. You are a wronged woman, and shall have justice. Do not bring police. If you do, all will be in vain. Your unknown friend.’ Well, really, this is a very pretty little mystery. What do you intend to do, Miss Morstan?”

“That is exactly what I want to ask you.”

“Then we shall most certainly go. You and I and—yes, why, Dr. Watson is the very man. Your correspondent says two friends. He and I have worked together before.”

“But would he come?” she asked, with something appealing in her voice and expression.

“I should be proud and happy,” said I, fervently, “if I can be of any service.”

“You are both very kind,” she answered. “I have led a retired life. I have no friends whom I could appeal to. If I am here at six it will do, I suppose?”

“You must not be later,” said Holmes. “There is one other point, however. Is this handwriting the same as that upon the pearl-box addresses?”

“I have them here,” she answered, producing half a dozen pieces of paper.

“You are certainly a model client. You have the correct feeling. Let us see, now.” He spread out the papers upon the table. He gave quick little looks from one to the other. “They are disguised hands, except the letter,” he said after a moment. “But there can be no question about who wrote them. See how the Greek _e_ always breaks out. And see the curl of the final _s_. They are certainly by the same person. I do not want to suggest false hopes, Miss Morstan, but is there any similarity between this hand and that of your father?”

“Nothing could be more different.”

“I expected to hear you say so. We shall look out for you, then, at six. Please allow me to keep the papers. I may look into the matter before then. It is only half-past three. _Au revoir_, then.”

“_Au revoir_,” said our visitor. With a bright, kind look from one to the other of us, she put her pearl-box back in her chest and hurried away. Standing at the window, I watched her walking quickly down the street. I watched until the grey turban and white feather were only a small spot in the dark crowd.

“What a very attractive woman!” I exclaimed, turning to my companion.

He had lit his pipe again. He was leaning back with heavy eyelids. “Is she?” he said slowly. “I did not notice.”

“You really are a machine,—a calculating-machine!” I cried. “There is something truly inhuman in you at times.”

He smiled gently. “It is of the first importance,” he said, “not to let your judgment be changed by personal qualities. A client is to me only a unit,—a factor in a problem. Feelings are opposed to clear reasoning. I assure you that the most winning woman I ever knew was hanged for poisoning three little children for their insurance-money. And the most repellant man I know is a philanthropist who has spent nearly a quarter of a million upon the London poor.”

“In this case, however—”

“I never make exceptions. An exception disproves the rule. Have you ever had occasion to study character in handwriting? What do you make of this man’s poor writing?”

“It is clear and regular,” I answered. “A man of business habits and some force of character.”

Holmes shook his head. “Look at his long letters,” he said. “They hardly rise above the common level. That _d_ might be an _a_, and that _l_ an _e_. Men of character always make their long letters different, however badly they may write. There is hesitation in his _k_’s and self-esteem in his capitals. I am going out now. I have some few references to make. Let me recommend this book,—one of the most remarkable ever written. It is Winwood Reade’s ‘Martyrdom of Man.’ I shall be back in an hour.”

I sat in the window with the book in my hand. But my thoughts were far from the writer’s bold ideas. My mind ran upon our late visitor. I thought of her smiles, the deep rich tones of her voice, the strange mystery which hung over her life. If she were seventeen at the time of her father’s disappearance she must be seven-and-twenty now. That is a sweet age, when youth has lost its self-consciousness and become a little sobered by experience. So I sat and thought, until such dangerous thoughts came into my head that I hurried away to my desk. I threw myself furiously into the latest treatise upon pathology. What was I, an army surgeon with a weak leg and a weaker banking-account, that I should dare to think of such things? She was a unit, a factor,—nothing more. If my future were black, it was better surely to face it like a man than to attempt to brighten it by mere false hopes of the imagination.

CHAPTER 3. In Quest of a Solution

In Quest of a Solution

It was half-past five before Holmes returned. He was bright, eager, and in excellent spirits. This mood, in his case, was one moment high and the next moment very low.

“There is no great mystery in this matter,” he said. He took the cup of tea which I had poured out for him. “The facts seem to allow only one explanation.”

“What! You have solved it already?”

“Well, that would be too much to say. I have found one important fact, that is all. It is, however, very important. The details are still to be added. I have just found, by looking at old copies of the Times, that Major Sholto, of Upper Norwood, late of the 34th Bombay Infantry, died on the 28th of April, 1882.”

“I may be very slow, Holmes, but I fail to see what this suggests.”

“No? You surprise me. Look at it in this way, then. Captain Morstan disappears. The only person in London whom he could have visited is Major Sholto. Major Sholto says he did not hear that Morstan was in London. Four years later Sholto dies. Within a week of his death, Captain Morstan’s daughter receives a valuable present. The present is repeated from year to year. Now it ends in a letter which describes her as a wronged woman. What wrong can it mean except this loss of her father? And why should the presents begin right after Sholto’s death? It must be that Sholto’s heir knows something of the mystery and wants to make up for it. Do you have any other idea which will fit the facts?”

“But what a strange way to make up for it! And how strangely it is done! Why, too, should he write a letter now, rather than six years ago? Again, the letter speaks of giving her justice. What justice can she have? It is too much to suppose that her father is still alive. There is no other wrong in her case that you know of.”

“There are difficulties; there are certainly difficulties,” said Sherlock Holmes, thoughtfully. “But our trip tonight will solve them all. Ah, here is a four-wheeler, and Miss Morstan is inside. Are you all ready? Then we had better go down, for it is a little past the hour.”

I picked up my hat and my heaviest stick. But I noticed that Holmes took his revolver from his drawer and slipped it into his pocket. It was clear that he thought our night’s work might be a serious one.

Miss Morstan was wrapped in a dark cloak. Her sensitive face was calm, but pale. She must have been more than a woman if she did not feel some worry at the strange business we were beginning. Yet her self-control was perfect. She readily answered the few additional questions which Sherlock Holmes put to her.

“Major Sholto was a very close friend of my father’s,” she said. “His letters were full of mentions of the major. He and my father were in command of the troops at the Andaman Islands, so they were thrown a great deal together. By the way, a curious paper was found in my father’s desk which no one could understand. I do not suppose that it is of the slightest importance. But I thought you might care to see it, so I brought it with me. It is here.”

Holmes opened the paper carefully and smoothed it out upon his knee. He then very carefully examined it all over with his double lens.

“It is paper of native Indian manufacture,” he remarked. “It has at some time been pinned to a board. The drawing on it appears to be a plan of part of a large building with many halls, corridors, and passages. At one point is a small cross done in red ink. Above it is ‘3.37 from left,’ in faded pencil-writing. In the left-hand corner is a curious sign like four crosses in a line with their arms touching. Beside it is written, in very rough and rough characters, ‘The sign of the four,—Jonathan Small, Mahomet Singh, Abdullah Khan, Dost Akbar.’ No, I confess that I do not see how this bears upon the matter. Yet it is clearly an important document. It has been kept carefully in a pocket-book, for the one side is as clean as the other.”

“It was in his pocket-book that we found it.”

“Keep it carefully, then, Miss Morstan, for it may prove to be of use to us. I begin to suspect that this matter may turn out to be much deeper and more subtle than I at first supposed. I must reconsider my ideas.” He leaned back in the cab. I could see by his drawn brow and his empty eye that he was thinking hard. Miss Morstan and I talked in a low voice about our present trip and its possible outcome. But our companion kept his deep reserve until the end of our journey.

It was a September evening, and not yet seven o’clock. But the day had been a dreary one, and a dense drizzly fog lay low upon the great city. Mud-coloured clouds hung sadly over the muddy streets. Down the Strand the lamps were only misty spots of spread light which threw a weak round glimmer upon the wet pavement. The yellow glare from the shop-windows streamed out into the steamy, vaporous air. It threw a murky, shifting radiance across the crowded street. To my mind, there was something eerie and ghost-like in the endless line of faces which moved across these narrow bars of light. Sad faces and glad, tired and merry. Like all human kind, they moved from the dark into the light, and so back into the dark once more. I am not subject to impressions, but the dull, heavy evening, with the strange business upon which we were engaged, combined to make me nervous and depressed. I could see from Miss Morstan’s manner that she was suffering from the same feeling. Holmes alone could rise above small influences. He held his open note-book upon his knee. From time to time he jotted down figures and notes in the light of his pocket-lantern.

At the Lyceum Theatre the crowds were already thick at the side-entrances. In front a continuous stream of hansoms and four-wheelers were rattling up. They discharged their loads of shirt-fronted men and beshawled, bediamonded women. We had hardly reached the third pillar, which was our meeting place, before a small, dark, brisk man in the dress of a coachman spoke to us.

“Are you the parties who come with Miss Morstan?” he asked.

“I am Miss Morstan, and these two gentlemen are my friends,” said she.

He bent a pair of wonderfully sharp and questioning eyes upon us. “You will excuse me, miss,” he said with a certain stubborn manner, “but I was to ask you to give me your word that neither of your companions is a police-officer.”

“I give you my word on that,” she answered.

He gave a high whistle. A street boy led a four-wheeled cab across and opened the door. The man who had spoken to us climbed up to the driver's seat. We got in. The driver whipped his horse, and we moved away very fast through the streets, which were full of thick cloud.

Our situation was strange. We were going to a place we did not know, for a reason we did not know. But our invitation was either a complete trick — which was impossible to believe — or we had good reason to think that important things might depend on our journey. Miss Morstan's manner was as firm and calm as ever. I tried to make her happy and interested by telling her memories of my adventures in Afghanistan. But, to tell the truth, I was so excited about our situation and so curious about our destination that my stories were a little confused. To this day she says that I told her one moving story about how a gun looked into my tent late at night, and how I fired a double-barrelled gun at a young tiger. At first I had some idea of the direction we were going. But soon, because of our speed, the thick cloud, and my own small knowledge of London, I lost my sense of direction. I knew nothing except that we seemed to be going a very long way. Sherlock Holmes was never wrong, however. He said the names quietly as the cab moved through squares and in and out by winding side streets.

"Rochester Row," he said. "Now Vincent Square. Now we come out on the Vauxhall Bridge Road. We are going toward the Surrey side, it seems. Yes, I thought so. Now we are on the bridge. You can see quick views of the river."

We did indeed get a quick view of a stretch of the Thames, with the lamps shining on the wide, silent water. But our cab hurried on, and was soon lost in a maze of streets on the other side.

"Wordsworth Road," said my companion. "Priory Road. Lark Hall Lane. Stockwell Place. Robert Street. Cold Harbor Lane. Our search does not seem to take us to very rich areas."

We had indeed reached a poor and unwelcoming area. Long lines of dull brick houses were only broken by the rough light and cheap show of public houses at the corner. Then came rows of two-storied villas, each with a small garden in front. Then again came endless lines of new, ugly brick buildings — the huge arms which the giant city was throwing out into the country. At last the cab stopped at the third house in a new row. None of the other houses were lived in. The one where we stopped was as dark as its neighbours, except for a single weak light in the kitchen window. When we knocked, however, the door was opened at once by a Hindoo servant. He wore a yellow turban, loose white clothes, and a yellow sash. There was something very strange and out of place in this Eastern figure, standing in the ordinary doorway of a third-rate house outside the city.

"The Sahib is waiting for you," he said. Even as he spoke, a high, piping voice came from some room inside. "Show them in to me, khitmutgar," it cried. "Show them straight in to me."

CHAPTER 4. The Story of the Bald-Headed Man

The Story of the Bald-Headed Man

We followed the Indian down a dirty and ordinary passage. It was poorly lit and poorly furnished. At last he came to a door on the right and opened it wide. A bright yellow light poured out at us. In the middle of the light stood a small man with a very high head. Red hair stood up all around the edge of it. His bare, shining head rose out of the hair like a mountain top out of fir trees. He twisted his hands together as he stood. His face moved all the time, never still for a moment. One moment he smiled, the next he frowned. Nature had given him a hanging lip and a very visible row of yellow, uneven teeth. He tried weakly to hide them by passing his hand over the lower part of his face again and again. Even though his bare head drew the eye, he still seemed young. In fact, he had just turned thirty.

“At your service, Miss Morstan,” he kept repeating in a thin, high voice. “At your service, gentlemen. Please step into my little private room. A small place, miss, but furnished to my own taste. A green spot of art in the wild desert of South London.”

We were all surprised by the look of the room he invited us into. In that poor house it seemed as out of place as a perfect diamond in a brass ring. The richest, shiniest curtains and wall hangings covered the walls. They were pulled back here and there to show a painting in a rich frame or an Eastern vase. The carpet was amber and black. It was so soft and thick that the foot sank into it pleasantly, as into a bed of moss. Two large tiger skins lay across it and added to the feeling of Eastern luxury. So did a huge hookah standing on a mat in the corner. A lamp shaped like a silver dove hung from a golden wire in the middle of the room. The wire was almost invisible. As the lamp burned, it filled the air with a faint, sweet smell.

“Mr. Thaddeus Sholto,” said the little man, still twitching and smiling. “That is my name. You are Miss Morstan, of course. And these gentlemen—”

“This is Mr. Sherlock Holmes, and this is Dr. Watson.”

“A doctor, eh?” he cried, very excited. “Have you your stethoscope? May I ask you—would you be so kind? I have serious doubts about my mitral valve, if you would be so very good. I may trust the aortic, but I should value your opinion on the mitral.”

I listened to his heart, as he asked. But I could find nothing wrong, except that he was in a state of great fear, for he shook from head to foot. “It appears to be normal,” I said. “You have no cause for worry.”

“You will excuse my anxiety, Miss Morstan,” he remarked lightly. “I am a great sufferer, and I have long had doubts about that valve. I am delighted to hear that they are without cause. Had your father, Miss Morstan, held back from putting a strain on his heart, he might be alive now.”

I could have struck the man across the face. I was so angry at this cold and careless mention of so sensitive a matter. Miss Morstan sat down, and her face grew white to the lips. “I knew in my heart that he was dead,” she said.

“I can give you all the information,” said he. “And what is more, I can do you justice. And I will, too, whatever Brother Bartholomew may say. I am so glad to have your friends here. They are not only an escort for you, but also witnesses to what I am about to do and say. The three of us can show a bold front to Brother Bartholomew. But let us have no outsiders—no police or officials. We can settle everything satisfactorily among ourselves, without any interference. Nothing would annoy Brother Bartholomew more than any publicity.” He sat down on a low settee. He blinked at us with his weak, watery blue eyes, as if asking a question.

“For my part,” said Holmes, “whatever you may choose to say will go no further.”

I nodded to show my agreement.

“That is well! That is well!” said he. “May I offer you a glass of Chianti, Miss Morstan? Or of Tokay? I keep no other wines. Shall I open a bottle? No? Well, then, I trust that you have no objection to tobacco smoke, to the mild, sweet smell of the Eastern tobacco. I am a little nervous, and I find my hookah a most valuable calmer.” He put a taper to the great bowl, and the smoke bubbled merrily through the rose water. We sat all three in a half circle, with our heads forward and our chins on our hands. The strange little twitching fellow, with his high, shining head, puffed uneasily in the middle.

“When I first decided to make this communication to you,” said he, “I might have given you my address. But I feared that you might ignore my request and bring unpleasant people with you. I took the liberty, therefore, of making an appointment in such a way that my man Williams might see you first. I have complete confidence in his good judgment. He had orders, if he were not satisfied, to go no further in the matter. You will excuse these precautions. But I am a man of somewhat quiet, and I might even say refined, tastes. And there is nothing less beautiful than a policeman. I naturally shrink from all forms of rough materialism. I seldom come in contact with the rough crowd. I live, as you see, with a little atmosphere of elegance around me. I may call myself a lover of the arts. It is my weakness. The landscape is a genuine Corot. And though an expert might perhaps doubt that Salvator Rosa, there can be no question at all about the Bouguereau. I am fond of the modern French school.”

“You will excuse me, Mr. Sholto,” said Miss Morstan, “but I am here at your request to learn something which you wish to tell me. It is very late, and I should like the meeting to be as short as possible.”

“At best it must take some time,” he answered, “for we shall certainly have to go to Norwood and see Brother Bartholomew. We shall all go and try if we can get the better of Brother Bartholomew. He is very angry with me for taking the course which has seemed right to me. I had quite sharp words with him last night. You cannot imagine what a terrible fellow he is when he is angry.”

“If we are to go to Norwood, it would perhaps be as well to start at once,” I ventured to remark.

He laughed until his ears were quite red. "That would hardly do," he cried. "I do not know what he would say if I brought you in that sudden way. No, I must prepare you by showing you how we all stand to each other. First, I must tell you that there are several points in the story that I myself do not know. I can only lay the facts before you as far as I know them myself.

"My father was, as you may have guessed, Major John Sholto, once of the Indian army. He retired some eleven years ago. He came to live at Pondicherry Lodge in Upper Norwood. He had done well in India. He brought back with him a large sum of money, a large collection of valuable curiosities, and a staff of native servants. With these advantages he bought himself a house. He lived in great luxury. My twin-brother Bartholomew and I were the only children.

"I very well remember the feeling caused by the disappearance of Captain Morstan. We read the details in the papers. We knew that he had been a friend of our father. We discussed the case openly in his presence. He used to join in our guesses about what could have happened. We never for a moment suspected that he had the whole secret hidden in his own heart. Of all men, he alone knew the fate of Arthur Morstan.

"We did know, however, that some mystery—some real danger—hung over our father. He was very afraid of going out alone. He always employed two prize-fighters to act as porters at Pondicherry Lodge. Williams, who drove you tonight, was one of them. He was once light-weight champion of England. Our father would never tell us what he feared. But he had a most marked dislike of men with wooden legs. On one occasion he actually fired his revolver at a wooden-legged man. The man proved to be a harmless tradesman asking for orders. We had to pay a large sum to keep the matter quiet. My brother and I used to think this was just a whim of my father's. But events have since led us to change our opinion.

"Early in 1882 my father received a letter from India. It was a great shock to him. He nearly fainted at the breakfast-table when he opened it. From that day he grew sicker and sicker until he died. We could never discover what was in the letter. But I could see as he held it that it was short and written in a rough hand. He had suffered for years from a large spleen. But now he became quickly worse. Towards the end of April we were told that he was beyond all hope. He wished to make a last communication to us.

"When we entered his room he was propped up with pillows and breathing heavily. He begged us to lock the door and to come upon either side of the bed. Then he grasped our hands. He made a remarkable statement to us, in a voice broken as much by feeling as by pain. I shall try and give it to you in his own very words.

"'I have only one thing,' he said, 'which weighs upon my mind at this supreme moment. It is my treatment of poor Morstan's orphan. The cursed greed which has been my besetting sin through life has kept from her the treasure. At least half of it should have been hers. And yet I have made no use of it myself. So blind and foolish a thing is avarice. The mere feeling of possession has been so dear to me that I could not bear to share it with another. See that chaplet dipped with pearls beside the quinine-bottle. Even that I could not bear to part with, although I had got it out with the design of sending it to her. You, my sons, will give her a fair share of the Agra treasure. But send her nothing—not even the chaplet—until I am gone. After all, men have been as bad as this and have recovered.

"'I will tell you how Morstan died,' he continued. 'He had suffered for years from a weak heart, but he concealed it from every one. I alone knew it. When in India, he and I, through a remarkable chain of circumstances, came into possession of a considerable treasure. I brought it over to England. On the night of Morstan's arrival he came straight over here to claim his share. He walked over from the station. He was let in by my faithful old Lal Chowdar, who is now dead. Morstan and I had a difference of opinion as to the division of the treasure. We came to heated words. Morstan had sprung out of his chair in a paroxysm of anger. Then he suddenly pressed his hand to his side. His face turned a dusky hue. He fell backwards, cutting his head against the corner of the treasure-chest. When I stooped over him I found, to my horror, that he was dead.

"'For a long time I sat half distracted, wondering what I should do. My first impulse was, of course, to call for assistance. But I could not but recognise that there was every chance that I would be accused of his murder. His death at the moment of a quarrel, and the gash in his head, would be black against me. Again, an official inquiry could not be made without bringing out some facts about the treasure. I was particularly anxious to keep these secret. He had told me that no soul upon earth knew where he had gone. There seemed to be no necessity why any soul ever should know.

"'I was still pondering over the matter when I looked up. I saw my servant, Lal Chowdar, in the doorway. He stole in and bolted the door behind him. "Do not fear, Sahib," he said. "No one need know that you have killed him. Let us hide him away, and who is the wiser?" "I did not kill him," said I. Lal Chowdar shook his head and smiled. "I heard it all, Sahib," said he. "I heard you quarrel, and I heard the blow. But my lips are sealed. All are asleep in the house. Let us put him away together." That was enough to decide me. If my own servant could not believe my innocence, how could I hope to make it good before twelve foolish tradesmen in a jury-box? Lal Chowdar and I disposed of the body that night. Within a few days the London papers were full of the mysterious disappearance of Captain Morstan. You will see from what I say that I can hardly be blamed in the matter. My fault lies in the fact that we concealed not only the body, but also the treasure. And I have clung to Morstan's share as well as to my own. I wish you, therefore, to make restitution. Put your ears down to my mouth. The treasure is hidden in—'"

“At this moment a horrible change came over his face. His eyes stared wide open. His jaw dropped. He yelled in a voice I can never forget: ‘Keep him out! For Christ’s sake keep him out!’ We both looked round at the window behind us. His eyes were fixed on it. A face was looking in at us from the darkness. We could see the nose turning white where it pressed against the glass. It was a face with a beard and lots of hair. It had wild, cruel eyes and a look of deep hate. My brother and I rushed to the window, but the man was gone. When we went back to my father, his head had fallen and his heart had stopped beating.

“We searched the garden that night. We found no sign of the man who had come in, except for one footmark just under the window in the flower-bed. Without that one mark, we might have thought our minds had made up that wild, fierce face. But soon we had another and stronger proof that secret forces were at work all around us. In the morning, the window of my father’s room was found open. His cupboards and boxes had been searched. On his chest was a torn piece of paper with the words ‘The sign of the four’ written across it in rough letters. We never knew what the words meant, or who our secret visitor was. As far as we can tell, none of my father’s property had really been stolen, though everything had been turned out. My brother and I naturally connected this strange event with the fear that stayed with my father all his life. But it is still a complete mystery to us.”

The little man stopped to light his pipe again and smoked slowly for a few moments. We had all sat still, listening to his strange story. When she heard the short account of her father’s death, Miss Morstan had turned very white. For a moment I feared she was about to faint. She recovered, however, after drinking a glass of water which I quietly poured for her from a Venetian glass bottle on the side-table. Sherlock Holmes leaned back in his chair with a far-away look. His eyelids were low over his bright eyes. As I looked at him, I could not help thinking how that very day he had complained bitterly that life was too ordinary. Here at least was a problem that would test his wisdom to the utmost. Mr. Thaddeus Sholto looked from one of us to the other. He was clearly proud of the effect his story had made. Then he went on between puffs of his very large pipe.

“My brother and I,” he said, “were, as you may imagine, very excited about the treasure my father had spoken of. For weeks and months we dug and searched in every part of the garden, without finding where it was. It was maddening to think that the hiding-place was on his very lips at the moment he died. We could judge the greatness of the missing riches by the string of pearls he had taken out. My brother Bartholomew and I had some small argument over this string of pearls. The pearls were clearly of great value, and he was against parting with them. Between friends, my brother himself was a little inclined to my father’s fault. He also thought that if we parted with the pearls, it might cause gossip and finally bring us trouble. It was all I could do to persuade him to let me find out Miss Morstan’s address and send her one pearl at fixed times, so that at least she might never feel poor.”

“It was a kind thought,” said our companion, earnestly. “It was extremely good of you.”

The little man waved his hand as if to say it was nothing. “We were your trustees,” he said. “That was how I saw it, though Brother Bartholomew could not quite see it that way. We had plenty of money ourselves. I wanted no more. Besides, it would have been in such bad taste to treat a young lady in so mean a way. ‘Le mauvais goût mène au crime.’ The French have a very neat way of putting these things. Our difference of opinion on this subject went so far that I thought it best to set up rooms for myself. So I left Pondicherry Lodge, taking the old khitmutgar and Williams with me. Yesterday, however, I learned that a very important event has occurred. The treasure has been found. I at once communicated with Miss Morstan, and now we only need to drive out to Norwood and demand our share. I explained my views last night to Brother Bartholomew, so we shall be expected visitors, if not welcome ones.”

Mr. Thaddeus Sholto stopped and sat moving nervously on his comfortable settee. We all remained silent, thinking about the new development in this mysterious business. Holmes was the first to jump to his feet.

“You have done well, sir, from first to last,” said he. “It is possible that we may be able to make you some small return by throwing some light on what is still dark to you. But, as Miss Morstan remarked just now, it is late, and we had best deal with the matter without delay.”

Our new acquaintance very slowly rolled up the tube of his pipe. From behind a curtain he produced a very long coat with fancy buttons, an Astrakhan collar and cuffs. He buttoned it tightly, even though the night was extremely warm. He finished his dress by putting on a rabbit-skin cap with hanging flaps that covered his ears, so that no part of him was visible except his lively, pointed face. “My health is somewhat fragile,” he remarked, as he led the way down the passage. “I am forced to be a valetudinarian.”

Our cab was waiting for us outside. Our plan was clearly arranged beforehand, for the driver started off at once at a fast pace. Thaddeus Sholto talked without stopping, in a voice that rose high above the rattle of the wheels.

“Bartholomew is a smart fellow,” he said. “How do you think he found out where the treasure was? He had decided it was somewhere inside the house. So he measured all the space in the house. He measured everywhere so that not one small part was missed. Among other things, he found that the building was seventy-four feet high. But he added together the heights of all the separate rooms. He also allowed for the space between them, which he checked by making holes. He could not make the total more than seventy feet. Four feet were not explained. These could only be at the top of the building. So he knocked a hole in the wood-and-plaster ceiling of the highest room. There, sure enough, he found another small room above it. It had been sealed up and no one knew about it. In the middle stood the treasure-chest, resting on two beams. He lowered it through the hole, and there it lies. He estimates the value of the jewels at not less than half a million pounds.”

At the mention of this huge sum we all stared at one another with wide eyes. Miss Morstan, if we could secure her rights, would change from a poor governess to the richest heiress in England. Surely it was the place of a true friend to be glad at such news. Yet I am ashamed to say that selfishness took hold of my soul. My heart turned as heavy as lead within me. I stammered out a few slow words of congratulation. Then I sat with my head down, my head low, deaf to the talk of our new acquaintance. He was clearly a confirmed hypochondriac. I was dreamily aware that he was pouring out endless lists of symptoms. He was begging for information about the composition and action of countless fake medicines. Some of them he carried about in a leather case in his pocket. I trust that he may not remember any of the answers I gave him that night. Holmes declares that he overheard me warn him against the great danger of taking more than two drops of castor oil. He says I recommended strychnine in large doses as a sleep medicine. However that may be, I was certainly relieved when our cab pulled up with a sudden stop and the driver jumped down to open the door.

“This, Miss Morstan, is Pondicherry Lodge,” said Mr. Thaddeus Sholto, as he handed her out.

CHAPTER 5. The Tragedy of Pondicherry Lodge

It was nearly eleven when we reached this last stage of our night's adventures. We had left the wet fog of the great city behind us. The night was fairly fine. A warm wind blew from the west. Heavy clouds moved slowly across the sky. A half moon looked out sometimes through the gaps. It was clear enough to see for some distance. But Thaddeus Sholto took one of the side lamps down from the carriage to give us a better light on our way.

Pondicherry Lodge stood in its own grounds. It was surrounded by a very high stone wall with broken glass on top. A single narrow iron door was the only way in. Our guide knocked on it with a strange postman-like rat-tat.

"Who is there?" cried a rough voice from inside.

"It is I, McMurdo. You surely know my knock by this time."

There was a grumbling sound and a clanking and shaking of keys. The door swung heavily back. A short, deep-chested man stood in the opening. The yellow light of the lamp shone on his thrust-out face and his twinkling, distrustful eyes.

"That you, Mr. Thaddeus? But who are the others? I had no orders about them from the master."

"No, McMurdo? You surprise me! I told my brother last night that I should bring some friends."

"He has not been out of his room today, Mr. Thaddeus, and I have no orders. You know very well that I must stick to rules. I can let you in, but your friends must just stop where they are."

This was an unexpected problem. Thaddeus Sholto looked around him in a confused and helpless way. "This is too bad of you, McMurdo!" he said. "If I promise for them, that is enough for you. There is the young lady, too. She cannot wait on the public road at this hour."

"Very sorry, Mr. Thaddeus," said the porter, without pity. "People may be friends of yours, and yet no friends of the master. He pays me well to do my duty, and my duty I will do. I do not know any of your friends."

"Oh, yes you do, McMurdo," cried Sherlock Holmes, warmly. "I do not think you can have forgotten me. Do you not remember the amateur who fought three rounds with you at Alison's rooms on the night of your benefit four years back?"

"Not Mr. Sherlock Holmes!" roared the prize-fighter. "God's truth! how could I have mistaken you? If instead of standing there so quiet you had just stepped up and given me that cross-hit of yours under the jaw, I would have known you without a question. Ah, you are one that has wasted your gifts, you have! You might have aimed high, if you had joined the fancy."

"You see, Watson, if all else fails me I still have one of the scientific professions open to me," said Holmes, laughing. "Our friend will not keep us out in the cold now, I am sure."

"In you come, sir, in you come, you and your friends," he answered. "Very sorry, Mr. Thaddeus, but orders are very strict. Had to be certain of your friends before I let them in."

Inside, a gravel path wound through empty grounds to a huge mass of a house. It was square and plain, all sunk in shadow except where a moonbeam struck one corner and shone in a small top window. The vast size of the building, with its gloom and its deathly silence, struck a chill to the heart. Even Thaddeus Sholto seemed uneasy. The lamp shook and rattled in his hand.

"I cannot understand it," he said. "There must be some mistake. I clearly told Bartholomew that we should be here, and yet there is no light in his window. I do not know what to make of it."

"Does he always guard the grounds in this way?" asked Holmes.

"Yes; he has followed my father's custom. He was the favourite son, you know. I sometimes think that my father may have told him more than he ever told me. That is Bartholomew's window up there where the moonshine strikes. It is quite bright, but there is no light from within, I think."

"None," said Holmes. "But I see the shine of a light in that little window beside the door."

"Ah, that is the housekeeper's room. That is where old Mrs. Bernstone sits. She can tell us all about it. But perhaps you would not mind waiting here for a minute or two. If we all go in together and she has no word of our coming, she may be alarmed. But hush! what is that?"

He held up the lamp, and his hand shook until the circles of light flickered and waved all round us. Miss Morstan seized my wrist, and we all stood with beating hearts, listening hard. From the great black house there sounded through the silent night the saddest and most pitiful of sounds: the shrill, broken crying of a frightened woman.

"It is Mrs. Bernstone," said Sholto. "She is the only woman in the house. Wait here. I shall be back in a moment." He hurried to the door, and knocked in his strange way. We could see a tall old woman let him in, and sway with pleasure at the very sight of him.

"Oh, Mr. Thaddeus, sir, I am so glad you have come! I am so glad you have come, Mr. Thaddeus, sir!" We heard her repeated rejoicings until the door was closed and her voice died away into a muffled monotone.

Our guide had left us the lamp. Holmes swung it slowly round, and looked keenly at the house, and at the great piles of rubbish which filled the grounds. Miss Morstan and I stood together, and her hand was in mine. A wondrous subtle thing is love, for here were we two who had never seen each other before that day. Between us no word or even look of affection had ever passed. And yet now in an hour of trouble our hands instinctively sought for each other. I have marvelled at it since, but at the time it seemed the most natural thing that I should go out to her so. And, as she has often told me, there was in her also the instinct to turn to me for comfort and protection. So we stood hand in hand, like two children, and there was peace in our hearts for all the dark things that surrounded us.

"What a strange place!" she said, looking round.

"It looks as though all the moles in England had been let loose in it. I have seen something of the sort on the side of a hill near Ballarat, where the prospectors had been at work."

"And from the same cause," said Holmes. "These are the traces of the treasure-seekers. You must remember that they were six years looking for it. No wonder that the grounds look like a gravel-pit."

At that moment the door of the house burst open, and Thaddeus Sholto came running out, with his hands thrown forward and terror in his eyes.

“Something is wrong with Bartholomew!” he cried. “I am afraid! My nerves cannot take it.” He was, in fact, half crying with fear. His weak, twitching face looked out from the big Astrakhan collar. It had the helpless, begging look of a terrified child.

“Come into the house,” said Holmes, in his firm, sharp way.

“Yes, do!” begged Thaddeus Sholto. “I really do not feel able to give directions.”

We all followed him into the housekeeper’s room, which was on the left side of the passage. The old woman was walking up and down with a scared look. Her fingers moved about without rest. But the sight of Miss Morstan seemed to calm her.

“God bless your sweet, calm face!” she cried, with a wild sob. “It does me good to see you. Oh, but I have been tried so hard this day!”

Our companion touched her thin, work-worn hand gently. He said a few kind, gentle words of comfort. They brought the colour back into the other woman’s pale cheeks.

“Master has locked himself in and will not answer me,” she explained. “All day I have waited to hear from him, for he often likes to be alone. But an hour ago I feared that something was wrong. So I went up and looked through the key-hole. You must go up, Mr. Thaddeus,—you must go up and look for yourself. I have seen Mr. Bartholomew Sholto in joy and in sorrow for ten long years, but I never saw him with such a face as that.”

Sherlock Holmes took the lamp and led the way, for Thaddeus Sholto’s teeth were knocking together. He was so shaken that I had to put my hand under his arm as we went up the stairs, for his knees were shaking under him. Twice as we went up, Holmes pulled his lens out of his pocket. He carefully examined marks which looked to me like mere shapeless spots of dust on the coconut matting that served as a stair-carpet. He walked slowly from step to step, holding the lamp, and throwing sharp glances to right and left. Miss Morstan had stayed behind with the frightened housekeeper.

The third flight of stairs ended in a straight passage of some length. There was a great picture in Indian tapestry on the right of it and three doors on the left. Holmes went along it in the same slow and orderly way, while we kept close behind him. Our long black shadows streamed backwards down the corridor. The third door was the one we were looking for. Holmes knocked without receiving any answer. Then he tried to turn the handle and force it open. It was locked on the inside, however, and by a broad and powerful bolt, as we could see when we put our lamp up against it. The key had been turned, but the hole was not entirely closed. Sherlock Holmes bent down to it, and at once rose again with a sharp breath.

“There is something evil in this, Watson,” said he, more moved than I had ever seen him before. “What do you make of it?”

I bent down to the hole, and pulled back in horror. Moonlight was streaming into the room, and it was bright with a vague, shifting light. Looking straight at me, and hanging, as it were, in the air, for all beneath was in shadow, there hung a face,—the very face of our companion Thaddeus. There was the same high, shining head, the same round bristle of red hair, the same pale face. The features were set, however, in a horrible smile, a fixed and unnatural grin. In that still, moonlit room it was more shocking to the nerves than any frown or twisted look. The face was so like that of our little friend that I looked round at him to make sure that he was indeed with us. Then I remembered that he had told us that his brother and he were twins.

“This is terrible!” I said to Holmes. “What is to be done?”

“The door must come down,” he answered. He jumped against it and put all his weight on the lock. It creaked and groaned, but did not yield. Together we threw ourselves upon it once more, and this time it gave way with a sudden snap. We found ourselves inside Bartholomew Sholto’s room.

It appeared to have been fitted up as a chemical laboratory. A double line of glass-stoppered bottles stood on the wall opposite the door. The table was covered with Bunsen burners, test-tubes, and retorts. In the corners stood large bottles of acid in wicker baskets. One of these appeared to leak or to have been broken, for a stream of dark-coloured liquid had trickled out from it. The air was heavy with a strangely sharp, tar-like smell. A set of steps stood at one side of the room, in the middle of a mess of lath and plaster. Above them there was an opening in the ceiling large enough for a man to pass through. At the foot of the steps a long coil of rope was thrown carelessly together.

By the table, in a wooden arm-chair, the master of the house was seated all in a heap. His head was sunk upon his left shoulder, and that ghastly, mysterious smile was upon his face. He was stiff and cold, and had clearly been dead many hours. It seemed to me that not only his features but all his limbs were twisted and turned in the most strange way. By his hand upon the table there lay a weird instrument,—a brown, close-grained stick, with a stone head like a hammer, roughly tied on with coarse twine. Beside it was a torn sheet of note-paper with some words written on it. Holmes glanced at it, and then handed it to me.

“You see,” he said, with a meaningful raising of the eyebrows.

In the light of the lantern I read, with a thrill of horror, “The sign of the four.”

“In God’s name, what does it all mean?” I asked.

“It means murder,” said he, bending over the dead man. “Ah, I expected it. Look here!” He pointed to what looked like a long, dark thorn stuck in the skin just above the ear.

“It looks like a thorn,” said I.

“It is a thorn. You may pick it out. But be careful, for it is poisoned.”

I took it up between my finger and thumb. It came away from the skin so easily that hardly any mark was left behind. One tiny spot of blood showed where the puncture had been.

“This is all a mystery I cannot solve,” said I. “It grows darker instead of clearer.”

“On the contrary,” he answered, “it clears every moment. I only need a few missing links to have a fully connected case.”

We had almost forgotten that our companion was there since we entered the room. He was still standing in the doorway. He looked very afraid. He was holding his hands together and making low sounds of pain to himself. Suddenly, however, he gave a sharp, complaining cry.

“The treasure is gone!” he said. “They have taken the treasure from him! There is the hole through which we lowered it. I helped him to do it! I was the last person who saw him! I left him here last night, and I heard him lock the door as I came down the stairs.”

“What time was that?”

“It was ten. And now he is dead, and the police will be called in, and I shall be suspected of having a part in it. Oh, yes, I am sure I shall. But you do not think so, gentlemen? Surely you do not think that it was I? Is it likely that I would have brought you here if it were I? Oh, dear! oh, dear! I know that I shall go mad!” He pulled his arms and hit his feet on the floor in a wild, uncontrolled way.

“You have no reason for fear, Mr. Sholto,” said Holmes, gently, putting his hand upon his shoulder. “Take my advice, and drive down to the station to report this matter to the police. Offer to help them in every way. We shall wait here until your return.”

The little man did as he was told in a half-confused way, and we heard him stumbling down the stairs in the dark.

CHAPTER 6. Sherlock Holmes Gives a Demonstration

“Now, Watson,” said Holmes, rubbing his hands, “we have half an hour to ourselves. Let us make good use of it. My case is, as I have told you, almost complete. But we must not be too confident. The case seems simple now, but there may be something deeper under it.”

“Simple!” I cried.

“Surely,” said he, with something of the air of a clinical professor explaining to his class. “Just sit in the corner there, so that your footprints may not make things difficult. Now to work! First of all, how did these people come, and how did they go? The door has not been opened since last night. What about the window?” He carried the lamp across to it, saying his observations in a low voice while he did so, but speaking to himself rather than to me. “Window is locked on the inner side. Frame is solid. No hinges at the side. Let us open it. No water-pipe near. Roof quite out of reach. Yet a man has climbed up by the window. It rained a little last night. Here is the print of a foot in soft earth upon the ledge. And here is a round mark of wet earth, and here again upon the floor, and here again by the table. See here, Watson! This is really a very pretty demonstration.”

I looked at the round, clear marks of wet earth. “This is not a footmark,” said I.

“It is something much more valuable to us. It is the mark of a wooden leg. You see here on the ledge is the boot-mark, a heavy boot with the broad metal heel, and beside it is the mark of the wooden toe.”

“It is the wooden-legged man.”

“Quite so. But there has been some one else,—a very able and efficient ally. Could you climb that wall, doctor?”

I looked out of the open window. The moon still shone brightly on that angle of the house. We were a good sixty feet from the ground, and, look where I would, I could see no place to stand, nor as much as a small crack in the brick-work.

“It is absolutely impossible,” I answered.

“Without aid it is so. But suppose you had a friend up here who lowered you this good strong rope which I see in the corner, tying one end of it to this great hook in the wall. Then, I think, if you were an active man, you might climb up, wooden leg and all. You would leave, of course, in the same way, and your ally would pull up the rope, untie it from the hook, shut the window, lock it on the inside, and get away in the way that he originally came. As a small point it may be noted,” he continued, touching the rope with his fingers, “that our wooden-legged friend, though a fair climber, was not a professional sailor. His hands were far from hard. My lens shows more than one blood-mark, especially towards the end of the rope. From this I gather that he slipped down with such speed that he took the skin off his hand.”

“This is all very well,” said I, “but the thing becomes more impossible to understand than ever. How about this mysterious ally? How did he come into the room?”

“Yes, the ally!” repeated Holmes, thoughtfully. “There are features of interest about this ally. He lifts the case out of the ordinary. I think that this ally breaks fresh ground in the records of crime in this country,—though similar cases suggest themselves from India, and, if my memory serves me, from Senegambia.”

“How did he come, then?” I repeated. “The door is locked, the window is impossible to reach. Was it through the chimney?”

“The fireplace is much too small,” he answered. “I had already considered that possibility.”

“How then?” I persisted.

“You will not apply my rule,” he said, shaking his head. “How often have I said to you that when you have eliminated the impossible whatever remains, _however improbable_, must be the truth? We know that he did not come through the door, the window, or the chimney. We also know that he could not have been hidden in the room, as there is no hiding place possible. From where, then, did he come?”

“He came through the hole in the roof,” I cried.

“Of course he did. He must have done so. If you will be kind enough to hold the lamp for me, we shall now extend our search to the room above,—the secret room in which the treasure was found.”

He climbed the steps, and, seizing a beam with either hand, he swung himself up into the attic. Then, lying on his face, he reached down for the lamp and held it while I followed him.

The room in which we found ourselves was about ten feet one way and six the other. The floor was formed by the beams, with thin lath-and-plaster between, so that in walking one had to step from beam to beam. The roof ran up to a point, and was evidently the inner shell of the true roof of the house. There was no furniture of any sort, and the dust of years lay thick upon the floor.

“Here you are, you see,” said Sherlock Holmes, putting his hand against the sloping wall. “This is a trap-door which leads out on to the roof. I can push it back, and here is the roof itself, sloping at a gentle angle. This, then, is the way by which Number One entered. Let us see if we can find any other traces of his individuality.”

He held down the lamp to the floor, and as he did so I saw for the second time that night a startled, surprised look come over his face. For myself, as I followed his gaze my skin was cold under my clothes. The floor was covered thickly with the prints of a naked foot,—clear, well defined, perfectly formed, but scarcely half the size of those of an ordinary man.

“Holmes,” I said, in a whisper, “a child has done the horrid thing.”

He had recovered his self-possession in an instant. “I was staggered for the moment,” he said, “but the thing is quite natural. My memory failed me, or I should have been able to foretell it. There is nothing more to be learned here. Let us go down.”

“What is your theory, then, as to those footmarks?” I asked, eagerly, when we had regained the lower room once more.

“My dear Watson, try a little analysis yourself,” said he, with a touch of impatience. “You know my methods. Apply them, and it will be instructive to compare results.”

“I cannot think of anything which will explain the facts,” I answered.

“It will be clear enough to you soon,” he said, in a casual way. “I think there is nothing else of importance here, but I will look.” He pulled out his lens and a tape measure. He hurried about the room on his knees, measuring, comparing, examining. His long thin nose was only a few inches from the floor boards. His small bright eyes were deep-set and shining like a bird’s. His movements were so quick, silent and secret, like a trained dog picking out a smell. I could not help thinking what a terrible criminal he would have made, if he had turned his energy and cleverness against the law instead of using them to defend it. As he hunted about, he kept talking quietly to himself. Finally he gave a loud cry of joy.

“We are certainly in luck,” said he. “We ought to have very little trouble now. Number One has had the bad luck to step in the creosote. You can see the outline of the edge of his small foot here at the side of this bad-smelling mess. The bottle has been cracked, you see, and the stuff has run out.”

“What then?” I asked.

“Why, we have got him, that is all,” said he. “I know a dog that would follow that smell to the end of the world. If a pack can track a dragged herring across a county, how far can a specially trained dog follow a smell as strong as this? It sounds like a sum in the rule of three. The answer should give us the—But hello! here are the official representatives of the law.”

Heavy steps and the noise of loud voices could be heard from below. The hall door shut with a loud crash.

“Before they come,” said Holmes, “just put your hand here on this poor man’s arm, and here on his leg. What do you feel?”

“The muscles are as hard as a board,” I answered.

“Quite so. They are in a state of extreme tightening, far beyond the usual difficulty of moving a dead body. Together with this twisted face, this Hippocratic smile, or ‘risus sardonicus,’ as the old writers called it, what conclusion would it suggest to your mind?”

“Death from some powerful plant poison,” I answered,—“some strychnine-like substance which would produce tetanus.”

“That was the idea which came to me the instant I saw the drawn muscles of the face. On getting into the room I at once looked for the way the poison had entered the body. As you saw, I found a sharp point which had been driven or shot with no great force into the skin of the head. You observe that the part struck was the one that would be turned towards the hole in the ceiling if the man were sitting up straight in his chair. Now examine the sharp point.”

I took it up carefully and held it in the light of the lantern. It was long, sharp, and black. Near the point it looked shiny, as though some sticky substance had dried upon it. The blunt end had been cut and rounded off with a knife.

“Is that an English sharp point?” he asked.

“No, it certainly is not.”

“With all these facts you should be able to draw some fair conclusion. But here are the regular police; so the helpers may beat a retreat.”

As he spoke, the steps which had been coming nearer sounded loudly in the passage. A very fat, heavy man in a grey suit walked heavily into the room. He was red-faced, big and swollen, with a pair of very small twinkling eyes which looked sharply out from between swollen and puffy bags. He was closely followed by an inspector in uniform, and by the still shaking Thaddeus Sholto.

“Here’s a business!” he cried, in a muffled, hoarse voice. “Here’s a pretty business! But who are all these? Why, the house seems to be as full as a rabbit-warren!”

“I think you must remember me, Mr. Athelney Jones,” said Holmes, quietly.

“Why, of course I do!” he wheezed. “It’s Mr. Sherlock Holmes, the theorist. Remember you! I’ll never forget how you lectured us all on causes and conclusions and effects in the Bishopgate jewel case. It’s true you set us on the right track; but you’ll admit now that it was more by good luck than good guidance.”

“It was a piece of very simple reasoning.”

“Oh, come, now, come! Never be ashamed to admit it. But what is all this? Bad business! Bad business! Hard facts here,—no room for theories. How lucky that I happened to be out at Norwood over another case! I was at the station when the message arrived. What do you think the man died of?”

“Oh, this is hardly a case for me to theorise over,” said Holmes, dryly.

“No, no. Still, we can’t deny that you hit the nail on the head sometimes. Dear me! Door locked, I understand. Jewels worth half a million missing. How was the window?”

“Fastened; but there are steps on the sill.”

“Well, well, if it was fastened the steps could have nothing to do with the matter. That’s common sense. Man might have died in a fit; but then the jewels are missing. Ha! I have a theory. These flashes come upon me at times.—Just step outside, sergeant, and you, Mr. Sholto. Your friend can remain.—What do you think of this, Holmes? Sholto was, on his own confession, with his brother last night. The brother died in a fit, on which Sholto walked off with the treasure. How’s that?”

“On which the dead man very considerately got up and locked the door on the inside.”

“Hum! There’s a flaw there. Let us apply common sense to the matter. This Thaddeus Sholto was with his brother; there was a quarrel; so much we know. The brother is dead and the jewels are gone. So much also we know. No one saw the brother from the time Thaddeus left him. His bed had not been slept in. Thaddeus is evidently in a most disturbed state of mind. His appearance is—well, not attractive. You see that I am weaving my web round Thaddeus. The net begins to close upon him.”

“You are not quite in possession of the facts yet,” said Holmes. “This splinter of wood, which I have every reason to believe is poisoned, was in the man’s head where you still see the mark. This card, written as you see it, was on the table. Beside it lay this rather curious stone-headed instrument. How does all that fit into your theory?”

“That proves it in every way,” said the fat detective, looking important. “The house is full of Indian curiosities. Thaddeus brought this thing up. If this small piece is poisonous, Thaddeus could have used it to kill, just like any other man. The card is some trick—most likely a blind. The only question is, how did he leave? Ah, of course, here is a hole in the roof.” With great energy, for a man of his size, he jumped up the steps and squeezed through into the small room under the roof. Right after that, we heard his happy voice saying he had found the trap-door.

“He can find something,” said Holmes, lifting his shoulders. “He has moments of reason now and then. _Il n’y a pas des sots si incommodes que ceux qui ont de l’esprit!_”

“You see!” said Athelney Jones, coming down the steps again. “Facts are better than mere theories, after all. My view of the case is confirmed. There is a trap-door leading to the roof, and it is partly open.”

“I was the one who opened it.”

“Oh, really! You did notice it, then?” He seemed a little disappointed by this discovery. “Well, whoever noticed it, it shows how our gentleman got away. Inspector!”

“Yes, sir,” came a voice from the passage.

“Ask Mr. Sholto to step this way.—Mr. Sholto, it is my duty to tell you that anything you say may be used against you. I arrest you in the Queen’s name as being involved in the death of your brother.”

“There, now! Didn’t I tell you!” cried the poor little man, throwing out his hands and looking from one of us to another.

“Don’t worry about it, Mr. Sholto,” said Holmes. “I think I can promise to clear you of the charge.”

“Don’t promise too much, Mr. Theorist—don’t promise too much!” snapped the detective. “You may find it harder than you think.”

“Not only will I clear him, Mr. Jones, but I will give you for free the name and description of one of the two people who were in this room last night. His name, I have every reason to believe, is Jonathan Small. He is a poorly educated man, small, active, with his right leg gone, and wearing a wooden stump that is worn away on the inner side. His left boot has a rough, square-toed sole, with an iron band around the heel. He is a middle-aged man, much sunburned, and has been a convict. These few details may help you a little, along with the fact that a good deal of skin is missing from the palm of his hand. The other man—”

“Ah! the other man—?” asked Athelney Jones, in a mocking voice, but still impressed, as I could easily see, by the exactness of the other man’s manner.

“Is a rather strange person,” said Sherlock Holmes, turning on his heel. “I hope before very long to be able to introduce you to the pair of them.—A word with you, Watson.”

He led me out to the top of the stair. “This unexpected event,” he said, “has made us rather lose sight of the original purpose of our journey.”

“I have just been thinking so,” I answered. “It is not right that Miss Morstan should remain in this stricken house.”

“No. You must take her home. She lives with Mrs. Cecil Forrester, in Lower Camberwell: so it is not very far. I will wait for you here if you drive out again. Or perhaps you are too tired?”

“Not at all. I don’t think I could rest until I know more of this strange business. I have seen something of the rough side of life, but I give you my word that this quick succession of strange surprises tonight has shaken my nerve completely. I should like, however, to see the matter through with you, now that I have got so far.”

“Your presence will be of great service to me,” he answered. “We shall work the case out independently, and leave this fellow Jones to exult over any mare’s-nest which he may choose to construct. When you have dropped Miss Morstan I wish you to go on to No. 3, Pinchin Lane, down near the water’s edge at Lambeth. The third house on the right-hand side is a bird-stuffer’s: Sherman is the name. You will see a weasel holding a young rabbit in the window. Knock old Sherman up, and tell him, with my compliments, that I want Toby at once. You will bring Toby back in the cab with you.”

“A dog, I suppose.”

“Yes—a strange mixed-breed dog, with a most amazing power of smell. I would rather have Toby’s help than that of the whole detective force of London.”

“I shall bring him, then,” said I. “It is one now. I ought to be back before three, if I can get a fresh horse.”

“And I,” said Holmes, “shall see what I can learn from Mrs. Bernstone, and from the Indian servant, who, Mr. Thaddeus tells me, sleeps in the next room under the roof. Then I shall study the great Jones’s methods and listen to his not too delicate sarcasms. ‘_Wir sind gewohnt dass die Menschen verhöhnen was sie nicht verstehen._’ Goethe is always pithy.”

CHAPTER 7. The Episode of the Barrel

The police had brought a cab with them. I went with Miss Morstan in it back to her home. Women are like angels. She had carried her trouble with a calm face as long as there was someone weaker than herself to support. I had found her bright and calm by the side of the frightened housekeeper. In the cab, however, she first felt faint. Then she burst into a flood of weeping. The adventures of the night had tried her so hard. She has told me since that she thought me cold and distant on that journey. She little guessed the struggle within my breast. She did not guess the effort of self-control which held me back. My sympathies and my love went out to her, even as my hand had in the garden. I felt that years of the usual ways of life could not teach me to know her sweet, brave nature as had this one day of strange experiences. Yet there were two thoughts which sealed the words of love upon my lips. She was weak and helpless, shaken in mind and nerve. It was to take her at a disadvantage to push love upon her at such a time. Worse still, she was rich. If Holmes's researches were successful, she would be a rich woman. Was it fair, was it right, that a half-pay surgeon should take such advantage of a closeness which chance had brought about? Might she not look upon me as a mere common fortune-seeker? I could not bear to risk that such a thought should cross her mind. This Agra treasure stood like an impassable wall between us.

It was nearly two when we reached Mrs. Cecil Forrester's. The servants had gone to bed hours ago. But Mrs. Forrester had been so interested by the strange message which Miss Morstan had received that she had sat up in the hope of her return. She opened the door herself, a middle-aged, graceful woman. It gave me joy to see how gently her arm stole round the other's waist. It gave me joy to hear how motherly was the voice in which she greeted her. She was clearly no mere paid servant, but an honoured friend. I was introduced, and Mrs. Forrester earnestly begged me to step in and tell her our adventures. I explained, however, the importance of my errand. I promised faithfully to call and report any progress which we might make with the case. As we drove away I stole a glance back. I still seem to see that little group on the step. I see the two graceful, clinging figures, the half-opened door, the hall-light shining through stained glass, the barometer, and the bright stair-rods. It was soothing to catch even that passing glimpse of a tranquil English home. It was soothing in the midst of the wild, dark business which had absorbed us.

And the more I thought of what had happened, the wilder and darker it grew. I reviewed the whole extraordinary sequence of events as I rattled on through the silent gas-lit streets. There was the original problem: that at least was pretty clear now. The death of Captain Morstan, the sending of the pearls, the advertisement, the letter,—we had had light upon all those events. They had only led us, however, to a deeper and far more tragic mystery. The Indian treasure, the curious plan found among Morstan's baggage, the strange scene at Major Sholto's death, the rediscovery of the treasure immediately followed by the murder of the discoverer, the very singular accompaniments to the crime, the footsteps, the remarkable weapons, the words upon the card, corresponding with those upon Captain Morstan's chart,—here was indeed a labyrinth in which a man less singularly endowed than my fellow-lodger might well despair of ever finding the clue.

Pinchin Lane was a row of shabby two-storied brick houses in the lower quarter of Lambeth. I had to knock for some time at No. 3 before I could make my impression. At last, however, there was the glint of a candle behind the blind, and a face looked out at the upper window.

"Go on, you drunken vagabone," said the face. "If you kick up any more row I'll open the kennels and let out forty-three dogs upon you."

"If you'll let one out it's just what I have come for," said I.

"Go on!" yelled the voice. "So help me gracious, I have a wiper in the bag, an' I'll drop it on your 'ead if you don't hook it."

"But I want a dog," I cried.

"I won't be argued with!" shouted Mr. Sherman. "Now stand clear, for when I say 'three,' down goes the wiper."

"Mr. Sherlock Holmes—" I began, but the words had a most magical effect, for the window instantly slammed down, and within a minute the door was unbarred and open. Mr. Sherman was a lanky, lean old man, with stooping shoulders, a stringy neck, and blue-tinted glasses.

"A friend of Mr. Sherlock is always welcome," said he. "Step in, sir. Keep clear of the badger; for he bites. Ah, naughty, naughty, would you take a nip at the gentleman?" This to a stoat which thrust its wicked head and red eyes between the bars of its cage. "Don't mind that, sir: it's only a slow-worm. It hain't got no fangs, so I gives it the run o' the room, for it keeps the beetles down. You must not mind my bein' just a little short wi' you at first, for I'm guyed at by the children, and there's many a one just comes down this lane to knock me up. What was it that Mr. Sherlock Holmes wanted, sir?"

"He wanted a dog of yours."

"Ah! that would be Toby."

"Yes, Toby was the name."

"Toby lives at No. 7 on the left here." He moved slowly forward with his candle among the queer animal family which he had gathered round him. In the uncertain, shadowy light I could see dimly that there were glancing, glimmering eyes peeping down at us from every cranny and corner. Even the rafters above our heads were lined by solemn fowls, who lazily shifted their weight from one leg to the other as our voices disturbed their slumbers.

Toby proved to be an ugly, long-haired, lop-eared creature, half spaniel and half lurcher, brown-and-white in colour, with a very clumsy waddling gait. It accepted after some hesitation a lump of sugar which the old naturalist handed to me, and, having thus sealed an alliance, it followed me to the cab, and made no difficulties about accompanying me. It had just struck three on the Palace clock when I found myself back once more at Pondicherry Lodge. The ex-prize-fighter McMurdo had, I found, been arrested as an accessory, and both he and Mr. Sholto had been marched off to the station. Two constables guarded the narrow gate, but they allowed me to pass with the dog on my mentioning the detective's name.

Holmes was standing on the door-step, with his hands in his pockets, smoking his pipe.

"Ah, you have him there!" he said. "Good dog, then! Athelney Jones has gone. We have had a huge show of energy since you left. He has arrested not only our friend Thaddeus, but the gatekeeper, the housekeeper, and the Indian servant. We have the place to ourselves, except for a sergeant upstairs. Leave the dog here, and come up."

We tied Toby to the hall table, and went up the stairs again. The room was as he had left it, except that a sheet had been laid over the central figure. A tired-looking police sergeant lay back in the corner.

"Lend me your lamp, sergeant," said my companion. "Now tie this bit of card round my neck, so it hangs in front of me. Thank you. Now I must kick off my boots and socks. Just carry them down with you, Watson. I am going to do a little climbing. And put my handkerchief into the creasote. That will do. Now come up into the garret with me for a moment."

We climbed up through the hole. Holmes turned his light once more upon the footprints in the dust.

"I want you to notice these footprints especially," he said. "Do you see anything important about them?"

"They belong," I said, "to a child or a small woman."

"Apart from their size, though. Is there nothing else?"

"They look much like other footprints."

"Not at all. Look here! This is the print of a right foot in the dust. Now I make one with my bare foot beside it. What is the main difference?"

"Your toes are all pressed together. The other print has each toe clearly separate."

"Quite so. That is the point. Keep that in mind. Now, would you please step over to that open window and smell the edge of the woodwork? I shall stay here, as I have this handkerchief in my hand."

I did as he directed, and at once I smelled a strong tar-like smell.

"That is where he put his foot in getting out. If you can follow him, I should think Toby will have no difficulty. Now run downstairs, let the dog go, and look out for Blondin."

By the time I got out into the grounds, Sherlock Holmes was on the roof. I could see him like a huge glowing worm crawling very slowly along the ridge. I lost sight of him behind a group of chimneys. But he soon appeared again, and then vanished once more on the opposite side. When I made my way round there, I found him sitting at one of the corner edges of the roof.

"That you, Watson?" he cried.

"Yes."

"This is the place. What is that black thing down there?"

"A water barrel."

"Top on it?"

"Yes."

"No sign of a ladder?"

"No."

"Confound the fellow! It's a most dangerous place. I ought to be able to come down where he could climb up. The water pipe feels pretty firm. Here goes, anyway."

There was a scuffling of feet, and the lamp began to come steadily down the side of the wall. Then with a light spring he came onto the barrel, and from there to the ground.

"It was easy to follow him," he said, pulling on his socks and boots. "Tiles were loose the whole way along, and in his hurry he had dropped this. It confirms my diagnosis, as you doctors say."

The object which he held up to me was a small pocket or bag woven out of coloured grasses, with a few cheap beads strung round it. In shape and size it was not unlike a cigarette case. Inside were half a dozen spines of dark wood, sharp at one end and rounded at the other, like the one that had struck Bartholomew Sholto.

"They are hellish things," he said. "Be careful that you don't prick yourself. I'm delighted to have them, for the chances are that they are all he has. There is less fear of you or me finding one in our skin before long. I would sooner face a Martini bullet myself. Are you ready for a six-mile walk, Watson?"

"Certainly," I answered.

"Your leg will stand it?"

"Oh, yes."

"Here you are, doggy! Good old Toby! Smell it, Toby, smell it!" He pushed the creasote handkerchief under the dog's nose. The creature stood with its fluffy legs apart, and with a most comical tilt to its head, like a connoisseur sniffing the bouquet of a famous wine. Holmes then threw the handkerchief to a distance, fastened a strong cord to the mongrel's collar, and led him to the foot of the water barrel. The creature at once broke into a series of high, trembling yelps. With his nose on the ground and his tail in the air, he pattered off upon the trail at a pace which strained his leash and kept us at top speed.

The east had been gradually growing white, and we could now see some distance in the cold grey light. The square, massive house, with its black, empty windows and high, bare walls, towered up, sad and lonely, behind us. Our course led right across the grounds, in and out among the trenches and pits with which they were scarred and crossed. The whole place, with its scattered piles of dirt and poor little shrubs, had a blighted, evil-looking appearance which matched the black tragedy that hung over it.

On reaching the boundary wall, Toby ran along, whining eagerly, underneath its shadow, and stopped at last in a corner screened by a young beech. Where the two walls joined, several bricks had been loosened. The gaps left were worn down and rounded on the lower side, as though they had often been used as steps. Holmes climbed up, and, taking the dog from me, he dropped it over onto the other side.

"There's the print of the wooden leg's hand," he remarked, as I climbed up beside him. "You see the slight smear of blood upon the white plaster. What a lucky thing it is that we have had no very heavy rain since yesterday! The scent will lie upon the road in spite of their twenty-eight hours' start."

I confess that I had my doubts when I thought about the great traffic which had passed along the London road in the interval. My fears were soon calmed, however. Toby never hesitated or turned aside, but waddled on in his peculiar rolling way. Clearly, the sharp smell of the creasote rose high above all other competing smells.

"Do not imagine," said Holmes, "that I depend for my success in this case upon the mere chance of one of these fellows having put his foot in the chemical. I have knowledge now which would enable me to trace them in many different ways. This, however, is the readiest way, and since fortune has put it into our hands, I should be to blame if I neglected it. It has, however, prevented the case from becoming the pretty little intellectual problem which it at one time promised to be. There might have been some credit to be gained out of it, but for this too obvious clue."

“There is credit, and more than enough,” said I. “I promise you, Holmes, that I wonder at the ways you get your results in this case, even more than I did in the Jefferson Hope Murder. The thing seems to me to be deeper and harder to explain. How, for example, could you describe the wooden-legged man with such confidence?”

“Pshaw, my dear boy! it was simple itself. I do not wish to be showy. It is all clear and open. Two officers who are in command of a convict-guard learn an important secret about buried treasure. A map is drawn for them by an Englishman named Jonathan Small. You remember that we saw the name upon the chart in Captain Morstan’s possession. He had signed it for himself and his friends,—the sign of the four, as he called it in a rather dramatic way. With the help of this chart, the officers—or one of them—gets the treasure and brings it to England. We will suppose that some condition under which he received it was left unfulfilled. Now, then, why did not Jonathan Small get the treasure himself? The answer is clear. The chart is dated at a time when Morstan was brought into close contact with convicts. Jonathan Small did not get the treasure because he and his friends were themselves convicts and could not get away.”

“But that is only guessing,” said I.

“It is more than that. It is the only idea which covers the facts. Let us see how it fits with what came after. Major Sholto remains at peace for some years, happy in the possession of his treasure. Then he receives a letter from India which gives him a great fear. What was that?”

“A letter to say that the men whom he had wronged had been set free.”

“Or had escaped. That is much more likely, for he would have known what their term of imprisonment was. It would not have been a surprise to him. What does he do then? He guards himself against a wooden-legged man,—a white man, mark you, for he mistakes a white tradesman for him, and actually fires a pistol at him. Now, only one white man’s name is on the chart. The others are Hindoos or Mohammedans. There is no other white man. Therefore we may say with confidence that the wooden-legged man is the same as Jonathan Small. Does the reasoning strike you as being faulty?”

“No: it is clear and short.”

“Well, now, let us put ourselves in the place of Jonathan Small. Let us look at it from his point of view. He comes to England with two ideas: to get back what he would consider to be his rights, and to have his revenge upon the man who had wronged him. He found out where Sholto lived, and very possibly he set up communications with some one inside the house. There is this butler, Lal Rao, whom we have not seen. Mrs. Bernstone gives him far from a good character. Small could not find out, however, where the treasure was hid, for no one ever knew, except the major and one faithful servant who had died. Suddenly Small learns that the major is on his death-bed. In a frenzy lest the secret of the treasure die with him, he runs the gauntlet of the guards, makes his way to the dying man’s window, and is only deterred from entering by the presence of his two sons. Mad with hate, however, against the dead man, he enters the room that night, searches his private papers in the hope of discovering some memorandum relating to the treasure, and finally leaves a momento of his visit in the short inscription upon the card. He had doubtless planned beforehand that should he slay the major he would leave some such record upon the body as a sign that it was not a common murder, but, from the point of view of the four friends, something in the nature of an act of justice. Whimsical and strange conceits of this kind are common enough in the records of crime, and usually give valuable clues as to the criminal. Do you follow all this?”

“Very clearly.”

“Now, what could Jonathan Small do? He could only continue to keep a secret watch upon the efforts made to find the treasure. Possibly he leaves England and only comes back at intervals. Then comes the discovery of the garret, and he is instantly informed of it. We again trace the presence of some friend in the household. Jonathan, with his wooden leg, is utterly unable to reach the lofty room of Bartholomew Sholto. He takes with him, however, a rather curious friend, who gets over this difficulty, but dips his naked foot into creasote, whence comes Toby, and a six-mile limp for a half-pay officer with a damaged tendo Achillis.”

“But it was the friend, and not Jonathan, who committed the crime.”

“Quite so. And rather to Jonathan’s disgust, to judge by the way he stamped about when he got into the room. He bore no grudge against Bartholomew Sholto, and would have preferred if he could have been simply bound and gagged. He did not wish to put his head in a halter. There was no help for it, however: the savage instincts of his companion had broken out, and the poison had done its work: so Jonathan Small left his record, lowered the treasure-box to the ground, and followed it himself. That was the train of events as far as I can decipher them. Of course as to his personal appearance he must be middle-aged, and must be sunburned after serving his time in such an oven as the Andamans. His height is readily calculated from the length of his stride, and we know that he was bearded. His hairiness was the one point which impressed itself upon Thaddeus Sholto when he saw him at the window. I do not know that there is anything else.”

“The friend?”

“Ah, well, there is no great mystery in that. But you will know all about it soon enough. How sweet the morning air is! See how that one little cloud floats like a pink feather from some gigantic flamingo. Now the red rim of the sun pushes itself over the London cloud-bank. It shines on a good many folk, but on none, I dare bet, who are on a stranger errand than you and I. How small we feel with our petty ambitions and strivings in the presence of the great elemental forces of nature! Are you well up in your Jean Paul?”

“Fairly so. I worked back to him through Carlyle.”

“That was like following the brook to the parent lake. He makes one curious but profound remark. It is that the chief proof of man’s real greatness lies in his perception of his own smallness. It argues, you see, a power of comparison and of appreciation which is in itself a proof of nobility. There is much food for thought in Richter. You have not a pistol, have you?”

“I have my stick.”

"It is just possible that we may need something of the sort if we get to their hiding place. Jonathan I shall leave to you. But if the other turns nasty I shall shoot him dead." He took out his revolver as he spoke. He loaded two of the rooms and put it back into the right-hand pocket of his jacket.

We had during this time been following the guidance of Toby down the half-rural roads lined with houses which lead to the city. Now, however, we were beginning to come among continuous streets. There labourers and dockmen were already moving about. Untidy women were taking down window covers and brushing door-steps. At the square-topped corner public houses business was just beginning. Rough-looking men were coming out, rubbing their arms across their faces after their morning drink. Strange dogs came up slowly and stared at us in wonder as we passed. But our wonderful Toby looked neither to the right nor to the left. He ran onwards with his nose to the ground and an occasional eager cry which spoke of a hot scent.

We had crossed Streatham, Brixton, Camberwell, and now found ourselves in Kennington Lane. We had turned away through the side-streets to the east of the Oval. The men whom we pursued seemed to have taken a strangely winding road, with the idea probably of escaping observation. They had never kept to the main road if a side-street going the same way would serve their turn. At the foot of Kennington Lane they had moved away to the left through Bond Street and Miles Street. Where the latter street turns into Knight's Place, Toby stopped advancing. He began to run backwards and forwards with one ear up and the other hanging down, the very picture of a dog's indecision. Then he walked round in circles, looking up to us from time to time, as if to ask for sympathy in his embarrassment.

"What in the world is the matter with the dog?" said Holmes in a low rough voice. "They surely would not take a cab, or go off in a balloon."

"Perhaps they stood here for some time," I suggested.

"Ah! it is all right. He is off again," said my companion, in a tone of relief.

He was indeed off. After smelling round again he suddenly made up his mind. He ran away with an energy and determination such as he had not yet shown. The scent appeared to be much hotter than before. He had not even to put his nose on the ground, but pulled at his leash and tried to break into a run. I could see by the light in Holmes's eyes that he thought we were nearing the end of our journey.

Our course now ran down Nine Elms until we came to Broderick and Nelson's large timber-yard, just past the White Eagle tavern. Here the dog, wild with excitement, turned down through the side-gate into the enclosure, where the sawyers were already at work. On the dog ran through sawdust and shavings, down an alley, round a passage, between two wood-piles. Finally, with a triumphant cry, he sprang upon a large barrel which still stood upon the hand-cart on which it had been brought. With hanging tongue and blinking eyes, Toby stood upon the cask. He looked from one to the other of us for some sign of approval. The boards of the barrel and the wheels of the cart were smeared with a dark liquid. The whole air was heavy with the smell of creasote.

Sherlock Holmes and I looked blankly at each other, and then burst at the same time into a fit of laughter we could not control.

CHAPTER 8. The Baker Street Irregulars

The Baker Street Irregulars

“What now?” I asked. “Toby has lost his special skill.”

“He did what he could,” said Holmes. He lifted the dog down from the barrel and walked him out of the wood yard. “Think about how much creasote is carried around London in one day. It is no surprise that our trail was crossed. People use it a lot now, especially to season wood. Poor Toby is not to blame.”

“We must get back on the main scent, I suppose.”

“Yes. And luckily, we have no distance to go. Clearly, what puzzled the dog at the corner of Knight’s Place was that two different trails ran in opposite directions. We took the wrong one. Now we only need to follow the other.”

There was no difficulty about this. We led Toby to the place where he had made his mistake. He cast about in a wide circle and finally ran off in a fresh direction.

“We must take care that he does not now bring us to the place where the creasote barrel came from,” I observed.

“I had thought of that. But you notice that he keeps on the pavement, while the barrel passed down the roadway. No, we are on the true scent now.”

It tended down towards the river-side. It ran through Belmont Place and Prince’s Street. At the end of Broad Street it ran right down to the water’s edge, where there was a small wooden wharf. Toby led us to the very edge of this. There he stood whining, looking out on the dark current beyond.

“We are out of luck,” said Holmes. “They have taken to a boat here.” Several small punts and skiffs were lying about in the water and on the edge of the wharf. We took Toby round to each in turn. But though he sniffed earnestly, he made no sign.

Close to the rude landing-stage was a small brick house. A wooden placard was slung out through the second window. “Mordecai Smith” was printed across it in large letters. Underneath it said, “Boats to hire by the hour or day.” A second inscription above the door told us that a steam launch was kept. A great pile of coke upon the jetty confirmed this. Sherlock Holmes looked slowly round, and his face took on an ominous expression.

“This looks bad,” said he. “These fellows are sharper than I expected. They seem to have covered their tracks. I fear there has been preconcerted management here.”

He was approaching the door of the house when it opened. A little, curly-headed lad of six came running out. A stoutish, red-faced woman with a large sponge in her hand followed him.

“You come back and be washed, Jack,” she shouted. “Come back, you young imp. If your father comes home and finds you like that, he’ll let us hear of it.”

“Dear little chap!” said Holmes, strategically. “What a rosy-cheeked young rascal! Now, Jack, is there anything you would like?”

The youth pondered for a moment. “I’d like a shillin’,” said he.

“Nothing you would like better?”

“I’d like two shillin’ better,” the prodigy answered, after some thought.

“Here you are, then! Catch! A fine child, Mrs. Smith!”

“Lor’ bless you, sir, he is that, and forward. He gets a’most too much for me to manage, ’specially when my man is away days at a time.”

“Away, is he?” said Holmes, in a disappointed voice. “I am sorry for that, for I wanted to speak to Mr. Smith.”

“He’s been away since yesterday mornin’, sir. And, truth to tell, I am beginnin’ to feel frightened about him. But if it was about a boat, sir, maybe I could serve as well.”

“I wanted to hire his steam launch.”

“Why, bless you, sir, it is in the steam launch that he has gone. That’s what puzzles me. For I know there ain’t more coals in her than would take her to about Woolwich and back. If he’d been away in the barge I’d ha’ thought nothin’. For many a time a job has taken him as far as Gravesend. And then if there was much doin’ there he might ha’ stayed over. But what good is a steam launch without coals?”

“He might have bought some at a wharf down the river.”

“He might, sir, but it weren’t his way. Many a time I’ve heard him call out at the prices they charge for a few odd bags. Besides, I don’t like that wooden-legged man, wi’ his ugly face and outlandish talk. What did he want always knockin’ about here for?”

“A wooden-legged man?” said Holmes, with bland surprise.

“Yes, sir, a brown, monkey-faced chap that’s called more’n once for my old man. It was him that roused him up yesternight. And what’s more, my man knew he was comin’, for he had steam up in the launch. I tell you straight, sir, I don’t feel easy in my mind about it.”

“But, my dear Mrs. Smith,” said Holmes, shrugging his shoulders, “you are frightening yourself about nothing. How could you possibly tell that it was the wooden-legged man who came in the night? I don’t quite understand how you can be so sure.”

“His voice, sir. I knew his voice, which is kind o’ thick and foggy. He tapped at the winder, about three it would be. ‘Show a leg, matey,’ says he. ‘Time to turn out guard.’ My old man woke up Jim, that’s my eldest, and away they went, without so much as a word to me. I could hear the wooden leg clackin’ on the stones.”

“And was this wooden-legged man alone?”

“Couldn’t say, I am sure, sir. I didn’t hear no one else.”

“I am sorry, Mrs. Smith, for I wanted a steam launch, and I have heard good reports of the—Let me see, what is her name?”

“The _Aurora_, sir.”

“Ah! She’s not that old green launch with a yellow line, very broad in the beam?”

“No, indeed. She’s as trim a little thing as any on the river. She’s been fresh painted, black with two red streaks.”

“Thanks. I hope that you will hear soon from Mr. Smith. I am going down the river. And if I should see anything of the _Aurora_ I shall let him know that you are uneasy. A black funnel, you say?”

“No, sir. Black with a white band.”

“Ah, of course. It was the sides which were black. Good-morning, Mrs. Smith. There is a boatman here with a wherry, Watson. We shall take it and cross the river.”

“The main thing with people of that sort,” said Holmes, as we sat in the sheets of the wherry, “is never to let them think that their information can be of the slightest importance to you. If you do, they will instantly shut up like an oyster. If you listen to them under protest, as it were, you are very likely to get what you want.”

“Our course now seems pretty clear,” said I.

“What would you do, then?”

“I would engage a launch and go down the river on the track of the _Aurora_.”

"My dear friend, it would be a very big task. She may have stopped at any dock on either side of the river between here and Greenwich. Below the bridge there is a whole maze of landing places for miles. It would take you days and days to try them all, if you did it alone."

"Use the police, then."

"No. I will probably call Athelney Jones in at the last moment. He is not a bad man, and I would not like to do anything that would hurt him in his work. But I want to work it out myself, now that we have gone so far."

"Could we put a notice in the paper, then, asking for information from the dock workers?"

"Worse and worse! Our men would know that we were close behind them, and they would leave the country. Even now, they are likely enough to leave, but as long as they think they are perfectly safe they will not hurry. Jones's energy will help us there, for his view of the case will surely get into the daily papers, and the runaways will think that everyone is following a false trail."

"What are we to do, then?" I asked, as we landed near Millbank Penitentiary.

"Take this cab, drive home, have some breakfast, and get an hour of sleep. It is quite possible that we may be on foot again tonight. Stop at a telegraph office, driver! We will keep Toby, for he may be of use to us yet."

We stopped at the Great Peter Street post office, and Holmes sent his message. "Who do you think that is to?" he asked, as we continued our journey.

"I am sure I do not know."

"You remember the Baker Street group of the detective police force whom I used in the Jefferson Hope case?"

"Well," said I, laughing.

"This is just the case where they might be very useful. If they fail, I have other plans, but I shall try them first. That message was to my dirty little assistant, Wiggins, and I expect that he and his group will be with us before we have finished our breakfast."

It was between eight and nine now, and I felt a strong reaction after the many excitements of the night. I was weak and tired, confused in mind and tired in body. I did not have the professional enthusiasm that carried my friend on, nor could I look at the matter as a mere abstract intellectual problem. As far as the death of Bartholomew Sholto went, I had heard little good of him, and could feel no strong dislike for his killers. The treasure, however, was a different matter. That, or part of it, belonged by right to Miss Morstan. While there was a chance of recovering it I was ready to give my life to the one aim. True, if I found it, it would probably put her forever beyond my reach. Yet it would be a small and selfish love that would be influenced by such a thought as that. If Holmes could work to find the criminals, I had ten times stronger reason to urge me on to find the treasure.

A bath at Baker Street and a complete change of clothes made me feel wonderfully fresh. When I came down to our room I found the breakfast laid and Holmes pouring out the coffee.

"Here it is," said he, laughing, and pointing to an open newspaper. "The energetic Jones and the ever-present reporter have arranged it between them. But you have had enough of the case. Better have your ham and eggs first."

I took the paper from him and read the short notice, which was headed "Mysterious Business at Upper Norwood."

"About twelve last night," said the _Standard_, "Mr. Bartholomew Sholto, of Pondicherry Lodge, Upper Norwood, was found dead in his room in circumstances which point to foul play. As far as we can learn, no actual traces of violence were found upon Mr. Sholto's person, but a valuable collection of Indian gems which the dead gentleman had inherited from his father has been carried off. The discovery was first made by Mr. Sherlock Holmes and Dr. Watson, who had called at the house with Mr. Thaddeus Sholto, brother of the dead man. By a singular piece of good fortune, Mr. Athelney Jones, the well-known member of the detective police force, happened to be at the Norwood Police Station, and was on the spot within half an hour of the first alarm. His trained and experienced abilities were at once directed towards the detection of the criminals, with the pleasing result that the brother, Thaddeus Sholto, has already been arrested, together with the housekeeper, Mrs. Bernstone, an Indian butler named Lal Rao, and a porter, or gatekeeper, named McMurdo. It is quite certain that the thief or thieves knew the house well, for Mr. Jones's well-known technical knowledge and his powers of close observation have enabled him to prove beyond doubt that the criminals could not have entered by the door or by the window, but must have made their way across the roof of the building, and so through a trap-door into a room which connected with that in which the body was found. This fact, which has been very clearly established, proves beyond doubt that it was no mere random burglary. The prompt and energetic action of the officers of the law shows the great advantage of the presence on such occasions of a single strong and masterful mind. We cannot but think that it supplies an argument to those who would wish to see our detectives more spread out, and so brought into closer and more effective touch with the cases which it is their duty to investigate."

"Isn't it wonderful!" said Holmes, smiling over his coffee cup. "What do you think of it?"

"I think that we ourselves have had a narrow escape from being arrested for the crime."

"So do I. I would not answer for our safety now, if he should happen to have another of his attacks of energy."

At this moment there was a loud ring at the bell, and I could hear Mrs. Hudson, our landlady, raising her voice in a cry of protest and dismay.

"By heaven, Holmes," I said, half rising, "I believe that they are really after us."

"No, it is not quite so bad as that. It is the unofficial force—the Baker Street irregulars."

As he spoke, there came a quick pattering of bare feet upon the stairs, a clatter of high voices, and in rushed a dozen dirty and ragged little street boys. There was some show of order among them, despite their wild entry, for they instantly drew up in line and stood facing us with expectant faces. One of their number, taller and older than the others, stood forward with a lazy air of superiority which was very funny in such a disreputable little scarecrow.

"Got your message, sir," said he, "and brought them on fast. Three shillings and sixpence for tickets."

“Here you are,” said Holmes, taking out some silver coins. “In future they can report to you, Wiggins, and you to me. I cannot let the house be full of boys like this. But it is good that you all hear the instructions. I want to find a steam launch called the _Aurora_. The owner is Mordecai Smith. The boat is black with two red lines. The funnel is black with a white band. She is somewhere down the river. I want one boy to wait at Mordecai Smith’s landing-stage, opposite Millbank. He should say if the boat comes back. You must divide the work among yourselves. Cover both banks well. Let me know as soon as you have news. Is that all clear?”

“Yes, guv’nor,” said Wiggins.

“Same pay as before. And a guinea for the boy who finds the boat. Here is one day’s pay in advance. Now off you go!” He gave them a shilling each. They ran noisily down the stairs. A moment later I saw them moving quickly down the street.

“If the launch is on the water, they will find her,” said Holmes. He rose from the table and lit his pipe. “They can go everywhere. They can see everything. They can hear everyone. I expect to hear before evening that they have seen her. Meanwhile, we can do nothing but wait for results. We cannot follow the broken trail until we find either the _Aurora_ or Mr. Mordecai Smith.”

“Toby could eat these scraps, I dare say. Are you going to bed, Holmes?”

“No, I am not tired. I have a strange body. I never remember feeling tired from work, though doing nothing makes me completely tired. I am going to smoke and think about this strange business. My fair client has brought us into it. If any man ever had an easy task, ours should be easy. Men with wooden legs are not so common. But the other man must be completely unique, I think.”

“That other man again!”

“I do not wish to make a mystery of him—not to you, anyway. But you must have formed your own opinion. Now, consider the facts. Small footmarks. Toes never held by boots. Bare feet. A wooden club with a stone head. Great quickness. Small poisoned darts. What do you make of all this?”

“A savage!” I exclaimed. “Perhaps one of those Indians who were with Jonathan Small.”

“Hardly that,” said he. “When I first saw signs of strange weapons, I was likely to think so. But the unusual character of the footmarks made me reconsider my views. Some people of the Indian Peninsula are small men. But none could have left marks like that. The Hindoo proper has long, thin feet. The Mohammedan who wears sandals has the big toe well separated from the others, because the thong usually passes between them. Also, these little darts could only be shot in one way. They come from a blow-pipe. Now, then, where are we to find our savage?”

“South American,” I said, guessing.

He reached up and took down a large book from the shelf. “This is the first volume of a gazetteer now being published. It may be seen as the very latest authority. What have we here? ‘Andaman Islands, situated 340 miles to the north of Sumatra, in the Bay of Bengal.’ Hum! hum! What is all this? Wet climate, coral reefs, sharks, Port Blair, convict-barracks, Rutland Island, cottonwoods—Ah, here we are. ‘The original people of the Andaman Islands may perhaps claim to be the smallest race on this earth. Some anthropologists prefer the Bushmen of Africa, the Digger Indians of America, and the Terra del Fuegians. The average height is a little below four feet. Yet many full-grown adults may be found who are much smaller than this. They are a fierce, gloomy, and hard-to-control people. But they can form very devoted friendships once their trust is gained.’ Mark that, Watson. Now listen to this. ‘They are naturally very ugly. They have large, badly shaped heads, small fierce eyes, and twisted features. Their feet and hands, however, are remarkably small. They are so hard to control and fierce that all the efforts of the British official have failed to win them over in any degree. They have always been a terror to shipwrecked crews. They hit survivors on the head with their stone-headed clubs, or shoot them with poisoned arrows. These killings always end with a cannibal feast.’ Nice, friendly people, Watson! If this fellow had been left to his own devices, this affair might have taken an even more terrible turn. I think that, even as it is, Jonathan Small would give a lot not to have employed him.”

“But how did he come to have such a strange companion?”

“Ah, that is more than I can tell. However, we had already decided that Small came from the Andamans. So it is not so very surprising that this islander should be with him. No doubt we shall know all about it in time. Look here, Watson. You look completely done. Lie down there on the sofa and see if I can put you to sleep.”

He took up his violin from the corner. As I stretched myself out, he began to play some low, dreamy, sweet music. It was his own, no doubt, for he had a remarkable gift for making up music. I have a vague memory of his thin limbs, his serious face, and the rise and fall of his bow. Then I seemed to float peacefully away on a soft sea of sound. At last I found myself in dreamland, with the sweet face of Mary Morstan looking down on me.

CHAPTER 9. A Break in the Chain

A Break in the Chain

It was late in the afternoon before I woke. I felt strong and rested. Sherlock Holmes still sat exactly as I had left him. The only difference was that he had put his violin aside and was deep in a book. He looked across at me as I moved, and I noticed that his face was dark and troubled.

“You have slept well,” he said. “I was afraid that our talk would wake you.”

“I heard nothing,” I answered. “Have you had new news, then?”

“Sadly, no. I admit that I am surprised and disappointed. I expected something clear by this time. Wiggins has just come up to report. He says that no trace can be found of the launch. It is a check that makes me angry, for every hour is of importance.”

“Can I do anything? I am perfectly fresh now, and quite ready for another night out.”

“No, we can do nothing. We can only wait. If we go ourselves, the message might come while we are away, and that would cause delay. You can do what you will, but I must remain on guard.”

“Then I shall go over to Camberwell and call upon Mrs. Cecil Forrester. She asked me to, yesterday.”

“On Mrs. Cecil Forrester?” asked Holmes, with a little smile in his eyes.

“Well, of course Miss Morstan too. They were anxious to hear what happened.”

“I would not tell them too much,” said Holmes. “Women are never to be entirely trusted,—not the best of them.”

I did not stop to argue over this terrible opinion. “I shall be back in an hour or two,” I said.

“All right! Good luck! But, I say, if you are crossing the river you may as well return Toby, for I do not think it is at all likely that we shall have any use for him now.”

I took our dog accordingly, and left him, together with a half-sovereign, at the old naturalist’s in Pinchin Lane. At Camberwell I found Miss Morstan a little tired after her night’s adventures, but very eager to hear the news. Mrs. Forrester, too, was full of interest. I told them all that we had done. However, I kept back the more terrible parts of the tragedy. So, although I spoke of Mr. Sholto’s death, I said nothing of the exact manner and method of it. Even with all I kept back, there was enough to surprise and shock them.

“It is a romance!” cried Mrs. Forrester. “An injured lady, half a million in treasure, a black cannibal, and a ruffian with a wooden leg. They take the place of the usual dragon or wicked earl.”

“And two knights on a quest to the rescue,” added Miss Morstan, with a bright glance at me.

“Why, Mary, your fortune depends upon the result of this search. I do not think that you are nearly excited enough. Just imagine what it must be to be so rich, and to have the world at your feet!”

It sent a little thrill of joy to my heart to notice that she showed no sign of joy at the prospect. On the contrary, she tossed her proud head, as though the matter were one in which she took little interest.

“It is for Mr. Thaddeus Sholto that I am anxious,” she said. “Nothing else is of any consequence; but I think that he has behaved most kindly and honourably throughout. It is our duty to clear him of this terrible and false charge.”

It was evening before I left Camberwell, and quite dark by the time I reached home. My friend’s book and pipe lay by his chair, but he had disappeared. I looked about in the hope of seeing a note, but there was none.

“I suppose that Mr. Sherlock Holmes has gone out,” I said to Mrs. Hudson as she came up to lower the blinds.

“No, sir. He has gone to his room, sir. Do you know, sir,” sinking her voice into an impressive whisper, “I am afraid for his health?”

“Why so, Mrs. Hudson?”

“Well, he is that strange, sir. After you was gone he walked and he walked, up and down, and up and down, until I was tired of the sound of his footstep. Then I heard him talking to himself and muttering, and every time the bell rang out he came on the stairhead, with ‘What is that, Mrs. Hudson?’ And now he has slammed off to his room, but I can hear him walking away the same as ever. I hope he is not going to be ill, sir. I ventured to say something to him about cooling medicine, but he turned on me, sir, with such a look that I do not know how ever I got out of the room.”

“I do not think that you have any cause to be uneasy, Mrs. Hudson,” I answered. “I have seen him like this before. He has some small matter upon his mind which makes him restless.” I tried to speak lightly to our worthy landlady, but I was myself somewhat uneasy when through the long night I still from time to time heard the dull sound of his tread, and knew how his keen spirit was chafing against this involuntary inaction.

At breakfast-time he looked worn and haggard, with a little fleck of feverish colour upon either cheek.

“You are knocking yourself up, old man,” I remarked. “I heard you marching about in the night.”

“No, I could not sleep,” he answered. “This hellish problem is consuming me. It is too much to be stopped by so small an obstacle, when all else had been overcome. I know the men, the launch, everything; and yet I can get no news. I have set other agencies at work, and used every means at my disposal. The whole river has been searched on either side, but there is no news, nor has Mrs. Smith heard of her husband. I shall come to the conclusion soon that they have scuttled the craft. But there are objections to that.”

“Or that Mrs. Smith has put us on a wrong scent.”

“No, I think that may be dismissed. I had inquiries made, and there is a launch of that description.”

“Could it have gone up the river?”

“I have considered that possibility too, and there is a search-party who will work up as far as Richmond. If no news comes to-day, I shall start off myself to-morrow, and go for the men rather than the boat. But surely, surely, we shall hear something.”

We did not, however. Not a word came to us from Wiggins or from the other agencies. There were articles in most of the papers about the Norwood tragedy. They all seemed rather hostile to the unfortunate Thaddeus Sholto. No fresh details were to be found in any of them, except that an inquest would be held on the following day. I walked over to Camberwell in the evening to report our bad success to the ladies. On my return I found Holmes dejected and somewhat morose. He would hardly reply to my questions. He busied himself all evening with an abstruse chemical analysis. It involved much heating of retorts and distilling of vapours. It ended at last in a smell which drove me out of the apartment. Up to the small hours of the morning I could hear the clinking of his test-tubes. That told me he was still engaged in his malodorous experiment.

In the early dawn I woke with a start. I was surprised to find him standing by my bedside. He was clad in a rude sailor dress with a pea-jacket, and a coarse red scarf round his neck.

“I am off down the river, Watson,” said he. “I have been turning it over in my mind. I can see only one way out of it. It is worth trying, at all events.”

“Surely I can come with you, then?” said I.

“No. You can be much more useful if you will remain here as my representative. I am loath to go. It is quite on the cards that some message may come during the day, though Wiggins was despondent about it last night. I want you to open all notes and telegrams. Act on your own judgment if any news should come. Can I rely upon you?”

“Most certainly.”

“I am afraid that you will not be able to wire to me. I can hardly tell yet where I may find myself. If I am in luck, however, I may not be gone so very long. I shall have news of some sort or other before I get back.”

I had heard nothing of him by breakfast-time. On opening the _Standard_, however, I found that there was a fresh allusion to the business. “With reference to the Upper Norwood tragedy,” it remarked, “we have reason to believe that the matter promises to be even more complex and mysterious than was originally supposed. Fresh evidence has shown that it is quite impossible that Mr. Thaddeus Sholto could have been in any way concerned in the matter. He and the housekeeper, Mrs. Bernstone, were both released yesterday evening. It is believed, however, that the police have a clue as to the real culprits. It is being prosecuted by Mr. Athelney Jones, of Scotland Yard, with all his well-known energy and sagacity. Further arrests may be expected at any moment.”

“That is satisfactory so far as it goes,” thought I. “Friend Sholto is safe, at any rate. I wonder what the fresh clue may be. It seems to be a stereotyped form whenever the police have made a blunder.”

I tossed the paper down upon the table. At that moment my eye caught an advertisement in the agony column. It ran in this way:

“Lost.—Whereas Mordecai Smith, boatman, and his son, Jim, left Smith’s Wharf at or about three o’clock last Tuesday morning in the steam launch _Aurora_, black with two red stripes, funnel black with a white band, the sum of five pounds will be paid to any one who can give information to Mrs. Smith, at Smith’s Wharf, or at 221_b_ Baker Street, as to the whereabouts of the said Mordecai Smith and the launch _Aurora_.”

This was clearly Holmes’s doing. The Baker Street address was enough to prove that. It struck me as rather ingenious. The fugitives might read it without seeing more than the natural anxiety of a wife for her missing husband.

It was a long day. Every time that a knock came to the door, or a sharp step passed in the street, I imagined that it was either Holmes returning or an answer to his advertisement. I tried to read, but my thoughts would wander off to our strange quest. They wandered to the ill-assorted and villainous pair whom we were pursuing. Could there be, I wondered, some radical flaw in my companion’s reasoning? Might he be suffering from some huge self-deception? Was it not possible that his nimble and speculative mind had built up this wild theory upon faulty premises? I had never known him to be wrong. And yet the keenest reasoner may occasionally be deceived. He was likely, I thought, to fall into error through the over-refinement of his logic. He preferred a subtle and bizarre explanation when a plainer and more commonplace one lay ready to his hand. Yet, on the other hand, I had myself seen the evidence. I had heard the reasons for his deductions. I looked back on the long chain of curious circumstances. Many of them were trivial in themselves, but all tended in the same direction. I could not disguise from myself that even if Holmes’s explanation were incorrect, the true theory must be equally _outré_ and startling.

At three o’clock in the afternoon there was a loud peal at the bell. There was an authoritative voice in the hall. To my surprise, no less a person than Mr. Athelney Jones was shown up to me. Very different was he, however, from the brusque and masterful professor of common sense who had taken over the case so confidently at Upper Norwood. His expression was downcast. His bearing was meek and even apologetic.

“Good-day, sir; good-day,” said he. “Mr. Sherlock Holmes is out, I understand.”

“Yes, and I cannot be sure when he will be back. But perhaps you would care to wait. Take that chair and try one of these cigars.”

“Thank you; I don’t mind if I do,” said he, mopping his face with a red bandanna handkerchief.

“And a whiskey-and-soda?”

“Well, half a glass. It is very hot for the time of year. I have had a good deal to worry and try me. You know my theory about this Norwood case?”

“I remember that you expressed one.”

“Well, I have been obliged to reconsider it. I had my net drawn tightly round Mr. Sholto, sir, when pop he went through a hole in the middle of it. He was able to prove an alibi which could not be shaken. From the time that he left his brother’s room he was never out of sight of some one or other. So it could not be he who climbed over roofs and through trap-doors. It’s a very dark case, and my professional credit is at stake. I should be very glad of a little assistance.”

“We all need help sometimes,” said I.

“Your friend Mr. Sherlock Holmes is a wonderful man, sir,” he said, in a low and secret voice. “He is a man who can not be beaten. I have known that young man go into a good many cases. But I never saw the case yet that he could not throw a light upon. He is not regular in his methods. And he is perhaps a little quick in jumping at theories. But on the whole, I think he would have made a most promising officer. And I do not care who knows it. I had a wire from him this morning. From it I understand that he has got some clue to this Sholto business. Here is the message.”

He took the telegram out of his pocket and handed it to me. It was dated from Poplar at twelve o’clock. “Go to Baker Street at once,” it said. “If I have not returned, wait for me. I am close on the track of the Sholto gang. You can come with us to-night if you want to be in at the finish.”

“This sounds well. He has clearly picked up the scent again,” said I.

“Ah, then he has been at fault too,” cried Jones, with clear satisfaction. “Even the best of us are thrown off sometimes. Of course this may prove to be a false alarm. But it is my duty as an officer of the law to allow no chance to slip. But there is some one at the door. Perhaps this is he.”

A heavy step was heard coming up the stair. There was a great wheezing and rattling, as from a man who was sorely put to it for breath. Once or twice he stopped, as though the climb were too much for him. But at last he made his way to our door and entered. His appearance matched the sounds which we had heard. He was an aged man, dressed in seafaring clothes, with an old pea-jacket buttoned up to his throat. His back was bowed, his knees were shaky, and his breathing was painfully asthmatic. He leaned upon a thick oaken cudgel. His shoulders heaved in the effort to draw the air into his lungs. He had a coloured scarf round his chin. I could see little of his face save a pair of keen dark eyes, overhung by bushy white brows, and long grey side-whiskers. Altogether he gave me the impression of a respectable master mariner who had fallen into years and poverty.

“What is it, my man?” I asked.

He looked about him in the slow methodical fashion of old age.

“Is Mr. Sherlock Holmes here?” he said.

“No; but I am acting for him. You can tell me any message you have for him.”

“It was to him himself I was to tell it,” he said.

“But I tell you that I am acting for him. Was it about Mordecai Smith’s boat?”

“Yes. I knows well where it is. And I knows where the men he is after are. And I knows where the treasure is. I knows all about it.”

“Then tell me, and I shall let him know.”

“It was to him I was to tell it,” he repeated, with the petulant obstinacy of a very old man.

“Well, you must wait for him.”

“No, no; I ain’t goin’ to lose a whole day to please no one. If Mr. Holmes ain’t here, then Mr. Holmes must find it all out for himself. I don’t care about the look of either of you, and I won’t tell a word.”

He shuffled towards the door, but Athelney Jones got in front of him.

“Wait a bit, my friend,” he said. “You have important information, and you must not walk off. We shall keep you, whether you like or not, until our friend returns.”

The old man made a little run towards the door. But Athelney Jones put his broad back up against it. Then the old man recognised the uselessness of resistance.

“Pretty sort of treatment this!” he cried, stamping his stick. “I come here to see a gentleman. And you two, who I never saw in my life, seize me and treat me in this fashion!”

“You will be none the worse,” I said. “We shall recompense you for the loss of your time. Sit over here on the sofa, and you will not have long to wait.”

He came across sullenly enough, and seated himself with his face resting on his hands. Jones and I resumed our cigars and our talk. Suddenly, however, Holmes’s voice broke in upon us.

“I think that you might offer me a cigar too,” he said.

We both started in our chairs. There was Holmes sitting close to us with an air of quiet amusement.

“Holmes!” I exclaimed. “You here! But where is the old man?”

“Here is the old man,” he said, holding out a heap of white hair. “Here he is,—wig, whiskers, eyebrows, and all. I thought my disguise was pretty good. But I hardly expected that it would stand that test.”

“Ah, you rogue!” cried Jones, highly delighted. “You would have made an actor, and a rare one. You had the proper workhouse cough. And those weak legs of yours are worth ten pounds a week. I thought I knew the glint of your eye, though. You didn’t get away from us so easily, you see.”

“I have been working in that get-up all day,” he said, lighting his cigar. “You see, a good many of the criminal classes begin to know me. Especially since our friend here took to publishing some of my cases. So I can only go on the war-path under some simple disguise like this. You got my wire?”

“Yes; that was what brought me here.”

“How has your case prospered?”

“It has all come to nothing. I have had to release two of my prisoners. And there is no evidence against the other two.”

“Never mind. We shall give you two others in the place of them. But you must put yourself under my orders. You are welcome to all the official credit. But you must act on the line that I point out. Is that agreed?”

“Entirely, if you will help me to the men.”

“Well, then, in the first place I shall want a fast police-boat—a steam launch—to be at the Westminster Stairs at seven o’clock.”

“That is easily managed. There is always one about there. But I can step across the road and telephone to make sure.”

“Then I shall want two stanch men, in case of resistance.”

“There will be two or three in the boat. What else?”

“When we secure the men we shall get the treasure. I think that it would be a pleasure to my friend here to take the box round to the young lady to whom half of it rightfully belongs. Let her be the first to open it.—Eh, Watson?”

“It would be a great pleasure to me.”

“This is quite an unusual way of doing things,” said Jones, shaking his head. “However, the whole thing is unusual, and I suppose we must close our eyes to it. Afterwards the treasure must be handed over to the authorities until the official investigation is over.”

“Certainly. That is easily arranged. One other point. I would very much like to have a few details about this matter from Jonathan Small himself. You know I like to work out the details of my cases. There is no problem with my having an informal meeting with him, either here in my rooms or somewhere else, as long as he is well guarded?”

“Well, you are in control of the situation. I have had no proof yet that this Jonathan Small exists. However, if you can catch him I do not see how I can refuse you a meeting with him.”

“That is agreed, then?”

“Perfectly. Is there anything else?”

“Only that I insist that you have dinner with us. It will be ready in half an hour. I have oysters and two grouse, with something a little special in white wines.—Watson, you have never yet appreciated my good qualities as a keeper of the house.”

CHAPTER 10. The End of the Islander

The End of the Islander

Our meal was a happy one. Holmes could talk very well when he wanted to, and that night he wanted to. He seemed to be in a state of nervous excitement. I have never known him so brilliant. He spoke on one quick subject after another—on miracle plays, on medieval pottery, on Stradivarius violins, on the Buddhism of Ceylon, and on the war-ships of the future. He handled each one as if he had made a special study of it. His bright humour showed the reaction from his black depression of the days before. Athelney Jones turned out to be a friendly soul in his hours of rest, and faced his dinner with the air of a man who loved good living. For myself, I felt happy at the thought that we were nearing the end of our task, and I caught something of Holmes's high spirits. None of us spoke during dinner of the cause which had brought us together.

When the cloth was cleared, Holmes looked at his watch, and filled three glasses with port. "One full glass," said he, "to the success of our little journey. And now it is high time we were off. Do you have a pistol, Watson?"

"I have my old service-revolver in my desk."

"You had best take it, then. It is good to be prepared. I see that the cab is at the door. I ordered it for half-past six."

It was a little past seven before we reached the Westminster wharf, and found our launch waiting for us. Holmes looked at it closely.

"Is there anything to mark it as a police-boat?"

"Yes—that green lamp at the side."

"Then take it off."

The small change was made, we stepped on board, and the ropes were cast off. Jones, Holmes, and I sat in the back. There was one man at the steering wheel, one to tend the engines, and two strong police-inspectors in front.

"Where to?" asked Jones.

"To the Tower. Tell them to stop opposite Jacobson's Yard."

Our boat was clearly a very fast one. We shot past the long lines of loaded barges as though they were standing still. Holmes smiled with satisfaction as we caught up with a river steamer and left her behind us.

"We ought to be able to catch anything on the river," he said.

"Well, hardly that. But there are not many launches to beat us."

"We shall have to catch the Aurora, and she has a name for being a fast sailer. I will tell you how the matter stands, Watson. You remember how annoyed I was at being stopped by so small a thing?"

"Yes."

"Well, I gave my mind a complete rest by throwing myself into a chemical analysis. One of our greatest statesmen has said that a change of work is the best rest. So it is. When I had succeeded in dissolving the hydrocarbon which I was at work at, I came back to our problem of the Sholtos, and thought the whole matter out again. My boys had been up the river and down the river without result. The launch was not at any landing-stage or wharf, nor had it returned. Yet it could hardly have been sunk to hide their tracks—though that always remained as a possible idea if all else failed. I knew this man Small had a certain degree of low cunning, but I did not think him capable of anything in the nature of delicate skill. That is usually a product of higher education. I then reflected that since he had certainly been in London some time—as we had evidence that he kept a continual watch over Pondicherry Lodge—he could hardly leave at a moment's notice. He would need some little time, if it were only a day, to arrange his affairs. That was the balance of probability, at any rate."

"It seems to me to be a little weak," said I. "It is more probable that he had arranged his affairs before ever he set out upon his journey."

"No, I hardly think so. This hiding place of his would be too valuable a retreat in case of need for him to give it up until he was sure that he could do without it. But a second thought struck me. Jonathan Small must have felt that the peculiar appearance of his companion, however much he may have covered him up, would give rise to talk, and possibly be connected with this Norwood tragedy. He was quite sharp enough to see that. They had started from their head-quarters under cover of darkness, and he would wish to get back before it was broad light. Now, it was past three o'clock, according to Mrs. Smith, when they got the boat. It would be quite bright, and people would be about in an hour or so. Therefore, I argued, they did not go very far. They paid Smith well to hold his tongue, reserved his launch for the final escape, and hurried to their rooms with the treasure-box. In a couple of nights, when they had time to see what view the papers took, and whether there was any suspicion, they would make their way under cover of darkness to some ship at Gravesend or in the Downs, where no doubt they had already arranged for passages to America or the Colonies."

"But the launch? They could not have taken that to their rooms."

"Quite so. I argued that the launch must be no great way off, in spite of its invisibility. I then put myself in the place of Small, and looked at it as a man of his ability would. He would probably consider that to send back the launch or to keep it at a wharf would make pursuit easy if the police did happen to get on his track. How, then, could he hide the launch and yet have her at hand when wanted? I wondered what I should do myself if I were in his place. I could only think of one way of doing it. I might hand the launch over to some boat-builder or repairer, with directions to make a small change in her. She would then be removed to his shed or yard, and so be effectively hidden, while at the same time I could have her at a few hours' notice."

"That seems simple enough."

“It is just these very simple things which are very easy to miss. However, I decided to act on the idea. I started at once in these harmless clothes of a sailor and asked at all the yards down the river. I found nothing at fifteen, but at the sixteenth—Jacobson’s—I learned that the _Aurora_ had been handed over to them two days ago by a man with a wooden leg. He gave some small directions about her steering. ‘There is nothing wrong with her steering,’ said the boss. ‘There she lies, with the red marks.’ At that moment who should come down but Mordecai Smith, the missing owner? He was rather drunk. I should not, of course, have known him, but he shouted out his name and the name of his boat. ‘I want her tonight at eight,’ said he,—‘eight sharp, mind, for I have two gentlemen who will not be kept waiting.’ They had clearly paid him well, for he had plenty of money, throwing coins about to the men. I followed him some distance, but he went into a beer house: so I went back to the yard. On the way I happened to pick up one of my boys, and I placed him as a guard over the boat. He is to stand at the edge of the water and wave his cloth to us when they start. We shall be lying off in the stream, and it will be a strange thing if we do not take men, treasure, and all.”

“You have planned it all very well, whether they are the right men or not,” said Jones; “but if the affair were in my hands I should have had a body of police in Jacobson’s Yard, and arrested them when they came down.”

“Which would have been never. This man Small is a pretty smart fellow. He would send a scout on ahead, and if anything made him feel doubt lie quiet for another week.”

“But you might have stuck to Mordecai Smith, and so been led to their hiding-place,” said I.

“In that case I should have wasted my day. I think that it is a hundred to one against Smith knowing where they live. As long as he has drink and good pay, why should he ask questions? They send him messages what to do. No, I thought over every possible course, and this is the best.”

While this conversation had been going on, we had been passing the long series of bridges which cross the Thames. As we passed the City the last rays of the sun were lighting the cross upon the top of St. Paul’s. It was dusk before we reached the Tower.

“That is Jacobson’s Yard,” said Holmes, pointing to a thick cluster of poles and ropes on the Surrey side. “Cruise gently up and down here under cover of this string of flat boats.” He took a pair of night-glasses from his pocket and gazed some time at the shore. “I see my guard at his post,” he remarked, “but no sign of a cloth.”

“Suppose we go down-stream a short way and lie in wait for them,” said Jones, with strong feeling. We were all eager by this time, even the policemen and firemen, who had a very unclear idea of what was going forward.

“We have no right to take anything for granted,” Holmes answered. “It is certainly ten to one that they go down-stream, but we cannot be certain. From this point we can see the entrance of the yard, and they can hardly see us. It will be a clear night and plenty of light. We must stay where we are. See how the people crowd over there in the gaslight.”

“They are coming from work in the yard.”

“Dirty-looking bad men, but I suppose every one has some little deathless spark hidden about him. You would not think it, to look at them. There is no obvious chance of it. A strange puzzle is man!”

“Some one calls him a soul hidden in an animal,” I suggested.

“Winwood Reade is good upon the subject,” said Holmes. “He remarks that, while the individual man is an unsolvable puzzle, in the whole he becomes a sure thing in numbers. You can, for example, never say beforehand what any one man will do, but you can say with exactness what an average number will be up to. Individuals vary, but percentages remain constant. So says the statistician. But do I see a cloth? Surely there is a white flutter over there.”

“Yes, it is your boy,” I cried. “I can see him plainly.”

“And there is the _Aurora_,” exclaimed Holmes, “and going like the devil! Full speed ahead, engineer. Make after that boat with the yellow light. By heaven, I shall never forgive myself if she proves to have the heels of us!”

She had slipped unseen through the yard-entrance and passed behind two or three small craft, so that she had fairly got her speed up before we saw her. Now she was flying down the stream, near in to the shore, going at a tremendous rate. Jones looked gravely at her and shook his head.

“She is very fast,” he said. “I doubt if we shall catch her.”

“We _must_ catch her!” cried Holmes, between his teeth. “Heap it on, firemen! Make her do all she can! If we burn the boat we must have them!”

We were fairly after her now. The furnaces roared, and the powerful engines whizzed and clanked, like a great metal heart. Her sharp, steep front cut through the river-water and sent two rolling waves to right and to left of us. With every beat of the engines we sprang and shook like a living thing. One great yellow lantern in our front threw a long, shaking funnel of light in front of us. Right ahead a dark blur upon the water showed where the _Aurora_ lay, and the swirl of white foam behind her spoke of the pace at which she was going. We flashed past barges, steamers, merchant-vessels, in and out, behind this one and round the other. Voices hailed us out of the darkness, but still the _Aurora_ thundered on, and still we followed close upon her track.

“Pile it on, men, pile it on!” cried Holmes, looking down into the engine-room, while the fierce glow from below beat upon his eager, thin face. “Get every pound of steam you can.”

“I think we gain a little,” said Jones, with his eyes on the _Aurora_.

“I am sure of it,” said I. “We shall be up with her in a very few minutes.”

At that moment, however, as our evil fate would have it, a small pulling boat with three flat boats tied behind it came suddenly between us. We only avoided a crash by turning our steering wheel hard. Before we could get around them and find our way again, the _Aurora_ had gained a good two hundred yards. She was still, however, well in view. The dark, uncertain evening light was turning into a clear night full of stars. Our engines were pushed to their limit. The thin shell of our boat shook and made noise from the fierce power that was driving us along. We had shot through the Pool, past the West India Docks, down the long Deptford Reach, and up again after rounding the Isle of Dogs. The grey shape in front of us now became clear enough as the neat _Aurora_. Jones turned our search-light upon her, so that we could clearly see the figures on her deck. One man sat by the back, with something black between his knees over which he bent. Beside him lay a dark mass which looked like a Newfoundland dog. The boy held the steering handle. Against the red light of the fire box I could see old Smith, bare to the waist, shovelling coal as fast as he could. They may have had some doubt at first about whether we were really following them. But now, as we followed every turn they took, there could no longer be any question about it. At Greenwich we were about three hundred paces behind them. At Blackwall we could not have been more than two hundred and fifty. I have chased many creatures in many countries during my varied life, but never did sport give me such a wild excitement as this mad, flying man-hunt down the Thames. Steadily we drew closer to them, yard by yard. In the silence of the night we could hear the panting and clanking of their engines. The man in the back still bent low on the deck. His arms were moving as though he were busy. Every now and then he would look up and measure with a glance the distance which still separated us. Nearer we came and nearer. Jones yelled to them to stop. We were not more than four boat lengths behind them, both boats flying at a tremendous pace. It was a clear stretch of the river, with Barking Level upon one side and the sad Plumstead Marshes upon the other. At our shout the man in the back sprang up from the deck and shook his two closed fists at us. He was cursing in a high, cracked voice. He was a good-sized, powerful man. As he stood balancing himself with legs apart, I could see that from the thigh downwards there was only a wooden stump upon the right side. At the sound of his loud, angry cries there was movement in the huddled bundle upon the deck. It straightened itself into a little black man—the smallest I have ever seen—with a great, badly shaped head and a shock of tangled, messy hair. Holmes had already drawn his revolver, and I pulled out mine at the sight of this wild, twisted creature. He was wrapped in some sort of dark overcoat or blanket, which left only his face exposed. But that face was enough to give a man a sleepless night. Never have I seen features so deeply marked with all animal nature and cruelty. His small eyes glowed and burned with a dark light. His thick lips were pulled back from his teeth, which grinned and chattered at us with a half animal fury.

“Fire if he raises his hand,” said Holmes, quietly. We were within a boat length by this time, and almost within touch of our quarry. I can see the two of them now as they stood, the white man with his legs far apart, shrieking out curses, and the unholy dwarf with his hideous face, and his strong yellow teeth biting at us in the light of our lantern.

It was well that we had so clear a view of him. Even as we looked he pulled out from under his covering a short, round piece of wood, like a school ruler, and put it to his lips. Our guns rang out together. He whirled round, threw up his arms, and with a kind of choking cough fell sideways into the stream. I caught one glimpse of his hateful, threatening eyes amid the white swirl of the waters. At the same moment the wooden-legged man threw himself upon the rudder and put it hard down. His boat made straight in for the southern bank, while we shot past her back, only clearing her by a few feet. We were round after her in an instant, but she was already nearly at the bank. It was a wild and empty place, where the moon glimmered upon a wide expanse of marsh-land, with pools of still water and beds of rotting plants. The boat with a dull thud ran up upon the mud-bank, with her front in the air and her back level with the water. The fleeing man sprang out, but his stump instantly sank its whole length into the wet soil. In vain he struggled and twisted. Not one step could he possibly take either forwards or backwards. He yelled in powerless anger, and kicked wildly into the mud with his other foot. But his struggles only pushed his wooden leg deeper into the sticky bank. When we brought our boat alongside he was so firmly stuck that we could only pull him out by throwing the end of a rope over his shoulders. We dragged him, like some evil fish, over our side. The two Smiths, father and son, sat silently in their boat, but came aboard quietly enough when commanded. The _Aurora_ herself we pulled off and tied to our back. A solid iron chest of Indian work stood upon the deck. This, there could be no question, was the same that had held the bad-luck treasure of the Sholtos. There was no key, but it was quite heavy, so we moved it carefully to our own little cabin. As we steamed slowly up-stream again, we flashed our search-light in every direction, but there was no sign of the Islander. Somewhere in the dark mud at the bottom of the Thames lie the bones of that strange visitor to our shores.

“See here,” said Holmes, pointing to the wooden hatchway. “We were hardly quick enough with our guns.” There, sure enough, just behind where we had been standing, stuck one of those murderous darts which we knew so well. It must have flown between us at the instant that we fired. Holmes smiled at it and shrugged his shoulders in his easy way. But I confess that it turned me sick to think of the horrible death which had passed so close to us that night.

CHAPTER 11. The Great Agra Treasure

Our prisoner sat in the cabin opposite the iron box. He had done so much and waited so long to get it. He was a man with skin darkened by the sun and wild eyes. Lines and small folds covered his dark reddish-brown face. They told of a hard life in the open air. His chin, which had a beard, stood out in a strange way. This showed a man who would not easily be turned from his purpose. He may have been fifty years old or about that, because his black, curling hair was mixed with much grey. His face when calm was not unpleasant. But his heavy eyebrows and strong chin gave him a terrible look when he was angry, as I had seen lately. He sat now with his hands tied in metal bands upon his lap. His head sank upon his chest. He looked with his sharp, bright eyes at the box that had caused his bad deeds. It seemed to me that there was more sadness than anger in his stiff and controlled face. Once he looked up at me with a small light in his eyes that was like a joke.

“Well, Jonathan Small,” said Holmes, lighting a cigar, “I am sorry that it has come to this.”

“And so am I, sir,” he answered, honestly. “I do not believe that I can escape the job. I give you my word on the book that I never raised a hand against Mr. Sholto. It was that little hell-hound Tonga who shot one of his cursed darts into him. I had no part in it, sir. I was as sad as if it had been my own blood relation. I hit the little devil with the loose end of the rope for it. But it was done, and I could not undo it again.”

“Have a cigar,” said Holmes; “and you had best take a drink from my flask, for you are very wet. How could you expect so small and weak a man as this black fellow to overpower Mr. Sholto and hold him while you were climbing the rope?”

“You seem to know as much about it as if you were there, sir. The truth is that I hoped to find the room empty. I knew the habits of the house pretty well. It was the time when Mr. Sholto usually went down to his supper. I shall make no secret of the business. The best defence that I can make is just the simple truth. Now, if it had been the old major I would have swung for him with a light heart. I would have thought no more of knifing him than of smoking this cigar. But it is cursed hard that I should be caught over this young Sholto, with whom I had no quarrel at all.”

“You are under the charge of Mr. Athelney Jones, of Scotland Yard. He is going to bring you up to my rooms, and I shall ask you for a true account of the matter. You must tell the whole truth, for if you do I hope that I may be of use to you. I think I can prove that the poison acts so quickly that the man was dead before ever you reached the room.”

“That he was, sir. I never got such a shock in my life as when I saw him grinning at me with his head on his shoulder as I climbed through the window. It fairly shook me, sir. I would have half killed Tonga for it if he had not scrambled off. That was how he came to leave his club, and some of his darts too, as he tells me. I dare say that helped to put you on our track. Though how you kept on it is more than I can tell. I do not feel any hate against you for it. But it does seem a strange thing,” he added, with a bitter smile, “that I who have a fair claim to nearly half a million of money should spend the first half of my life building a breakwater in the Andamans, and am likely to spend the other half digging drains at Dartmoor. It was an evil day for me when first I set eyes on the merchant Achmet and had to do with the Agra treasure. It never brought anything but a curse yet upon the man who owned it. To him it brought murder, to Major Sholto it brought fear and guilt, to me it has meant slavery for life.”

At this moment Athelney Jones pushed his broad face and heavy shoulders into the tiny cabin. “Quite a family party,” he remarked. “I think I shall have a drink from that flask, Holmes. Well, I think we may all congratulate each other. It is a pity we did not take the other alive; but there was no choice. I say, Holmes, you must confess that you cut it rather fine. It was all we could do to catch her.”

“All is well that ends well,” said Holmes. “But I certainly did not know that the _Aurora_ was such a fast ship.”

“Smith says she is one of the fastest launches on the river. He says that if he had had another man to help him with the engines we should never have caught her. He swears he knew nothing of this Norwood business.”

“Neither he did,” cried our prisoner,—“not a word. I chose his launch because I heard that she was a fast one. We told him nothing, but we paid him well. He was to get something handsome if we reached our vessel, the _Esmeralda_, at Gravesend, outward bound for the Brazils.”

“Well, if he has done no wrong we shall see that no wrong comes to him. If we are pretty quick in catching our men, we are not so quick in condemning them.” It was amusing to notice how the self-important Jones was already beginning to give himself airs on the strength of the capture. From the slight smile which played over Sherlock Holmes’s face, I could see that the speech had not been lost upon him.

“We will be at Vauxhall Bridge presently,” said Jones, “and shall land you, Dr. Watson, with the treasure-box. I need hardly tell you that I am taking a very grave responsibility upon myself in doing this. It is most irregular; but of course an agreement is an agreement. I must, however, as a matter of duty, send an inspector with you, since you have so valuable a charge. You will drive, no doubt?”

“Yes, I shall drive.”

“It is a pity there is no key, that we may make a list first. You will have to break it open. Where is the key, my man?”

“At the bottom of the river,” said Small, shortly.

“Hum! There was no use your giving this unnecessary trouble. We have had work enough already through you. However, doctor, I need not warn you to be careful. Bring the box back with you to the Baker Street rooms. You will find us there, on our way to the station.”

They brought me to Vauxhall with my heavy iron box. A friendly inspector came with me. A short drive of fifteen minutes took us to Mrs. Cecil Forrester's. The servant seemed surprised to see a visitor so late. She explained that Mrs. Cecil Forrester was out for the evening. She would likely be very late. Miss Morstan was in the drawing-room, however. So I went to the drawing-room with the box in my hand. I left the kind inspector in the cab.

She was sitting by the open window. She wore a kind of thin white material with a little red at the neck and waist. The soft light of a shaded lamp fell on her as she leaned back in the basket chair. The light played over her sweet, serious face. It gave a dull, metal-like shine to the thick waves of her rich hair. One white arm and hand hung over the side of the chair. Her whole pose and figure spoke of a deep sadness. At the sound of my step she jumped to her feet, however. A bright red of surprise and pleasure came to her pale cheeks.

"I heard a cab drive up," she said. "I thought that Mrs. Forrester had come back very early. But I never dreamed that it might be you. What news have you brought me?"

"I have brought something better than news," I said. I put down the box on the table. I spoke in a happy, loud way, though my heart was heavy inside me. "I have brought you something worth all the news in the world. I have brought you a fortune."

She looked at the iron box. "Is that the treasure, then?" she asked, in a calm enough way.

"Yes, this is the great Agra treasure. Half of it is yours and half is Thaddeus Sholto's. You will have a couple of hundred thousand each. Think of that! Ten thousand pounds a year. There will be few richer young ladies in England. Is it not wonderful?"

I think I must have been acting my joy too strongly. She must have heard a false note in my kind words. I saw her eyebrows rise a little, and she looked at me with interest.

"If I have it," she said, "I owe it to you."

"No, no," I answered. "Not to me, but to my friend Sherlock Holmes. With all the will in the world, I could never have followed a clue that even his careful mind found hard. As it was, we very nearly lost it at the last moment."

"Please sit down and tell me all about it, Dr. Watson," she said.

I told her briefly what had happened since I last saw her. I told of Holmes's new way of searching, the discovery of the _Aurora_, the arrival of Athelney Jones, our trip in the evening, and the wild chase down the Thames. She listened with open lips and shining eyes as I told our adventures. When I spoke of the arrow that had so nearly hit us, she turned so white that I feared she would faint.

"It is nothing," she said, as I hurried to pour her some water. "I am all right again. It was a shock to hear that I had put my friends in such terrible danger."

"That is all over," I answered. "It was nothing. I will tell you no more sad details. Let us turn to something brighter. There is the treasure. What could be brighter than that? I got leave to bring it with me. I thought it would interest you to be the first to see it."

"It would interest me very much," she said. There was no excitement in her voice, however. It had surely struck her that it might seem unkind to show no interest in a prize that had cost so much to win.

"What a pretty box!" she said, bending over it. "This is Indian work, I suppose?"

"Yes, it is Benares metal-work."

"And so heavy!" she exclaimed, trying to lift it. "The box alone must be of some value. Where is the key?"

"Small threw it into the Thames," I answered. "I must borrow Mrs. Forrester's poker." At the front there was a thick, broad metal bar made in the shape of a sitting Buddha. Under this I pushed the end of the poker and twisted it outward as a lever. The bar sprang open with a loud snap. With shaking fingers I threw back the lid. We both stood looking in surprise. The box was empty!

No wonder it was heavy. The iron was two-thirds of an inch thick all around. It was huge, well made, and solid, like a chest built to carry things of great price. But not one shred or crumb of metal or jewelry lay inside it. It was completely and totally empty.

"The treasure is lost," said Miss Morstan, calmly.

As I listened to the words and understood what they meant, a great shadow seemed to pass from my soul. I did not know how this Agra treasure had weighed me down until now that it was finally gone. It was selfish, no doubt, disloyal, wrong. But I could feel nothing except that the golden barrier was gone from between us. "Thank God!" I said from my very heart.

She looked at me with a quick, questioning smile. "Why do you say that?" she asked.

"Because you are within my reach again," I said, taking her hand. She did not pull it away. "Because I love you, Mary, as truly as ever a man loved a woman. Because this treasure, these riches, sealed my lips. Now that they are gone I can tell you how I love you. That is why I said, 'Thank God.'"

"Then I say, 'Thank God,' too," she whispered, as I drew her to my side. Whoever had lost a treasure, I knew that night that I had gained one.

CHAPTER 12. The Strange Story of Jonathan Small（1）

That inspector in the cab was a very patient man. It was a long, tiring time before I came back to him. His face turned dark when I showed him the empty box.

“There goes the reward!” he said sadly. “Where there is no money, there is no pay. Tonight’s work would have been worth ten pounds each to Sam Brown and me if the treasure had been there.”

“Mr. Thaddeus Sholto is a rich man,” I said. “He will make sure you are rewarded, with or without the treasure.”

The inspector shook his head in disappointment. “It’s a bad business,” he said again. “And Mr. Athelney Jones will think so too.”

He was right. The detective looked quite empty-faced when I got to Baker Street and showed him the empty box. They had just arrived — Holmes, the prisoner, and he — because they had changed their plans and reported at a station on the way. My friend sat back in his arm-chair with his usual tired, uninterested look. Small sat stiffly opposite him, with his wooden leg crossed over his good one. When I showed the empty box, he leaned back in his chair and laughed out loud.

“This is your doing, Small,” said Athelney Jones, in an angry voice.

“Yes, I have put it somewhere where you will never lay a hand on it,” he cried, in triumph. “It is my treasure. And if I can’t have the stolen goods, I’ll take good care that no one else does. I tell you that no living man has any right to it, except three men in the Andaman convict buildings and myself. I know now that I cannot use it, and I know that they cannot. I have acted all along for them as much as for myself. It has always been the sign of four with us. I well know that they would have had me do just what I have done. They would have had me throw the treasure into the Thames rather than let it go to friends or family of Sholto or of Morstan. We did not do for Achmet to make them rich. You will find the treasure where the key is, and where little Tonga is. When I saw that your boat must catch us, I put the stolen goods away in a safe place. There are no rupees for you this journey.”

“You are deceiving us, Small,” said Athelney Jones, in a strict voice. “If you had wished to throw the treasure into the Thames, it would have been easier for you to throw box and all.”

“Easier for me to throw, and easier for you to find again,” he answered, with a sharp, sideways look. “The man who was clever enough to hunt me down is clever enough to pick an iron box from the bottom of a river. Now that they are spread over five miles or so, it may be a harder job. It went to my heart to do it, though. I was half mad when you came up with us. However, there is no good grieving over it. I have had good times in my life, and I have had bad times, but I have learned not to cry over spilled milk.”

“This is a very serious matter, Small,” said the detective. “If you had helped justice, instead of blocking it in this way, you would have had a better chance at your trial.”

“Justice!” snarled the former convict. “A pretty justice! Whose stolen goods are these, if they are not ours? Where is the justice that I should give it up to those who have never earned it? Look how I have earned it! Twenty long years in that fever-filled swamp, all day at work under the mangrove-tree, all night chained up in the filthy convict huts, bitten by mosquitoes, racked with ague, bullied by every cursed black-faced policeman who loved to take it out of a white man. That was how I earned the Agra treasure. And you talk to me of justice because I cannot bear to feel that I have paid this price only so that another may enjoy it! I would rather hang a score of times, or have one of Tonga’s darts in my skin, than live in a convict’s cell and feel that another man is at his ease in a palace with the money that should be mine.” Small had dropped his mask of calm, and all this came out in a wild rush of words. His eyes blazed, and the handcuffs clanked together as his hands moved with passion. As I saw the fury and the passion of the man, I could understand that it was no groundless or unnatural terror which had possessed Major Sholto when he first learned that the injured convict was upon his track.

“You forget that we know nothing of all this,” said Holmes quietly. “We have not heard your story, and we cannot tell how far justice may originally have been on your side.”

“Well, sir, you have been very fair-spoken to me, though I can see that I have you to thank that I have these bracelets upon my wrists. Still, I bear no grudge for that. It is all fair and above-board. If you want to hear my story, I have no wish to hold it back. What I say to you is God’s truth, every word of it. Thank you; you can put the glass beside me here, and I’ll put my lips to it if I am dry.

“I am a Worcestershire man myself, born near Pershore. I dare say you would find a heap of Smalls living there now if you were to look. I have often thought of taking a look round there, but the truth is that I was never much of a credit to the family, and I doubt if they would be so very glad to see me. They were all steady, chapel-going folk, small farmers, well-known and respected over the country-side, while I was always a bit of a rover. At last, however, when I was about eighteen, I gave them no more trouble, for I got into a mess over a girl, and could only get out of it again by taking the Queen’s shilling and joining the 3rd Buffs, which was just starting for India.

"I was not meant to do much soldiering, however. I had just got past the goose-step, and learned to handle my gun, when I was foolish enough to go swimming in the Ganges. Luckily for me, my company sergeant, John Holder, was in the water at the same time. He was one of the finest swimmers in the service. A crocodile took me just as I was half-way across. It bit off my right leg as clean as a doctor could have done it, just above the knee. From the shock and the loss of blood, I fainted. I should have drowned if Holder had not caught hold of me and paddled for the bank. I was five months in hospital over it. When at last I was able to limp out with this wooden toe strapped to my stump, I found myself sent out of the army as an invalid and unfit for any active work.

"I was, as you can imagine, pretty low in my luck at this time. I was a useless cripple, though not yet in my twentieth year. However, my bad luck soon proved to be a blessing in disguise. A man named Abel White, who had come out there as a grower of indigo, wanted a manager to look after his workers and keep them at their work. He happened to be a friend of our colonel, who had taken an interest in me since the accident. To make a long story short, the colonel recommended me strongly for the post. The work was mostly to be done on horseback, so my leg was no great problem. I had enough knee left to keep a good grip on the saddle. What I had to do was to ride over the estate, to keep an eye on the men as they worked, and to report the lazy ones. The pay was fair, I had comfortable rooms, and altogether I was content to spend the rest of my life in growing indigo. Mr. Abel White was a kind man. He would often drop into my little hut and smoke a pipe with me. White folk out there feel their hearts warm to each other as they never do here at home.

"Well, I was never lucky for long. Suddenly, without a note of warning, the great mutiny broke upon us. One month India lay as still and peaceful, to all appearance, as Surrey or Kent. The next there were two hundred thousand black devils let loose, and the country was a perfect hell. Of course you know all about it, gentlemen—a deal more than I do, very like, since reading is not in my line. I only know what I saw with my own eyes. Our estate was at a place called Muttra, near the border of the Northwest Provinces. Night after night the whole sky was lit up with the burning houses, and day after day we had small groups of Europeans passing through our land with their wives and children, on their way to Agra, where the nearest troops were. Mr. Abel White was a stubborn man. He had it in his head that the affair had been exaggerated, and that it would blow over as suddenly as it had sprung up. There he sat on his veranda, drinking whiskey and smoking cigars, while the country was in a blaze about him. Of course we stuck by him, I and Dawson, who, with his wife, used to do the book-work and the managing. Well, one fine day the crash came. I had been away on a distant estate, and was riding slowly home in the evening. Then my eye fell upon something all huddled together at the bottom of a steep valley. I rode down to see what it was. The cold struck through my heart when I found it was Dawson's wife, all cut into ribbons, and half eaten by wild dogs and native dogs. A little further up the road Dawson himself was lying on his face, quite dead, with an empty revolver in his hand and four Sepoys lying across each other in front of him. I pulled up my horse, wondering which way I should turn. At that moment I saw thick smoke curling up from Abel White's house and the flames beginning to burst through the roof. I knew then that I could do my employer no good. I would only throw my own life away if I interfered in the matter. From where I stood I could see hundreds of the black fiends, with their red coats still on their backs, dancing and howling round the burning house. Some of them pointed at me, and a couple of bullets sang past my head. So I broke away across the paddy-fields, and found myself late at night safe within the walls at Agra.

"As it proved, however, there was no great safety there, either. The whole country was up like a swarm of bees. Wherever the English could collect in little bands they held just the ground that their guns commanded. Everywhere else they were helpless fugitives. It was a fight of the millions against the hundreds. The cruellest part of it was that these men that we fought against, foot, horse, and gunners, were our own picked troops. We had taught and trained them. They were handling our own weapons, and blowing our own bugle-calls. At Agra there were the 3rd Bengal Fusiliers, some Sikhs, two troops of horse, and a battery of artillery. A volunteer corps of clerks and merchants had been formed, and this I joined, wooden leg and all. We went out to meet the rebels at Shahgunge early in July. We beat them back for a time, but our powder gave out, and we had to fall back upon the city.

"Nothing but the worst news came to us from every side. This is not to be wondered at, for if you look at the map you will see that we were right in the heart of it. Lucknow is rather better than a hundred miles to the east, and Cawnpore about as far to the south. From every point on the compass there was nothing but torture and murder and outrage."

“The city of Agra is a great place, full of fanatics and fierce devil-worshippers of all sorts. Our small group of men were lost among the narrow, winding streets. So our leader moved across the river. He took up his position in the old fort at Agra. I do not know if any of you gentlemen have ever read or heard anything of that old fort. It is a very strange place. It is the strangest place I was ever in, and I have been in some strange corners too. First of all, it is huge in size. I should think the walled area must be acres and acres. There is a modern part. It took all our fort soldiers, women, children, supplies, and everything else, with plenty of room left over. But the modern part is nothing like the size of the old quarter. Nobody goes there, and it is given over to the scorpions and the centipedes. It is all full of great empty halls, and winding passages, and long corridors twisting in and out. So it is easy enough for people to get lost in it. For this reason people seldom went into it, though now and again a group with torches might go exploring.

“The river runs along the front of the old fort, and so protects it. But on the sides and behind there are many doors. These had to be guarded, of course, in the old quarter as well as in the part actually held by our troops. We were short of men. We hardly had enough men to guard the corners of the building and to serve the guns. So it was impossible for us to station a strong guard at every one of the many gates. What we did was to set up a central guard-house in the middle of the fort. We left each gate in the charge of one white man and two or three local men. I was chosen to take charge during certain hours of the night of a small isolated door on the southwest side of the building. Two Sikh soldiers were placed under my command. I was told that if anything went wrong I should fire my gun. Then I could rely on help coming at once from the central guard. However, the guard was a good two hundred paces away. And the space between was cut up into a maze of passages and corridors. So I had great doubts whether they could arrive in time to be of any use in case of an actual attack.

“Well, I was pretty proud at having this small command given me, since I was a new recruit, and a lame one at that. For two nights I kept the watch with my Punjaubees. They were tall, fierce-looking men, Mahomet Singh and Abdullah Khan by name. Both were old fighting-men who had carried arms against us at Chilian-wallah. They could talk English pretty well, but I could get little out of them. They preferred to stand together and talk all night in their strange Sikh language. For myself, I used to stand outside the gate, looking down on the broad, winding river and on the twinkling lights of the great city. The beating of drums, the sound of tomtoms, and the yells and cries of the rebels, drunk with opium and with bang, were enough to remind us all night of our dangerous neighbours across the stream. Every two hours the officer of the night used to come round to all the posts, to make sure that all was well.

“The third night of my watch was dark and dirty, with a small, driving rain. It was dull work standing in the gate hour after hour in such weather. I tried again and again to make my Sikhs talk, but without much success. At two in the morning the rounds passed, and broke for a moment the tiredness of the night. Finding that my companions would not be led into conversation, I took out my pipe, and laid down my gun to strike the match. In an instant the two Sikhs were upon me. One of them grabbed my gun up and aimed it at my head. The other held a great knife to my throat and swore between his teeth that he would push it into me if I moved a step.

“My first thought was that these fellows were in league with the rebels, and that this was the beginning of an assault. If our door were in the hands of the Sepoys the place must fall, and the women and children be treated as they were in Cawnpore. Maybe you gentlemen think that I am just making out a case for myself, but I give you my word that when I thought of that, though I felt the point of the knife at my throat, I opened my mouth with the intention of giving a scream, if it was my last one, which might alarm the main guard. The man who held me seemed to know my thoughts. For, even as I braced myself to it, he whispered, ‘Do not make a noise. The fort is safe enough. There are no rebel dogs on this side of the river.’ There was the ring of truth in what he said, and I knew that if I raised my voice I was a dead man. I could read it in the fellow’s brown eyes. So I waited in silence, to see what it was that they wanted from me.

“‘Listen to me, Sahib,’ said the taller and fiercer of the pair, the one whom they called Abdullah Khan. ‘You must either be with us now or you must be silenced forever. The thing is too great a one for us to hesitate. Either you are heart and soul with us on your oath on the cross of the Christians, or your body this night shall be thrown into the ditch and we shall pass over to our brothers in the rebel army. There is no middle way. Which is it to be, death or life? We can only give you three minutes to decide, for the time is passing, and all must be done before the rounds come again.’

“‘How can I decide?’ said I. ‘You have not told me what you want of me. But I tell you now that if it is anything against the safety of the fort I will have no truck with it, so you can drive home your knife and welcome.’

“‘It is nothing against the fort,’ said he. ‘We only ask you to do that which your countrymen come to this land for. We ask you to be rich. If you will be one of us this night, we will swear to you upon the naked knife, and by the threefold oath which no Sikh was ever known to break, that you shall have your fair share of the loot. A quarter of the treasure shall be yours. We can say no fairer.’

"'But what is the treasure, then?' I asked. 'I am as ready to be rich as you are, if you will just show me how it can be done.'

"'You will swear, then,' he said, 'by the bones of your father, by the good name of your mother, by the cross of your faith. You will swear to raise no hand and speak no word against us, either now or later?'

"'I will swear it,' I answered, 'if the fort is not put in danger.'

"'Then my friend and I will swear that you shall have a quarter of the treasure. It shall be divided equally among the four of us.'

"'There are only three,' I said.

"'No; Dost Akbar must have his share. We can tell the tale to you while we wait for them. Do you stand at the gate, Mahomet Singh, and give notice of their coming. The thing stands thus, Sahib. I tell it to you because I know that a promise is binding upon a Feringhee, and that we may trust you. If you had been a lying Hindoo, even if you had sworn by all the gods in their false temples, your blood would have been on the knife, and your body in the water. But the Sikh knows the Englishman, and the Englishman knows the Sikh. Listen, then, to what I have to say."

CHAPTER 13. The Strange Story of Jonathan Small（2）

"'There is a rajah in the northern provinces who has much wealth, though his lands are small. Much has come to him from his father, and more still he has saved by himself. He is of a low nature and keeps his gold rather than spends it. When the troubles broke out he would be friends both with the lion and the tiger—with the Sepoy and with the Company's Raj. Soon, however, it seemed to him that the white men's day had come. Through all the land he could hear of nothing but their death and their overthrow. Yet he was a careful man. He made such plans that, come what might, half at least of his treasure should be left to him. He kept the gold and silver by him in the vaults of his palace. But the most precious stones and the choicest pearls that he had, he put in an iron box. He sent it by a trusty servant who, under the guise of a merchant, should take it to the fort at Agra. There it should lie until the land is at peace. Thus, if the rebels won he would have his money. But if the Company conquered, his jewels would be saved to him. Having thus divided his hoard, he threw himself into the cause of the Sepoys, since they were strong upon his borders. By doing this, mark you, Sahib, his property becomes the due of those who have been true to their salt.

"'This pretended merchant, who travels under the name of Achmet, is now in the city of Agra. He desires to gain his way into the fort. He has with him as travelling-companion my foster-brother Dost Akbar, who knows his secret. Dost Akbar has promised this night to lead him to a side-postern of the fort. He has chosen this one for his purpose. Here he will come presently, and here he will find Mahomet Singh and myself awaiting him. The place is lonely, and none shall know of his coming. The world shall know of the merchant Achmet no more. But the great treasure of the rajah shall be divided among us. What say you to it, Sahib?'

"In Worcestershire the life of a man seems a great and a sacred thing. But it is very different when there is fire and blood all round you. You have been used to meeting death at every turn. Whether Achmet the merchant lived or died was a thing as light as air to me. But at the talk about the treasure my heart turned to it. I thought of what I might do in the old country with it. I thought how my folk would stare when they saw their ne'er-do-well coming back with his pockets full of gold moidores. I had, therefore, already made up my mind. Abdullah Khan, however, thought that I hesitated, and pressed the matter more closely.

"'Consider, Sahib,' said he, 'that if this man is taken by the commandant he will be hung or shot. His jewels will be taken by the government, so that no man will be a rupee the better for them. Now, since we do the taking of him, why should we not do the rest as well? The jewels will be as well with us as in the Company's coffers. There will be enough to make every one of us rich men and great chiefs. No one can know about the matter, for here we are cut off from all men. What could be better for the purpose? Say again, then, Sahib, whether you are with us, or if we must look upon you as an enemy.'

"'I am with you heart and soul,' said I.

"'It is well,' he answered, handing me back my firelock. 'You see that we trust you, for your word, like ours, is not to be broken. We have now only to wait for my brother and the merchant.'

"'Does your brother know, then, of what you will do?' I asked.

"'The plan is his. He has devised it. We will go to the gate and share the watch with Mahomet Singh.'

"The rain was still falling steadily, for it was just the beginning of the wet season. Brown, heavy clouds were drifting across the sky. It was hard to see more than a stone-cast. A deep moat lay in front of our door. But the water was in places nearly dried up, and it could easily be crossed. It was strange to me to be standing there with those two wild Punjaubees. We were waiting for the man who was coming to his death.

"Suddenly my eye caught the glint of a shaded lantern at the other side of the moat. It vanished among the mound-heaps, and then appeared again coming slowly in our direction.

"'Here they are!' I exclaimed.

"'You will challenge him, Sahib, as usual,' whispered Abdullah. 'Give him no cause for fear. Send us in with him, and we shall do the rest while you stay here on guard. Have the lantern ready to uncover, that we may be sure that it is indeed the man.'

"The light had flickered onwards, now stopping and now advancing, until I could see two dark figures upon the other side of the moat. I let them scramble down the sloping bank, splash through the mire, and climb half-way up to the gate, before I challenged them.

"'Who goes there?' said I, in a subdued voice.

"'Friends,' came the answer. I uncovered my lantern and threw a flood of light upon them. The first was an enormous Sikh, with a black beard which swept nearly down to his cummerbund. Outside of a show I have never seen so tall a man. The other was a little, fat, round fellow, with a great yellow turban, and a bundle in his hand, done up in a shawl. He seemed to be all in a quiver with fear. His hands twitched as if he had the ague, and his head kept turning to left and right. He had two bright little twinkling eyes, like a mouse when he ventures out from his hole. It gave me the chills to think of killing him, but I thought of the treasure. My heart set as hard as a flint within me. When he saw my white face he gave a little chirrup of joy and came running up towards me.

"'Your protection, Sahib,' he panted,—'your protection for the unhappy merchant Achmet. I have travelled across Rajpootana that I might seek the shelter of the fort at Agra. I have been robbed and beaten and abused because I have been the friend of the Company. It is a blessed night this when I am once more in safety,—I and my poor possessions.'

"'What have you in the bundle?' I asked."

"'An iron box,' he answered. 'It holds one or two small family things. They are of no value to others, but I would be sorry to lose them. Yet I am not a beggar. I shall reward you, young Sahib, and your governor also, if he will give me the shelter I ask.'

"I could not trust myself to speak longer with the man. The more I looked at his fat, frightened face, the harder it seemed that we should kill him in cold blood. It was best to get it over.

"'Take him to the main guard,' said I. The two Sikhs closed in upon him on each side. The giant walked behind, while they marched in through the dark gateway. Never was a man so surrounded by death. I remained at the gateway with the lantern.

"I could hear the measured sound of their footsteps through the lonely corridors. Suddenly it stopped. I heard voices, and a struggle, with the sound of blows. A moment later came, to my horror, a rush of footsteps coming my way, with the loud breathing of a running man. I turned my lantern down the long, straight passage. There was the fat man, running like the wind, with a smear of blood across his face. Close at his heels, jumping like a tiger, was the great black-bearded Sikh, with a knife flashing in his hand. I have never seen a man run so fast as that little merchant. He was gaining on the Sikh. I could see that if he once passed me and got to the open air he would save himself yet. My heart softened to him, but again the thought of his treasure turned me hard and bitter. I threw my firelock between his legs as he raced past, and he rolled twice over like a shot rabbit. Before he could stagger to his feet the Sikh was upon him, and buried his knife twice in his side. The man never uttered a moan nor moved a muscle, but lay where he had fallen. I think myself that he may have broken his neck with the fall. You see, gentlemen, that I am keeping my promise. I am telling you every part of the business just exactly as it happened, whether it is in my favour or not."

He stopped, and held out his manacled hands for the whiskey-and-water which Holmes had brewed for him. For myself, I confess that I had now conceived the utmost horror of the man. It was not only for this cold-blooded business in which he had been concerned, but even more for the somewhat flippant and careless way in which he narrated it. Whatever punishment was in store for him, I felt that he might expect no sympathy from me. He may have observed it, for there was a touch of defiance in his voice and manner as he proceeded.

"Well, we carried him in, Abdullah, Akbar, and I. A fine weight he was, too, for all that he was so short. Mahomet Singh was left to guard the door. We took him to a place which the Sikhs had already prepared. It was some distance off, where a winding passage leads to a great empty hall. The brick walls of it were all crumbling to pieces. The earth floor had sunk in at one place, making a natural grave. So we left Achmet the merchant there, having first covered him over with loose bricks. This done, we all went back to the treasure.

"It lay where he had dropped it when he was first attacked. The box was the same which now lies open upon your table. A key was hung by a silken cord to that carved handle upon the top. We opened it, and the light of the lantern gleamed upon a collection of gems such as I have read of and thought about when I was a little lad at Pershore. It was blinding to look upon them. When we had feasted our eyes we took them all out and made a list of them. There were one hundred and forty-three diamonds of the first water, including one which has been called, I believe, 'the Great Mogul' and is said to be the second largest stone in existence. Then there were ninety-seven very fine emeralds, and one hundred and seventy rubies, some of which, however, were small. There were forty carbuncles, two hundred and ten sapphires, sixty-one agates, and a great quantity of beryls, onyxes, cats'-eyes, turquoises, and other stones. I did not know the very names of them at the time, though I have become more familiar with them since. Besides this, there were nearly three hundred very fine pearls, twelve of which were set in a gold coronet. By the way, these last had been taken out of the chest and were not there when I recovered it.

"After we had counted our treasures we put them back into the chest and carried them to the gateway to show them to Mahomet Singh. Then we solemnly renewed our oath to stand by each other and be true to our secret. We agreed to conceal our loot in a safe place until the country should be at peace again, and then to divide it equally among ourselves. There was no use dividing it at present. If gems of such value were found upon us it would cause suspicion. There was no privacy in the fort nor any place where we could keep them. We carried the box, therefore, into the same hall where we had buried the body. There, under certain bricks in the best-preserved wall, we made a hollow and put our treasure. We made careful note of the place. Next day I drew four plans, one for each of us, and put the sign of the four of us at the bottom. For we had sworn that we should each always act for all, so that none might take advantage. That is an oath that I can put my hand to my heart and swear that I have never broken."

“Well, it is no use telling you gentlemen what came of the Indian mutiny. After Wilson took Delhi and Sir Colin relieved Lucknow, the back of the business was broken. Fresh troops came pouring in. Nana Sahib made himself scarce over the frontier. A flying column under Colonel Greathed came round to Agra and cleared the Pandies away from it. Peace seemed to be settling upon the country. We four were beginning to hope that the time was near when we might safely go off with our shares of the plunder. In a moment, however, our hopes were broken by our being arrested as the murderers of Achmet.

“It came about in this way. When the rajah put his valuable stones into the hands of Achmet, he did it because he knew that he was a trusty man. They are suspicious folk in the East, however. So what does this rajah do but take a second even more trusty servant and set him to play the spy upon the first? This second man was ordered never to let Achmet out of his sight. He followed him like his shadow. He went after him that night and saw him pass through the doorway. Of course he thought he had taken refuge in the fort. He applied for admission there himself next day, but could find no trace of Achmet. This seemed to him so strange that he spoke about it to a sergeant of guides. The sergeant brought it to the ears of the commandant. A thorough search was quickly made, and the body was discovered. Thus at the very moment that we thought that all was safe, we were all four seized and brought to trial on a charge of murder. Three of us were charged because we had held the gate that night. The fourth was charged because he was known to have been in the company of the murdered man. Not a word about the valuable stones came out at the trial, for the rajah had been deposed and driven out of India. So no one had any particular interest in them. The murder, however, was clearly made out, and it was certain that we must all have been concerned in it. The three Sikhs got penal servitude for life. I was condemned to death, though my sentence was afterwards commuted into the same as the others.

“It was rather a strange position that we found ourselves in then. There we were, all four tied by the leg and with very little chance of ever getting out again. Yet we each held a secret which might have put each of us in a palace if we could only have made use of it. It was enough to make a man eat his heart out to have to stand the kick and the cuff of every petty jack-in-office. It was enough to have to eat rice and drink water when that gorgeous fortune was ready for him outside, just waiting to be picked up. It might have driven me mad. But I was always a pretty stubborn one, so I just held on and bided my time.

“At last it seemed to me to have come. I was changed from Agra to Madras, and from there to Blair Island in the Andamans. There are very few white convicts at this settlement. As I had behaved well from the first, I soon found myself a sort of privileged person. I was given a hut in Hope Town, which is a small place on the slopes of Mount Harriet. I was left pretty much to myself. It is a dreary, fever-stricken place. All beyond our little clearings was infested with wild cannibal natives, who were ready enough to blow a poisoned dart at us if they saw a chance. There was digging, and ditching, and yam-planting, and a dozen other things to be done. So we were busy enough all day, though in the evening we had a little time to ourselves. Among other things, I learned to dispense drugs for the surgeon, and picked up a smattering of his knowledge. All the time I was on the lookout for a chance of escape. But it is hundreds of miles from any other land, and there is little or no wind in those seas. So it was a terribly difficult job to get away.

“The surgeon, Dr. Somerton, was a fast, sporting young chap. The other young officers would meet in his rooms of an evening and play cards. The surgery, where I used to make up my drugs, was next to his sitting-room, with a small window between us. Often, if I felt lonesome, I used to turn out the lamp in the surgery. Then, standing there, I could hear their talk and watch their play. I am fond of a hand at cards myself, and it was almost as good as having one to watch the others. There was Major Sholto, Captain Morstan, and Lieutenant Bromley Brown, who were in command of the native troops. There was the surgeon himself, and two or three prison-officials, crafty old hands who played a nice sly safe game. A very snug little party they used to make.

“Well, there was one thing which very soon struck me. That was that the soldiers used always to lose and the civilians to win. Mind, I do not say that there was anything unfair, but so it was. These prison-chaps had done little else than play cards ever since they had been at the Andamans. They knew each other’s game to a point, while the others just played to pass the time and threw their cards down anyhow. Night after night the soldiers got up poorer men. The poorer they got, the more keen they were to play. Major Sholto was the hardest hit. He used to pay in notes and gold at first, but soon it came to notes of hand and for big sums. He sometimes would win for a few deals, just to give him heart. Then the luck would set in against him worse than ever. All day he would wander about as black as thunder, and he took to drinking a deal more than was good for him.

“One night he lost even more heavily than usual. I was sitting in my hut when he and Captain Morstan came stumbling along on the way to their quarters. They were bosom friends, those two, and never far apart. The major was raving about his losses.

“‘It is all up, Morstan,’ he was saying, as they passed my hut. ‘I shall have to send in my papers. I am a ruined man.’

“‘Nonsense, old chap!’ said the other, slapping him upon the shoulder. ‘I have had a nasty facer myself, but—’ That was all I could hear, but it was enough to set me thinking.

“A couple of days later Major Sholto was strolling on the beach. So I took the chance of speaking to him.

“‘I wish to have your advice, major,’ said I.

“‘Well, Small, what is it?’ he asked, taking his cheroot from his lips.

CHAPTER 14. The Strange Story of Jonathan Small（3）

"'I wanted to ask you, sir,' I said. 'Who is the right person to give hidden treasure to? I know where half a million worth lies. I cannot use it myself. So I thought the best thing would be to give it to the right authorities. Then perhaps they would make my sentence shorter for me.'

'Half a million, Small?' he said, breathing in sharply. He looked hard at me to see if I was serious.

'Quite that, sir — in jewels and pearls. It lies there ready for anyone. The strange thing is that the real owner is an outlaw and cannot hold property. So it belongs to the first person who comes.'

'To the government, Small,' he said with a stammer. 'To the government.' But he said it in a slow, uncertain way. And in my heart I knew I had him.

'You think, then, sir, that I should give the information to the Governor-General?' I said quietly.

'Well, well, you must not do anything foolish, or you might be sorry later. Let me hear all about it, Small. Give me the facts.'

I told him the whole story, with small changes so that he could not identify the places. When I had finished he stood completely still and full of thought. I could see by the twitch of his lip that there was a struggle going on inside him.

'This is a very important matter, Small,' he said at last. 'You must not say a word to anyone about it. I shall see you again soon.'

Two nights later he and his friend Captain Morstan came to my hut in the dead of night with a lantern.

'I want you just to let Captain Morstan hear that story from your own lips, Small,' he said.

I repeated it as I had told it before.

'It sounds true, doesn't it?' he said. 'It's good enough to act on?'

Captain Morstan nodded.

'Look here, Small,' said the major. 'We have been talking it over, my friend here and I. We have come to the conclusion that this secret of yours is hardly a government matter after all. It is a private concern of your own. Of course you have the power to deal with it as you think best. Now the question is, what price would you ask for it? We might be willing to take it up, and at least look into it, if we could agree on terms.' He tried to speak in a cool, careless way. But his eyes were shining with excitement and greed.

'Why, as to that, gentlemen,' I answered. I also tried to be cool, but I felt as excited as he did. 'There is only one deal which a man in my position can make. I shall want you to help me to my freedom, and to help my three companions to theirs. We shall then take you into partnership, and give you a fifth share to divide between you.'

'Hum!' he said. 'A fifth share! That is not very tempting.'

'It would come to fifty thousand each,' I said.

'But how can we gain your freedom? You know very well that you ask for something impossible.'

'Nothing of the sort,' I answered. 'I have thought it all out to the last detail. The only bar to our escape is that we can get no boat fit for the voyage, and no provisions to last us for so long a time. There are plenty of little yachts and yawls at Calcutta or Madras which would serve our turn well. Do you bring one over. We shall promise to get aboard her by night. And if you will drop us on any part of the Indian coast you will have done your part of the deal.'

'If there were only one,' he said.

'None or all,' I answered. 'We have sworn it. The four of us must always act together.'

'You see, Morstan,' he said. 'Small is a man of his word. He does not flinch from his friend. I think we may very well trust him.'

'It's a dirty business,' the other answered. 'Yet, as you say, the money would save our commissions handsomely.'

'Well, Small,' said the major, 'we must, I suppose, try and meet you. We must first, of course, test the truth of your story. Tell me where the box is hid. And I shall get leave of absence and go back to India in the monthly relief-boat to inquire into the affair.'

'Not so fast,' I said. I grew colder as he got hot. 'I must have the consent of my three comrades. I tell you that it is four or none with us.'

'Nonsense!' he broke in. 'What have three black fellows to do with our agreement?'

'Black or blue,' I said, 'they are in with me, and we all go together.'

Well, the matter ended by a second meeting. At that meeting Mahomet Singh, Abdullah Khan, and Dost Akbar were all present. We talked the matter over again, and at last we came to an arrangement. We were to provide both the officers with charts of the part of the Agra fort. We were to mark the place in the wall where the treasure was hid. Major Sholto was to go to India to test our story. If he found the box he was to leave it there. He was to send out a small yacht provisioned for a voyage. It was to lie off Rutland Island, and we were to make our way to it. And finally he was to return to his duties. Captain Morstan was then to apply for leave of absence, to meet us at Agra. There we were to have a final division of the treasure. He was to take the major's share as well as his own. All this we sealed by the most solemn oaths that the mind could think or the lips utter. I sat up all night with paper and ink. By the morning I had the two charts all ready, signed with the sign of four — that is, of Abdullah, Akbar, Mahomet, and myself.

"Well, gentlemen, I am tiring you with my long story. I know my friend Mr. Jones cannot wait to get me safely locked up in prison. I will make it as short as I can. The bad man Sholto went off to India, but he never came back again. Captain Morstan showed me his name on a list of passengers in one of the mail-boats very soon after that. His uncle had died and left him a fortune. He had left the army. Yet he could lower himself to treat five men the way he had treated us. Morstan went over to Agra soon after that. He found, as we expected, that the treasure was really gone. The bad man had stolen it all. He did not carry out even one of the conditions on which we had sold him the secret. From that day I lived only for revenge. I thought of it by day and I held it close by night. It became a strong, all-consuming passion with me. I cared nothing for the law. I cared nothing for the hangman's rope. To escape, to hunt down Sholto, to have my hand upon his throat—that was my one thought. Even the Agra treasure had become a smaller thing in my mind than the killing of Sholto.

"Well, I have set my mind on many things in this life, and never one that I did not carry out. But it was tiring years before my time came. I have told you that I had picked up some knowledge of medicine. One day when Dr. Somerton was down with a fever, a little Andaman Islander was picked up by a group of convicts in the woods. He was sick to death and had gone to a lonely place to die. I took him in hand, though he was as dangerous as a young snake. After a couple of months I got him all right and able to walk. He took a kind of liking to me then. He would hardly go back to his woods, but was always hanging about my small house. I learned a little of his language from him, and this made him like me even more.

"Tonga—for that was his name—was a fine boatman. He owned a big, wide canoe of his own. When I found that he was devoted to me and would do anything to serve me, I saw my chance of escape. I talked it over with him. He was to bring his boat round on a certain night to an old dock which was never guarded. There he was to pick me up. I gave him directions to have several containers of water and a lot of sweet potatoes, coconuts, and other roots.

"He was loyal and true, was little Tonga. No man ever had a more faithful mate. On the night I named he had his boat at the dock. As it happened, however, there was one of the convict-guards down there—a terrible Pathan who had never missed a chance of insulting and hurting me. I had always promised myself revenge, and now I had my chance. It was as if fate had placed him in my way so that I might pay my debt before I left the island. He stood on the bank with his back to me, and his gun on his shoulder. I looked about for a stone to beat out his brains with, but I could see none. Then a strange thought came into my head and showed me where I could find a weapon. I sat down in the darkness and took off my wooden leg. With three long jumps I was on him. He put his gun to his shoulder, but I struck him full and knocked the whole front of his skull in. You can see the split in the wood now where I hit him. We both went down together, for I could not keep my balance. But when I got up I found him lying still enough. I made for the boat, and in an hour we were well out at sea. Tonga had brought all his belongings with him, his weapons and his gods. Among other things, he had a long bamboo spear and some Andaman coconut matting, with which I made a sort of sail. For ten days we sailed about, trusting to luck. On the eleventh day we were picked up by a trader going from Singapore to Jiddah with a load of Malay pilgrims. They were a strange crowd, and Tonga and I soon managed to settle down among them. They had one very good quality: they left you alone and asked no questions.

"Well, if I were to tell you all the adventures that my little friend and I went through, you would not thank me, for I would keep you here until the sun was shining. Here and there we drifted about the world, something always turning up to keep us from London. All the time, however, I never lost sight of my purpose. I would dream of Sholto at night. A hundred times I have killed him in my sleep. At last, however, some three or four years ago, we found ourselves in England. I had no great difficulty in finding where Sholto lived. I set to work to discover whether he had spent the treasure or if he still had it. I made friends with someone who could help me—I name no names, for I don't want to get anyone else in trouble—and I soon found that he still had the jewels. Then I tried to get at him in many ways. But he was pretty clever and always had two prize-fighters, besides his sons and his servant, on guard over him.

"One day, however, I got word that he was dying. I hurried at once to the garden, angry that he should slip out of my hands like that. Looking through the window, I saw him lying in his bed, with his sons on each side of him. I would have come through and taken my chance with the three of them, only even as I looked at him his jaw dropped, and I knew that he was gone. I got into his room that same night, though, and I searched his papers to see if there was any record of where he had hidden our jewels. There was not a line, however. So I came away, as bitter and angry as a man could be. Before I left I thought that if I ever met my Sikh friends again it would be a satisfaction to know that I had left some mark of our hatred. So I wrote down the sign of the four of us, as it had been on the chart, and I pinned it on his chest. It was too much that he should be taken to the grave without some sign from the men whom he had robbed and tricked."

“We earned a living at this time by showing poor Tonga at fairs and other such places as the black cannibal. He would eat raw meat and dance his war-dance. So we always had a hatful of pennies after a day’s work. I still heard all the news from Pondicherry Lodge. For some years there was no news to hear, except that they were hunting for the treasure. At last, however, came what we had waited for so long. The treasure had been found. It was up at the top of the house, in Mr. Bartholomew Sholto’s chemical laboratory. I came at once and had a look at the place. But I could not see how with my wooden leg I was to make my way up to it. I learned, however, about a trap-door in the roof. I also learned about Mr. Sholto’s supper-hour. It seemed to me that I could manage the thing easily through Tonga. I brought him out with me with a long rope wound round his waist. He could climb like a cat. He soon made his way through the roof. But, as bad luck would have it, Bartholomew Sholto was still in the room, to his cost. Tonga thought he had done something very smart in killing him. When I came up by the rope I found him walking about as proud as a peacock. He was very much surprised when I went at him with the end of the rope. I called him a little blood-thirsty devil. I took the treasure-box and let it down. Then I slid down myself. I had first left the sign of the four upon the table, to show that the jewels had come back at last to those who had most right to them. Tonga then pulled up the rope, closed the window, and left the way that he had come.

“I do not know that I have anything else to tell you. I had heard a boatman speak of the speed of Smith’s launch the _Aurora_. So I thought she would be a useful boat for our escape. I made a deal with old Smith. I was to give him a big sum if he got us safe to our ship. He knew, no doubt, that something was wrong. But he was not in our secrets. All this is the truth. If I tell it to you, gentlemen, it is not to amuse you,—for you have not done me a very good turn,—but it is because I believe the best defence I can make is just to hold back nothing. I want to let all the world know how badly Major Sholto treated me. I want them to know how innocent I am of the death of his son.”

“Well, Holmes,” said Athelney Jones, “You are a man who must be humoured. We all know that you are an expert in crime. But duty is duty. I have gone rather far in doing what you and your friend asked me. I shall feel more at ease when we have our story-teller here safe under lock and key. The cab still waits, and there are two inspectors downstairs. I am much obliged to you both for your assistance. Of course you will be wanted at the trial. Good-night to you.”

“Good-night, gentlemen both,” said Jonathan Small.

“You first, Small,” remarked the wary Jones as they left the room. “I’ll take particular care that you do not hit me with your wooden leg, whatever you may have done to the gentleman at the Andaman Isles.”

“Well, and there is the end of our little drama,” I remarked, after we had sat some time smoking in silence. “I fear that it may be the last investigation in which I shall have the chance of studying your methods. Miss Morstan has done me the honour to accept me as a husband in prospective.”

He gave a most dismal groan. “I feared as much,” said he. “I really cannot congratulate you.”

I was a little hurt. “Have you any reason to be dissatisfied with my choice?” I asked.

“Not at all. I think she is one of the most charming young ladies I ever met. She might have been most useful in such work as we have been doing. She had a clear genius that way. See the way in which she kept that Agra plan safe from all the other papers of her father. But love is an emotional thing. Whatever is emotional is opposed to that true cold reason which I place above all things. I should never marry myself, in case it affects my judgment.”

“I trust,” said I, laughing, “that my judgment may survive the ordeal. But you look weary.”

“Yes, the reaction is already upon me. I shall be as limp as a rag for a week.”

“Strange,” said I, “how times of what in another man I should call laziness alternate with your fits of splendid energy and vigour.”

“Yes,” he answered, “there are in me the makings of a very fine lazy fellow and also of a pretty lively sort of fellow. I often think of those lines of old Goethe,—

Schade dass die Natur nur _einen_ Mensch aus Dir schuf, Denn zum würdigen Mann war und zum Schelmen der Stoff.

“By the way, _à propos_ of this Norwood business, you see that they had, as I surmised, a confederate in the house. He could be none other than Lal Rao, the butler. So Jones actually has the undivided honour of having caught one fish in his great haul.”

“The division seems rather unfair,” I remarked. “You have done all the work in this business. I get a wife out of it, Jones gets the credit, pray what remains for you?”

“For me,” said Sherlock Holmes, “there still remains the cocaine-bottle.” And he stretched his long white hand up for it.
