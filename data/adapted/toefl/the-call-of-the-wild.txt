CHAPTER 1. Into the Primitive

"Old longings wander and leap, pulling against the chain of habit; again from its winter sleep the wild nature wakes."

Buck did not read the newspapers. If he had, he would have known that trouble was coming. It was coming not only for him, but for every strong dog with warm, long hair along the coast, from Puget Sound to San Diego. Men searching in the Arctic darkness had found a yellow metal. Steamship and transport companies were telling everyone about it. Thousands of men were rushing into the Northland. These men wanted dogs. They wanted heavy dogs with strong muscles for hard work. They wanted dogs with thick coats to keep them warm in the cold.

Buck lived at a big house in the sunny Santa Clara Valley. It was called Judge Miller's place. The house stood back from the road, half hidden among the trees. Through the trees you could see the wide, cool porch that ran around all four sides. Gravel roads led to the house. They wound through wide lawns and under the crossing branches of tall trees. At the back, everything was even bigger than at the front. There were great stables, where a dozen stable workers and boys worked. There were rows of small houses for servants, covered with vines. There was a long, orderly line of small buildings, long grape arbors, green fields, orchards, and berry patches. Then there was the pump house for the deep well. And there was the big cement tank where Judge Miller's boys swam in the morning and kept cool on hot afternoons.

Buck ruled over this great estate. He was born here, and he had lived here all four years of his life. It was true that there were other dogs. There had to be other dogs on such a big place, but they did not matter. They came and went. They lived in the crowded dog houses. Or they lived hidden in the back of the house, like Toots, the Japanese pug, or Ysabel, the Mexican hairless dog. These were strange creatures that rarely went outside or touched the ground. On the other hand, there were the fox terriers, at least twenty of them. They barked scary promises at Toots and Ysabel when those two looked out the windows. A whole army of housemaids with brooms and mops protected Toots and Ysabel.

But Buck was not a house dog or a dog-house dog. The whole estate was his. He jumped into the swimming tank or went hunting with the Judge's sons. He walked with Mollie and Alice, the Judge's daughters, on long evening or early morning walks. On cold nights he lay at the Judge's feet in front of the roaring library fire. He carried the Judge's grandsons on his back, or rolled them in the grass. He guarded their steps through wild adventures down to the fountain in the stable yard, and even beyond, where the fields were, and the berry patches. Among the terriers he walked like a king. Toots and Ysabel he completely ignored, for he was king. He was king over all the crawling, flying things of Judge Miller's place, humans included.

His father, Elmo, a huge St. Bernard, had been the Judge's closest companion. Buck seemed likely to follow in his father's path. He was not so large. He weighed only one hundred and forty pounds, because his mother, Shep, had been a Scotch sheep dog. Still, one hundred and forty pounds, plus the dignity that comes from good living and respect from everyone, let him carry himself like a king. During the four years since he was a puppy, he had lived the life of a rich, satisfied noble. He was proud of himself. He was even a little self-centered, as country gentlemen sometimes become because they live apart from others. But he had saved himself by not becoming just a spoiled house dog. Hunting and other outdoor joys had kept down his fat and made his muscles hard. And like people who swim in cold water, he loved water. It kept him healthy and strong.

And this was the kind of dog Buck was in the fall of 1897. That was when the Klondike strike pulled men from all over the world into the frozen North. But Buck did not read the newspapers. He did not know that Manuel, one of the garden workers, was a bad person to know. Manuel had one big weakness. He loved to play Chinese lottery. Also, in his gambling, he had one big weakness: faith in a system. And this made his ruin certain. For to play a system requires money, and the wages of a garden worker do not stretch far enough for a wife and many children.

The Judge was at a meeting of the Raisin Growers' Association. The boys were busy organizing an athletic club. It was on that memorable night that Manuel was treacherous. No one saw him and Buck go off through the orchard on what Buck thought was just a walk. And except for one lone man, no one saw them arrive at the little flag station called College Park. This man talked with Manuel, and money clinked between them.

"You might wrap up the goods before you deliver 'm," the stranger said roughly. Manuel doubled a piece of strong rope around Buck's neck under the collar.

"Twist it, an' you'll choke 'm plentee," said Manuel. The stranger grunted a quick yes.

Buck had accepted the rope with quiet dignity. To be sure, it was a strange thing to do. But he had learned to trust men he knew. He gave them credit for wisdom greater than his own. But when the ends of the rope were placed in the stranger's hands, he growled a warning. He had only shown his displeasure. In his pride, he believed that showing it was the same as giving a command. But to his surprise, the rope tightened around his neck, cutting off his breath. In quick anger he jumped at the man. The man met him halfway, grabbed him close by the throat, and with a quick twist threw him over on his back. Then the rope tightened without mercy. Buck struggled in a fury. His tongue hung out of his mouth, and his great chest panted uselessly. Never in all his life had he been treated so badly. Never in all his life had he been so angry. But his strength faded, his eyes went glassy, and he knew nothing when the train was flagged and the two men threw him into the baggage car.

The next he knew, he was dimly aware that his tongue was hurting. He was being shaken along in some kind of vehicle. The hoarse shriek of a train whistle at a crossing told him where he was. He had traveled too often with the Judge not to know the feeling of riding in a baggage car. He opened his eyes, and into them came the wild anger of a kidnapped king. The man jumped for his throat, but Buck was too quick for him. His jaws closed on the hand, and they did not let go until his senses were choked out of him once more.

“Yep, he has fits,” the man said. He hid his damaged hand from the baggageman, who had come over because of the sounds of struggle. “I’m taking him up for the boss to ’Frisco. A dog doctor there thinks he can cure him.”

About that night’s ride, the man spoke very openly for himself, in a little shed behind a bar on the San Francisco waterfront.

“All I get is fifty for it,” he complained. “And I would not do it again for a thousand, in cash.”

His hand was wrapped in a bloody cloth. The right trouser leg was torn from knee to ankle.

“How much did the other man get?” the bar owner asked.

“A hundred,” was the reply. “He would not take a penny less, I swear.”

“That makes a hundred and fifty,” the bar owner calculated. “And he is worth it, or I am a fool.”

The kidnapper undid the bloody cloths and looked at his torn hand. “If I do not get rabies—”

“It will be because you were born to hang,” the bar owner laughed. “Here, lend me a hand before you leave,” he added.

Dazed and suffering terrible pain from throat and tongue, with the life half choked out of him, Buck tried to face his tormentors. But he was thrown down and choked again and again. At last they succeeded in filing the heavy brass collar off his neck. Then the rope was removed, and he was thrown into a cage-like crate.

There he lay for the rest of the weary night, holding his anger and wounded pride. He could not understand what it all meant. What did they want with him, these strange men? Why were they keeping him shut up in this narrow crate? He did not know why, but he felt pressed down by a vague sense of coming disaster. Several times during the night he jumped to his feet when the shed door rattled open. He expected to see the Judge, or at least the boys. But each time it was the bulging face of the bar owner that looked in at him by the weak light of a candle. And each time the joyful bark that trembled in Buck’s throat was twisted into a fierce growl.

But the bar owner left him alone. In the morning four men entered and picked up the crate. More tormentors, Buck decided, for they were evil-looking creatures, ragged and untidy. He stormed and raged at them through the bars. They only laughed and poked sticks at him. He promptly attacked the sticks with his teeth until he realized that that was what they wanted. Then he lay down sullenly and allowed the crate to be lifted into a wagon. Then he, and the crate in which he was imprisoned, began a passage through many hands. Clerks in the express office took charge of him. He was carted about in another wagon. A truck carried him, with an assortment of boxes and parcels, onto a ferry steamer. He was trucked off the steamer into a great railway depot, and finally he was deposited in an express car.

For two days and nights this express car was dragged along at the tail of shrieking locomotives. For two days and nights Buck neither ate nor drank. In his anger he had met the first advances of the express messengers with growls, and they had retaliated by teasing him. When he flung himself against the bars, quivering and foaming, they laughed at him and taunted him. They growled and barked like hateful dogs, mewed, and flapped their arms and crowed. It was all very silly, he knew. But therefore the more outrage to his dignity, and his anger grew and grew. He did not mind the hunger so much, but the lack of water caused him severe suffering and fanned his anger to fever heat. For that matter, high-strung and finely sensitive, the ill treatment had thrown him into a fever. The inflammation of his parched and swollen throat and tongue fed the fever.

He was glad for one thing: the rope was off his neck. That had given them an unfair advantage. But now that it was off, he would show them. They would never get another rope around his neck. Upon that he was resolved. For two days and nights he neither ate nor drank. During those two days and nights of torment, he accumulated a fund of anger that boded ill for whoever first fell foul of him. His eyes turned blood-shot, and he was transformed into a raging fiend. So changed was he that the Judge himself would not have recognized him. The express messengers breathed with relief when they bundled him off the train at Seattle.

Four men carefully carried the crate from the wagon into a small, high-walled back yard. A stout man, with a red sweater that sagged loosely at the neck, came out and signed the book for the driver. That was the man, Buck guessed, the next tormentor, and he hurled himself fiercely against the bars. The man smiled grimly, and brought a small axe and a club.

“You are not going to take him out now?” the driver asked.

“Sure,” the man replied, driving the small axe into the crate for a pry.

There was an instantaneous scattering of the four men who had carried it in. From safe perches on top the wall they prepared to watch the performance.

Buck rushed at the splintering wood, sinking his teeth into it, surging and wrestling with it. Wherever the small axe fell on the outside, he was there on the inside, snarling and growling. He was as furiously anxious to get out as the man in the red sweater was calmly intent on getting him out.

“Now, you red-eyed devil,” he said, when he had made an opening sufficient for the passage of Buck’s body. At the same time he dropped the small axe and shifted the club to his right hand.

And Buck was truly a red-eyed devil, as he drew himself together for the spring, hair bristling, mouth foaming, a mad glitter in his blood-shot eyes. Straight at the man he launched his one hundred and forty pounds of fury, full of the held-back passion of two days and nights. In mid air, just as his jaws were about to close on the man, he received a shock that checked his body and brought his teeth together with an agonizing clip. He whirled over, fetching the ground on his back and side. He had never been struck by a club in his life, and did not understand. With a snarl that was part bark and more scream he was again on his feet and launched into the air. And again the shock came and he was brought crushingly to the ground. This time he was aware that it was the club, but his madness knew no caution. A dozen times he charged, and as often the club broke the charge and smashed him down.

After a very hard blow, he crawled to his feet. He was too confused to rush. He staggered about with little strength. Blood flowed from his nose, mouth and ears. His beautiful coat was sprayed and dotted with bloody spit. Then the man moved forward and gave him a terrible blow on the nose on purpose. All the pain he had suffered was as nothing compared with the sharp agony of this. With a roar that was almost like a lion in its anger, he again threw himself at the man. But the man moved the club from right to left. He calmly caught him by the lower jaw. At the same time he twisted it down and back. Buck made a complete circle in the air, and half of another. Then he crashed to the ground on his head and chest.

For the last time he rushed. The man struck the clever blow he had held back on purpose for so long. Buck collapsed and went down, knocked completely unconscious.

"He's no new hand at dog-breaking, that is what I say," one of the men on the wall cried with great enthusiasm.

"I would rather break wild horses any day, and twice on Sundays," was the reply of the driver, as he climbed on the wagon and started the horses.

Buck's senses came back to him, but not his strength. He lay where he had fallen. From there he watched the man in the red sweater.

"'Answers to the name of Buck,'" the man said to himself, quoting from the letter of the saloon owner which had announced the delivery of the crate and its contents. "Well, Buck, my boy," he went on in a friendly voice, "we have had our little fight, and the best thing we can do is to let it go at that. You have learned your place, and I know mine. Be a good dog and all will go well and the goose hang high. Be a bad dog, and I will beat the stuffing out of you. Understand?"

As he spoke he patted without fear the head he had so without mercy pounded. Though Buck's hair stood up by itself at the touch of the hand, he endured it without protest. When the man brought him water he drank eagerly. Later he swallowed a generous meal of raw meat, chunk by chunk, from the hand of the man.

He was beaten (he knew that); but he was not broken. He saw, once and for all, that he stood no chance against a man with a club. He had learned the lesson, and in all his later life he never forgot it. That club was a revelation. It was his introduction to the rule of primitive law, and he met the introduction halfway. The facts of life took on a fiercer look. While he faced that look without fear, he faced it with all the hidden cunning of his nature awakened. As the days went by, other dogs came, in crates and at the ends of ropes. Some came quietly, and some raging and roaring as he had come. One and all, he watched them pass under the power of the man in the red sweater. Again and again, as he looked at each brutal act, the lesson was driven home to Buck: a man with a club was a lawgiver, a master to be obeyed, though not necessarily won over. Of this last Buck was never guilty, though he did see beaten dogs that fawned upon the man, and wagged their tails, and licked his hand. Also he saw one dog, that would neither win over nor obey, finally killed in the struggle for mastery.

Now and again men came, strangers, who talked with excitement, with coaxing, and in all kinds of ways to the man in the red sweater. And at such times money passed between them, and the strangers took one or more of the dogs away with them. Buck wondered where they went, for they never came back. But the fear of the future was strong upon him, and he was glad each time when he was not selected.

Yet his time came, in the end, in the form of a little shriveled man who spat broken English and many strange and rough exclamations which Buck could not understand.

"Sacredam!" he cried, when his eyes lit upon Buck. "Dat one dam bully dog! Eh? How moch?"

"Three hundred, and a present at that," was the quick reply of the man in the red sweater. "And seeing it's government money, you ain't got no kick coming, eh, Perrault?"

Perrault grinned. Considering that the price of dogs had been pushed skyward by the unusual demand, it was not an unfair sum for so fine an animal. The Canadian Government would be no loser, nor would its messages travel the slower. Perrault knew dogs, and when he looked at Buck he knew that he was one in a thousand—"One in ten t'ousand," he commented mentally.

Buck saw money pass between them. He was not surprised when Curly, a good-natured Newfoundland, and he were led away by the little shriveled man. That was the last he saw of the man in the red sweater. As Curly and he looked at Seattle growing smaller from the deck of the _Narwhal_, it was the last he saw of the warm Southland. Curly and he were taken below by Perrault and handed over to a black-faced giant called François. Perrault was a French-Canadian, and dark-skinned; but François was a French-Canadian half-breed, and twice as dark-skinned. They were a new kind of men to Buck (of which he was destined to see many more). While he developed no affection for them, he none the less grew honestly to respect them. He quickly learned that Perrault and François were fair men, calm and impartial in administering justice, and too wise in the way of dogs to be fooled by dogs.

In the lower deck of the _Narwhal_, Buck and Curly joined two other dogs. One of them was a big, snow-white fellow from Spitzbergen who had been brought away by a whaling captain, and who had later accompanied a Geological Survey into the Barrens. He was friendly, in a treacherous sort of way, smiling into one's face while he planned some secret trick. For instance, he stole from Buck's food at the first meal. As Buck sprang to punish him, the lash of François's whip sang through the air, reaching the guilty one first. Nothing remained to Buck but to recover the bone. That was fair of François, he decided, and the half-breed began his rise in Buck's estimation.

The other dog did not come near them. He did not accept any attention from them either. He did not try to steal from the new dogs. He was a sad and unfriendly fellow. He showed Curly clearly that he wanted only to be left alone. He also showed that there would be trouble if he was not left alone. His name was “Dave.” He ate and slept. Between times, he yawned. He took interest in nothing. He did not even take interest when the _Narwhal_ crossed Queen Charlotte Sound. The ship rolled and pitched and jumped like a wild thing. Buck and Curly became excited and half wild with fear. Dave raised his head as if he was upset. He gave them a cold glance with no interest. He yawned and went back to sleep.

Day and night, the ship shook with the steady beat of the propeller. One day was very like another. But Buck could see that the weather was slowly getting colder. At last, one morning, the propeller was quiet. An excited feeling spread through the _Narwhal_. Buck felt it, and so did the other dogs. He knew that a change was coming. François put them on leashes and brought them on deck. Buck stepped onto the cold surface. His feet sank into a white soft thing very like mud. He jumped back with a loud breath. More of this white stuff was falling through the air. He shook himself, but more of it fell on him. He smelled it with interest. Then he licked some up with his tongue. It bit like fire, and the next moment it was gone. This puzzled him. He tried it again, with the same result. The people watching laughed loudly. He felt ashamed, and he did not know why. It was his first snow.

CHAPTER 2. The Law of Club and Fang

Buck's first day on the Dyea beach was like a bad dream. Every hour was full of shock and surprise. He had been suddenly pulled from the heart of civilization and thrown into the heart of ancient, wild life. This was no lazy, sun-warmed life, with nothing to do but rest and be bored. Here there was no peace, no rest, no safety even for a moment. Everything was confusion and action, and every moment life and body were in danger. There was a strong need to be always careful. These dogs and men were not town dogs and men. They were wild, all of them, who knew no law but the law of club and tooth.

He had never seen dogs fight as these wolf-like creatures fought, and his first experience taught him a lesson he would never forget. It is true, it was an indirect experience, or he would not have lived to learn from it. Curly was the victim. They were camped near the log store, where she, in her friendly way, went up to a large dog the size of a full-grown wolf, though not half so large as she. There was no warning, only a leap like a flash, a sharp click of teeth, a leap out just as fast, and Curly's face was torn open from eye to jaw.

It was the wolf way of fighting, to strike and leap away. But there was more to it than this. Thirty or forty large dogs ran to the spot and surrounded the fighters in a focused and silent circle. Buck did not understand that silent focus, nor the eager way they were licking their lips. Curly rushed her enemy, who struck again and leaped aside. He met her next rush with his chest, in a strange way that knocked her off her feet. She never got up again. This was what the watching dogs had waited for. They closed in on her, growling and crying out, and she was buried, screaming in pain, beneath the mass of raised bodies.

It was so sudden, and so unexpected, that Buck was shocked. He saw Spitz run out his red tongue in a way he had of laughing. And he saw François, swinging an ax, jump into the mess of dogs. Three men with clubs were helping him to scatter them. It did not take long. Two minutes from the time Curly went down, the last of her attackers were beaten off. But she lay there still and dead in the bloody, stepped-on snow, almost literally torn to pieces, the dark half-breed standing over her and cursing terribly. The scene often came back to Buck to trouble him in his sleep. So that was the way. No fair play. Once down, that was the end of you. Well, he would make sure that he never went down. Spitz ran out his tongue and laughed again, and from that moment Buck hated him with a bitter and endless hatred.

Before he had recovered from the shock caused by the sad death of Curly, he received another shock. François put on him a set of straps and fasteners. It was a harness, such as he had seen the grooms put on the horses at home. And as he had seen horses work, so he was set to work, pulling François on a sled to the forest that edged the valley, and returning with a load of firewood. Though his pride was badly hurt by being made a work animal like this, he was too wise to rebel. He got down to work with determination and did his best, though it was all new and strange. François was strict, demanding instant obedience, and by his whip receiving instant obedience. Dave, who was an experienced wheel dog, nipped Buck's back legs whenever he was wrong. Spitz was the leader, also experienced, and while he could not always reach Buck, he growled sharp correction now and again, or cleverly threw his weight in the ropes to pull Buck into the way he should go. Buck learned easily, and under the combined teaching of his two mates and François made remarkable progress. Before they returned to camp he knew enough to stop at "ho," to go ahead at "mush," to turn wide on the bends, and to keep clear of the wheel dog when the loaded sled shot downhill at their heels.

"T'ree vair' good dogs," François told Perrault. "Dat Buck, heem pool lak hell. I tich heem queek as anyt'ing."

By afternoon, Perrault, who was in a hurry to be on the trail with his messages, returned with two more dogs. "Billee" and "Joe" he called them, two brothers, and true large dogs both. Sons of the same mother though they were, they were as different as day and night. Billee's one fault was his too-good nature, while Joe was the very opposite, sour and thinking too much, with a constant growl and a hateful eye. Buck received them in a friendly way, Dave ignored them, while Spitz went on to beat first one and then the other. Billee wagged his tail in a calming way, turned to run when he saw that calming did no good, and cried (still in a calming way) when Spitz's sharp teeth cut his side. But no matter how Spitz circled, Joe turned around on his heels to face him, hair standing up, ears laid back, lips twisting and growling, jaws biting together as fast as he could snap, and eyes shining like a devil—the very picture of fighting fear. So terrible was his appearance that Spitz was forced to give up disciplining him. But to cover his own embarrassment he turned on the harmless and crying Billee and drove him to the edges of the camp.

By evening Perrault got another dog, an old large dog, long and lean and thin, with a face full of battle scars and a single eye which flashed a warning of skill that commanded respect. He was called Sol-leks, which means the Angry One. Like Dave, he asked nothing, gave nothing, expected nothing. And when he marched slowly and carefully into their middle, even Spitz left him alone. He had one strange habit which Buck was unlucky enough to discover. He did not like to be approached on his blind side. Of this offense Buck was unknowingly guilty, and the first knowledge he had of his mistake was when Sol-leks turned on him and cut his shoulder to the bone for three inches up and down. Forever after Buck avoided his blind side, and to the last of their friendship had no more trouble. His only clear ambition, like Dave's, was to be left alone. Though, as Buck was later to learn, each of them had one other and even more important ambition.

That night Buck faced the great problem of sleeping. The tent was lit by a candle and glowed warmly in the middle of the white plain. When he entered it, as a matter of course, both Perrault and François attacked him with curses and cooking pots. He recovered from his shock and fled in shame into the outer cold. A cold wind was blowing. It cut him sharply and bit with special poison into his wounded shoulder. He lay down on the snow and tried to sleep, but the frost soon drove him shivering to his feet. Miserable and sad, he wandered about among the many tents. He only found that one place was as cold as another. Here and there fierce dogs rushed upon him. But he raised his neck-hair and snarled (for he was learning fast), and they let him go his way without being attacked.

Finally an idea came to him. He would return and see how his own team-mates were doing. To his surprise, they had disappeared. Again he wandered about through the great camp, looking for them, and again he returned. Were they in the tent? No, that could not be, or he would not have been driven out. Then where could they possibly be? With drooping tail and shivering body, very sad indeed, he circled the tent without purpose. Suddenly the snow gave way beneath his front legs and he sank down. Something moved under his feet. He sprang back, raising his hair and snarling, fearful of the unseen and unknown. But a friendly little yelp reassured him, and he went back to investigate. A breath of warm air rose to his nose, and there, curled up under the snow in a tight ball, lay Billee. He whined in a soothing way, moved and twisted to show his good will and intentions. He even dared, as a bribe for peace, to lick Buck’s face with his warm wet tongue.

Another lesson. So that was the way they did it, right? Buck confidently chose a spot, and with much fuss and wasted effort began to dig a hole for himself. In a moment the heat from his body filled the small space and he was asleep. The day had been long and hard, and he slept deeply and comfortably, though he growled and barked and struggled with bad dreams.

He did not open his eyes until the noises of the waking camp woke him. At first he did not know where he was. It had snowed during the night and he was completely buried. The snow walls pressed him on every side, and a great wave of fear swept through him—the fear of the wild thing for the trap. It was a sign that he was going back through his own life to the lives of his ancestors. For he was a civilized dog, an overly civilized dog, and from his own experience he knew no trap and so could not fear it by himself. The muscles of his whole body tightened suddenly and by instinct, the hair on his neck and shoulders stood up, and with a fierce snarl he jumped straight up into the blinding day. The snow flew about him in a flashing cloud. Before he landed on his feet, he saw the white camp spread out before him. He knew where he was and remembered all that had passed from the time he went for a walk with Manuel to the hole he had dug for himself the night before.

A shout from François greeted his appearance. “Wot I say?” the dog-driver cried to Perrault. “Dat Buck for sure learn queek as anyt’ing.”

Perrault nodded seriously. As courier for the Canadian Government, carrying important messages, he was anxious to get the best dogs. He was especially glad to have Buck.

Three more huskies were added to the team within an hour, making a total of nine. Before another quarter of an hour had passed, they were in their pulling gear and swinging up the trail toward the Dyea Cañon. Buck was glad to be gone, and though the work was hard he found he did not particularly hate it. He was surprised at the eagerness that filled the whole team and passed to him. But still more surprising was the change in Dave and Sol-leks. They were new dogs, completely transformed by the pulling gear. All their passiveness and lack of concern had dropped from them. They were alert and active, anxious that the work should go well. They were fiercely angry with anything that slowed the work by delay or confusion. The hard work of the traces seemed the highest expression of their being. It was all that they lived for and the only thing in which they took delight.

Dave was the wheel dog, or sled dog, pulling in front of him was Buck, then came Sol-leks. The rest of the team was stretched out ahead, single file, to the leader, which position was filled by Spitz.

Buck had been purposely placed between Dave and Sol-leks so that he might receive instruction. He was a quick learner, and they were equally quick teachers. They never allowed him to stay long in error, and they enforced their teaching with their sharp teeth. Dave was fair and very wise. He never bit Buck without cause, and he never failed to bite him when he needed it. As François’s whip backed him up, Buck found it was easier to change his ways than to fight back. Once, during a brief stop, when he got tangled in the traces and delayed the start, both Dave and Sol-leks flew at him and gave him a sound beating. The resulting tangle was even worse, but Buck took good care to keep the traces clear after that. Before the day was done, he had mastered his work so well that his mates stopped nagging him. François’s whip snapped less often, and Perrault even honored Buck by lifting up his feet and carefully examining them.

It was a hard day’s run, up the Cañon, through Sheep Camp, past the Scales and the timber line, across glaciers and snowdrifts hundreds of feet deep, and over the great Chilcoot Divide, which stands between the salt water and the fresh and guards forbiddingly the sad and lonely North. They made good time down the chain of lakes which fills the craters of extinct volcanoes. Late that night they pulled into the huge camp at the head of Lake Bennett, where thousands of goldseekers were building boats against the break-up of the ice in the spring. Buck made his hole in the snow and slept the sleep of the exhausted just, but all too early he was routed out in the cold darkness and harnessed with his mates to the sled.

That day they traveled forty miles, because the trail was already packed. But the next day, and for many days after, they had to break their own trail. They worked harder and made slower progress. Usually, Perrault traveled ahead of the team. He packed the snow with his snowshoes to make it easier for the dogs. François guided the sled at the back. Sometimes he and Perrault switched places, but not often. Perrault was in a hurry. He was proud of his knowledge of ice. This knowledge was essential, because the autumn ice was very thin. Where the water moved fast, there was no ice at all.

Day after day, for many days, Buck worked in the harness. They always broke camp in the dark. The first gray light of dawn found them on the trail, with new miles already behind them. And they always set up camp after dark. They ate their small piece of fish and crawled into the snow to sleep. Buck was extremely hungry. His daily portion was one and a half pounds of dried salmon, and it seemed to disappear. He never had enough, and he suffered from constant hunger pains. But the other dogs weighed less and were born to this life. They received only one pound of fish and still stayed in good condition.

He quickly lost the picky habits of his old life. He had been a fussy eater, but he found that his mates finished first and stole his unfinished food. There was no way to defend it. While he was fighting off two or three dogs, the food was disappearing down the throats of the others. To fix this, he ate as fast as they did. Hunger pushed him so hard that he was even willing to take what did not belong to him. He watched and learned. He saw Pike, one of the new dogs, a clever lazy dog and thief, secretly steal a piece of bacon when Perrault's back was turned. The next day, Buck did the same thing and got away with the whole piece. There was a big noise of anger, but no one suspected him. Dub, a clumsy dog who was always getting caught, was punished for Buck's crime.

This first theft showed that Buck was fit to survive in the hostile Northland. It showed his ability to adapt, his ability to adjust to changing conditions. Without this ability, he would have died quickly and terribly. It also showed the decay of his moral nature. Morals were useless and a disadvantage in the cruel struggle to survive. In the Southland, under the law of love and friendship, it was fine to respect private property and personal feelings. But in the Northland, under the law of club and tooth, anyone who cared about such things was a fool. If he followed such rules, he would fail to do well.

Buck did not reason this out. He was fit, that was all. Without thinking about it, he adjusted himself to the new way of life. All his life, no matter the odds, he had never run from a fight. But the club of the man in the red sweater had beaten a deeper, more primitive code into him. As a civilized dog, he could have died for a moral idea, such as defending Judge Miller's riding whip. But now the completeness of his return to a wild state was shown by his ability to run from the defense of a moral idea and save his skin. He did not steal for the joy of it. He stole because his stomach demanded it. He did not rob openly. He stole secretly and cleverly, out of respect for club and tooth. In short, he did the things he did because it was easier to do them than not to do them.

His development (or backward step) was fast. His muscles became as hard as iron, and he grew used to all ordinary pain. He became efficient inside and out. He could eat anything, no matter how disgusting or hard to digest. Once eaten, the juices of his stomach pulled out the last little bit of food value. His blood carried it to the farthest parts of his body and built it into the toughest and strongest tissue. His sight and smell became remarkably sharp. His hearing became so keen that in his sleep he heard the faintest sound and knew whether it meant peace or danger. He learned to bite the ice out with his teeth when it collected between his toes. When he was thirsty and there was a thick layer of ice over the water hole, he would break it by standing up and striking it with his stiff front legs. His most noticeable trait was his ability to smell the wind and predict it a night in advance. No matter how still the air was when he dug his nest by a tree or bank, the wind that later blew always found him on the sheltered side, protected and comfortable.

And he did not only learn by experience. Instincts long dead became alive again. The domesticated generations fell away from him. In unclear ways, he remembered back to the youth of his breed. He remembered the time when wild dogs roamed in packs through the ancient forest and killed their meat by running it down. It was no task for him to learn to fight with cut and slash and the quick wolf bite. Forgotten ancestors had fought in this way. They awakened the old life within him. The old tricks they had stamped into the breed's heredity were his tricks. They came to him without effort or discovery, as though they had always been his. And on still cold nights, when he pointed his nose at a star and howled long and like a wolf, it was his ancestors, dead and dust, pointing nose at star and howling down through the centuries and through him. And his rhythms were their rhythms. These rhythms voiced their sorrow and what the stiffness, the cold, and the dark meant to them.

Thus, as a sign of what a puppet thing life is, the ancient song rose through him and he came into his own again. And he came because men had found a yellow metal in the North, and because Manuel was a gardener's helper whose wages did not cover the needs of his wife and several small copies of himself.

CHAPTER 3. The Dominant Primordial Beast

The strong wild animal inside Buck was powerful. Under the hard conditions of trail life, it grew and grew. Yet it was a secret growth. His new cleverness gave him balance and control. He was too busy getting used to the new life to feel comfortable. He did not pick fights, and he avoided them whenever possible. A certain care marked his attitude. He was not likely to act too fast or without thought. In the bitter hatred between him and Spitz, he showed no impatience and avoided all offensive acts.

On the other hand, perhaps because he sensed in Buck a dangerous rival, Spitz never missed a chance to show his teeth. He even went out of his way to bully Buck. He tried constantly to start the fight that could end only in the death of one or the other. Early in the trip this might have happened if not for an unusual accident. At the end of this day they made a bleak and miserable camp on the shore of Lake Le Barge. Driving snow, a wind that cut like a white-hot knife, and darkness had forced them to search for a camping place. They could hardly have done worse. At their backs rose a straight wall of rock. Perrault and François were forced to make their fire and spread their sleeping robes on the ice of the lake itself. They had left the tent at Dyea in order to travel light. A few sticks of driftwood gave them a fire that melted down through the ice and left them to eat supper in the dark.

Close under the sheltering rock Buck made his nest. It was so comfortable and warm that he was unwilling to leave it when François handed out the fish he had first melted over the fire. But when Buck finished his share and returned, he found his nest occupied. A warning growl told him that the intruder was Spitz. Until now Buck had avoided trouble with his enemy, but this was too much. The beast in him roared. He sprang upon Spitz with a fury that surprised them both, and Spitz especially. His whole experience with Buck had taught him that his rival was an unusually timid dog, who managed to hold his own only because of his great weight and size.

François was surprised, too, when they shot out in a tangled mass from the broken nest and he guessed the cause of the trouble. “A-a-ah!” he cried to Buck. “Gif it to heem, by Gar! Gif it to heem, the dirty t’eef!”

Spitz was equally willing. He was crying with sheer rage and eagerness as he circled back and forth for a chance to spring in. Buck was no less eager, and no less cautious, as he likewise circled back and forth for the advantage. But it was then that the unexpected happened. It was the thing that pushed their struggle for supremacy far into the future, past many a weary mile of trail and toil.

An oath from Perrault, the loud impact of a club upon a bony frame, and a shrill yelp of pain announced the sudden outbreak of wild confusion. The camp was suddenly found to be alive with creeping furry forms—starving huskies, four or five score of them, who had smelled the camp from some Indian village. They had crept in while Buck and Spitz were fighting. When the two men sprang among them with stout clubs, they showed their teeth and fought back. They were crazed by the smell of the food. Perrault found one with its head buried in the food box. His club landed heavily on the thin ribs, and the food box was knocked over on the ground. In an instant a score of the starving animals were scrambling for the bread and bacon. The clubs fell upon them unheeded. They yelped and howled under the rain of blows, but struggled none the less wildly until the last crumb had been eaten.

In the meantime the astonished team-dogs had burst out of their nests only to be attacked by the fierce invaders. Never had Buck seen such dogs. It seemed as though their bones would burst through their skins. They were mere skeletons, loosely covered in dirty hides, with blazing eyes and dripping fangs. But the hunger-madness made them terrifying and impossible to resist. There was no opposing them. The team-dogs were swept back against the cliff at the first attack. Buck was surrounded by three huskies, and in a moment his head and shoulders were torn and cut. The noise was frightful. Billee was crying as usual. Dave and Sol-leks, dripping blood from a score of wounds, were fighting bravely side by side. Joe was snapping like a demon. Once his teeth closed on the foreleg of a husky, and he crunched down through the bone. Pike, the malingerer, leaped upon the crippled animal and broke its neck with a quick flash of teeth and a jerk. Buck got a foaming adversary by the throat and was sprayed with blood when his teeth sank through the jugular. The warm taste of it in his mouth drove him to greater fierceness. He threw himself upon another, and at the same time felt teeth sink into his own throat. It was Spitz, treacherously attacking from the side.

Perrault and François, having cleared out their part of the camp, hurried to save their sled-dogs. The wild wave of starving beasts rolled back before them, and Buck shook himself free. But it was only for a moment. The two men were forced to run back to save the food, and upon this the huskies returned to the attack on the team. Billee, terrified into bravery, sprang through the savage circle and fled away over the ice. Pike and Dub followed on his heels, with the rest of the team behind. As Buck drew himself together to spring after them, out of the tail of his eye he saw Spitz rush upon him with the clear intention of overthrowing him. Once off his feet and under that mass of huskies, there was no hope for him. But he braced himself to the shock of Spitz’s charge, then joined the flight out on the lake.

Later, the nine team-dogs gathered together and looked for shelter in the forest. No one was chasing them, but they were in a bad state. There was not one who was not wounded in four or five places, while some were wounded badly. Dub was badly hurt in a back leg. Dolly, the last dog of the team added at Dyea, had a badly torn throat. Joe had lost an eye. Billee, the good-tempered one, had an ear chewed and torn to pieces. He cried and made sad sounds throughout the night. At first light they walked with difficulty and care back to camp. They found the attackers gone and the two men in bad moods. Fully half their food supply was gone. The dogs had chewed through the sled ropes and canvas covers. In fact, nothing had escaped them, no matter how slightly possible to eat. They had eaten a pair of Perrault’s moose-hide shoes, chunks out of the leather straps, and even two feet of rope from the end of François’s whip. He broke from a sad study of it to look over his wounded dogs.

“Ah, my friends,” he said softly, “maybe it make you mad dog, those many bites. Maybe all mad dog, sacredam! What you think, eh, Perrault?”

The messenger shook his head with doubt. With four hundred miles of trail still between him and Dawson, he could not well afford to have madness break out among his dogs. Two hours of cursing and hard work got the harnesses into shape. The wound-stiff team was under way, struggling painfully over the hardest part of the trail they had yet met. For that matter, it was the hardest between them and Dawson.

The Thirty Mile River was wide open. Its wild water resisted the frost. The ice held at all only in the small turning waters and in the quiet places. Six days of exhausting work were needed to cover those thirty terrible miles. And terrible they were, for every foot of them was done at the risk of life to dog and man. A dozen times, Perrault, feeling the way, broke through the ice bridges. He was saved by the long pole he carried. He so held it that it fell each time across the hole made by his body. But a cold snap was on, the thermometer registering fifty below zero. Each time he broke through he was forced for very life to build a fire and dry his clothes.

Nothing frightened him. It was because nothing frightened him that he had been chosen for government messenger. He took all kinds of risks, firmly pushing his little wrinkled face into the frost and struggling on from dim dawn to dark. He went along the frowning shores on edge ice that bent and cracked under foot. They dared not stop on it. Once, the sled broke through, with Dave and Buck. They were half-frozen and all but drowned by the time they were pulled out. The usual fire was necessary to save them. They were coated solidly with ice. The two men kept them running around the fire, sweating and thawing, so close that they were singed by the flames.

At another time Spitz went through, dragging the whole team after him up to Buck. Buck pulled backward with all his strength, his front paws on the slippery edge and the ice shaking and snapping all around. But behind him was Dave, likewise pulling backward. Behind the sled was François, pulling till his tendons cracked.

Again, the edge ice broke away before and behind. There was no escape except up the cliff. Perrault climbed it by a miracle, while François prayed for just that miracle. Every strap and sled rope and the last bit of harness was turned into a long rope. The dogs were lifted, one by one, to the cliff top. François came up last, after the sled and load. Then came the search for a place to go down. That descent was in the end made with the help of the rope. Night found them back on the river with a quarter of a mile to the day’s credit.

By the time they made the Hootalinqua and good ice, Buck was worn out. The rest of the dogs were in like condition. But Perrault, to make up lost time, pushed them late and early. The first day they covered thirty-five miles to the Big Salmon. The next day thirty-five more to the Little Salmon. The third day forty miles, which brought them well up toward the Five Fingers.

Buck’s feet were not so compact and hard as the feet of the other dogs. His had softened during the many generations since the day his last wild ancestor was tamed by a cave-dweller or river man. All day long he walked with pain. Once camp was made, he lay down like a dead dog. Hungry as he was, he would not move to receive his share of fish. François had to bring it to him. Also, the dog-driver rubbed Buck’s feet for half an hour each night after supper. He sacrificed the tops of his own shoes to make four shoes for Buck. This was a great relief. One morning François forgot the shoes. Buck lay on his back, his four feet waving appealingly in the air, and refused to move without them. This caused even the wrinkled face of Perrault to twist itself into a grin. Later his feet grew hard to the trail, and the worn-out foot-gear was thrown away.

At the Pelly one morning, as they were putting on the harness, Dolly, who had never been important for anything, went suddenly mad. She announced her condition by a long, heartbreaking wolf howl that sent every dog bristling with fear. Then she sprang straight for Buck. He had never seen a dog go mad, nor did he have any reason to fear madness. Yet he knew that here was horror, and fled away from it in a panic. Straight away he raced, with Dolly, panting and foaming, one jump behind. She could not gain on him, so great was his terror. He could not leave her, so great was her madness. He plunged through the wooded breast of the island and flew down to the lower end. He crossed a back channel filled with rough ice to another island. He gained a third island, curved back to the main river, and in desperation started to cross it. And all the time, though he did not look, he could hear her snarling just one jump behind. François called to him a quarter of a mile away. He doubled back, still one jump ahead, gasping painfully for air. He put all his faith in that François would save him. The dog-driver held the axe poised in his hand. As Buck shot past him the axe crashed down upon mad Dolly’s head.

Buck fell against the sled. He was worn out, gasping for breath, and helpless. This was Spitz’s chance. He jumped on Buck. Twice his teeth sank into his helpless enemy and tore the flesh to the bone. Then François’s whip came down. Buck was glad to see Spitz get the worst beating any team had yet received.

“One devil, that Spitz,” said Perrault. “Some day he kill that Buck.”

“That Buck two devils,” François answered. “All the time I watch that Buck I know for sure. Listen: some fine day he get mad like hell and then he chew that Spitz all up and spit him out on the snow. Sure. I know.”

From then on it was war between them. Spitz was lead dog and the accepted master of the team. He felt his position threatened by this strange dog from the Southland. And Buck was strange to him. Of the many Southland dogs he had known, not one had done well in camp and on the trail. They were all too soft. They died under the hard work, the cold, and hunger. Buck was different. He alone endured and did well. He matched the husky in strength, wildness, and cleverness. He was also a powerful dog. What made him dangerous was this: the club of the man in the red sweater had knocked all blind courage and reckless haste out of his wish to lead. He was very clever. He could wait for his time with a patience that was almost primitive.

A fight for leadership was certain to come. Buck wanted it. He wanted it because it was his nature. He had been gripped tight by that strange, deep pride of the trail and the ropes. That pride holds dogs to the hard work until their last breath. It makes them glad to die in the ropes. It breaks their hearts if they are taken out of the ropes. This was the pride of Dave as wheel dog, and of Sol-leks as he pulled with all his strength. This pride took hold of them when camp broke. It turned them from sour and silent animals into straining, eager, ambitious creatures. It drove them on all day and dropped them at night when camp was reached. Then it let them fall back into dark restlessness and discontent. This was the pride that held Spitz up. It made him beat the sled dogs who made mistakes and avoided work in the ropes, or hid at rope-up time in the morning. It was also this pride that made him fear Buck as a possible lead dog. And this was Buck’s pride, too.

He openly threatened the other’s leadership. He came between Spitz and the lazy dogs Spitz should have punished. And he did it on purpose. One night there was a heavy snowfall. In the morning Pike, the lazy one, did not appear. He was safely hidden in his nest under a foot of snow. François called him and looked for him, but could not find him. Spitz was wild with anger. He ran through the camp, smelling and digging in every likely place. He snarled so terribly that Pike heard him and shook in his hiding place.

But when Pike was at last found, and Spitz jumped at him to punish him, Buck jumped between them with equal anger. It was so unexpected, and so cleverly done, that Spitz was thrown backward off his feet. Pike, who had been shaking with fear, took heart at this open rebellion. He jumped on his fallen leader. Buck also jumped on Spitz. Fair play was a forgotten rule to Buck. But François laughed at the incident while he still did his duty. He brought his whip down on Buck with all his strength. This did not drive Buck away from his fallen rival, so François used the handle of the whip. Half-stunned by the blow, Buck was knocked backward. The whip came down on him again and again, while Spitz gave Pike a good punishment for his many offenses.

In the days that followed, as Dawson grew closer and closer, Buck still kept interfering between Spitz and the guilty dogs. But he did it cleverly, when François was not around. With Buck’s hidden rebellion, a general disobedience grew and spread. Dave and Sol-leks were not affected, but the rest of the team went from bad to worse. Things no longer went right. There was constant quarreling and noise. Trouble was always near, and at the bottom of it was Buck. He kept François busy. The dog driver was always afraid of the life-and-death struggle between the two that he knew must happen sooner or later. More than once at night, the sounds of quarreling and fighting among the other dogs made him get out of his sleeping bag. He feared that Buck and Spitz were at it.

But the chance did not come, and they pulled into Dawson one dull afternoon with the great fight still to come. Here were many men and countless dogs, and Buck found them all at work. It seemed the fixed order of things that dogs should work. All day they went up and down the main street in long teams. At night their ringing bells still went by. They pulled cabin logs and firewood, carried loads up to the mines, and did all kinds of work that horses did in the Santa Clara Valley. Here and there Buck met Southland dogs, but mostly they were the wild wolf husky breed. Every night, regularly, at nine, at twelve, at three, they raised a night song, a strange and eerie chant. Buck was happy to join in.

With the northern lights burning coldly overhead, or the stars jumping in the cold dance, and the land numb and frozen under its cover of snow, this song of the huskies might have been a challenge to life. But it was sung in a minor key, with long wails and half-sobs, and was more a begging of life, the clear hard work of existence. It was an old song, as old as the breed itself. It was one of the first songs of the younger world, in a day when songs were sad. It was full of the sorrow of countless generations. This sad song stirred Buck in a strange way. When he moaned and sobbed, it was with the pain of living that was long ago the pain of his wild fathers. It was with the fear and mystery of the cold and dark that was fear and mystery to them. And that he should be stirred by it showed how completely he had gone back through the ages of fire and roof to the raw beginnings of life in the howling ages.

Seven days after they arrived in Dawson, they went down the steep bank by the Barracks to the Yukon Trail. They headed for Dyea and Salt Water. Perrault was carrying messages, and these were even more urgent than the ones he had brought in. Also, he felt proud about travel. He planned to make the fastest trip of the year. Several things helped him with this. The week's rest had made the dogs strong again and put them in perfect shape. The trail they had broken into the country was packed hard by later travelers. And also, the police had placed stores of food for dog and man in two or three places. He was traveling with little baggage.

They reached Sixty Mile, which is a fifty-mile run, on the first day. On the second day they moved fast up the Yukon, well on their way to Pelly. But such fine running was not achieved without great trouble and irritation for François. The secret rebellion led by Buck had destroyed the unity of the team. It was no longer like one dog leaping in the traces. The encouragement Buck gave the rebels led them into all kinds of small bad acts. Spitz was no longer a leader who was greatly feared. The old respect was gone, and they became equal to challenging his authority. Pike stole half a fish from him one night, and swallowed it down while Buck protected him. Another night Dub and Joe fought Spitz and made him give up the punishment they deserved. And even Billee, the good-natured one, was less good-natured, and whined not half so gently as in former days. Buck never came near Spitz without snarling and raising his hair in a threatening way. In fact, his conduct came close to that of a bully. He liked to walk proudly up and down right in front of Spitz's nose.

The breakdown of discipline also affected the dogs in their relations with one another. They argued and fought more than ever among themselves, until at times the camp was a howling mess. Dave and Sol-leks alone were unchanged, though they were made angry by the endless arguing. François swore strange wild oaths, and stamped the snow in useless anger, and tore his hair. His whip was always singing among the dogs, but it was of little use. As soon as his back was turned they were at it again. He backed up Spitz with his whip, while Buck backed up the rest of the team. François knew he was behind all the trouble, and Buck knew he knew. But Buck was too clever to ever be caught in the act again. He worked faithfully in the harness, for the hard work had become a delight to him. Yet it was a greater delight to secretly start a fight among his mates and tangle the traces.

At the mouth of the Tahkeena, one night after supper, Dub found a snowshoe rabbit, blundered it, and missed. In a second the whole team was in full cry. A hundred yards away was a camp of the Northwest Police, with fifty dogs, huskies all, who joined the chase. The rabbit ran fast down the river, turned off into a small creek, and held steadily up the frozen bed of it. It ran lightly on the surface of the snow, while the dogs ploughed through by main strength. Buck led the pack, sixty strong, around bend after bend, but he could not gain. He lay down low to the race, whining eagerly, his splendid body flashing forward, leap by leap, in the pale white moonlight. And leap by leap, like some pale frost ghost, the snowshoe rabbit flashed on ahead.

All that stirring of old instincts which at stated periods drives men out from the sounding cities to forest and plain to kill things by chemically propelled leaden pellets, the blood lust, the joy to kill—all this was Buck's, only it was infinitely more intimate. He was ranging at the head of the pack, running the wild thing down, the living meat, to kill with his own teeth and wash his muzzle to the eyes in warm blood.

There is a joy that marks the summit of life, and beyond which life cannot rise. And such is the paradox of living, this joy comes when one is most alive, and it comes as a complete forgetfulness that one is alive. This joy, this forgetfulness of living, comes to the artist, caught up and out of himself in a sheet of flame. It comes to the soldier, war-mad on a stricken field and refusing mercy. And it came to Buck, leading the pack, sounding the old wolf-cry, straining after the food that was alive and that fled swiftly before him through the moonlight. He was sounding the deeps of his nature, and of the parts of his nature that were deeper than he, going back into the womb of Time. He was mastered by the sheer surging of life, the tidal wave of being, the perfect joy of each separate muscle, joint, and sinew in that it was everything that was not death, that it was aglow and rampant, expressing itself in movement, flying exultantly under the stars and over the face of dead matter that did not move.

But Spitz, cold and calculating even in his supreme moods, left the pack and cut across a narrow neck of land where the creek made a long bend around. Buck did not know of this, and as he rounded the bend, the frost ghost of a rabbit still flitting before him, he saw another and larger frost ghost leap from the overhanging bank into the immediate path of the rabbit. It was Spitz. The rabbit could not turn, and as the white teeth broke its back in mid air it screamed as loudly as a stricken man may scream. At sound of this, the cry of Life plunging down from Life's apex in the grip of Death, the full pack at Buck's heels raised a hell's chorus of delight.

Buck did not cry out. He did not check himself, but drove in upon Spitz, shoulder to shoulder, so hard that he missed the throat. They rolled over and over in the powdery snow. Spitz gained his feet almost as though he had not been overthrown, slashing Buck down the shoulder and leaping clear. Twice his teeth clipped together, like the steel jaws of a trap, as he backed away for better footing, with lean and lifting lips that writhed and snarled.

In a flash Buck knew it. The time had come. It was to the death. They circled around each other, making low sounds, ears laid back, watching closely for any advantage. The scene came to Buck with a sense of knowing it well. He seemed to remember it all—the white woods, and earth, and moonlight, and the thrill of battle. Over the whiteness and silence lay a strange calm. There was not the faintest whisper of air—nothing moved, not a leaf shook. The visible breaths of the dogs rose slowly and stayed in the cold air. They had made short work of the snowshoe rabbit, these dogs that were poorly tamed wolves. And they were now drawn up in a waiting circle. They, too, were silent. Only their eyes were shining and their breaths drifted slowly upward. To Buck it was nothing new or strange, this scene of old time. It was as though it had always been, the usual way of things.

Spitz was an experienced fighter. From Spitzbergen through the Arctic, and across Canada and the Barrens, he had held his own with all kinds of dogs and gained mastery over them. Bitter anger was his, but never blind anger. In his passion to tear and destroy, he never forgot that his enemy was in like passion to tear and destroy. He never rushed till he was prepared to receive a rush. He never attacked till he had first defended that attack.

Buck tried without success to sink his teeth in the neck of the big white dog. Wherever his teeth struck for the softer flesh, they were met by the teeth of Spitz. Tooth clashed tooth, and lips were cut and bleeding, but Buck could not break through his enemy's guard. Then he warmed up and wrapped Spitz in a storm of rushes. Time and time again he tried for the snow-white throat, where life beat near to the surface. Each time and every time Spitz cut him and got away. Then Buck took to rushing, as though for the throat. But suddenly he pulled back his head and curved in from the side. He would drive his shoulder at the shoulder of Spitz, like a ram trying to knock him over. But instead, Buck's shoulder was cut down each time as Spitz jumped lightly away.

Spitz was untouched, while Buck was streaming with blood and panting hard. The fight was growing desperate. And all the while the silent and wild circle waited to finish off whichever dog went down. As Buck grew short of breath, Spitz took to rushing, and he kept him staggering for footing. Once Buck went over, and the whole circle of sixty dogs started up. But he recovered himself, almost in mid air, and the circle sank down again and waited.

But Buck had a quality that made for greatness—imagination. He fought by instinct, but he could fight by head as well. He rushed, as though attempting the old shoulder trick. But at the last instant he swept low to the snow and in. His teeth closed on Spitz's left front leg. There was a crunch of breaking bone, and the white dog faced him on three legs. Three times he tried to knock him over. Then he repeated the trick and broke the right front leg. Despite the pain and helplessness, Spitz struggled madly to keep up. He saw the silent circle, with shining eyes, hanging tongues, and silver breaths drifting upward. They were closing in upon him as he had seen similar circles close in upon beaten opponents in the past. Only this time he was the one who was beaten.

There was no hope for him. Buck was unstoppable. Mercy was a thing reserved for gentler climates. He moved for the final rush. The circle had tightened till he could feel the breaths of the huskies on his sides. He could see them, beyond Spitz and to either side, half crouching for the spring, their eyes fixed upon him. A pause seemed to fall. Every animal was still as though turned to stone. Only Spitz shook and raised his hair as he staggered back and forth. He made an angry sound with horrible threat, as though to frighten off coming death. Then Buck sprang in and out. But while he was in, shoulder had at last squarely met shoulder. The dark circle became a dot on the moon-flooded snow as Spitz disappeared from view. Buck stood and looked on, the successful champion, the dominant ancient beast who had made his kill and found it good.

CHAPTER 4. Who Has Won to Mastership

"What? What did I say? I spoke true when I said that Buck is two devils." This was François's speech next morning when he found Spitz missing and Buck covered with wounds. He pulled him to the fire and by its light pointed them out.

"That Spitz fight like hell," said Perrault, as he looked at the open cuts and tears.

"And that Buck fight like two hells," was François's answer. "And now we make good time. No more Spitz, no more trouble, sure."

While Perrault packed the camp equipment and loaded the sled, the dog-driver went on to put the harness on the dogs. Buck walked up to the place Spitz would have taken as leader. But François, not noticing him, brought Sol-leks to the wanted position. In his judgment, Sol-leks was the best lead-dog left. Buck jumped on Sol-leks in a fury, driving him back and standing in his place.

"What? What?" François cried, slapping his thighs with joy. "Look at that Buck. He kill that Spitz, he think to take the job."

"Go away, Chook!" he cried, but Buck refused to move.

He took Buck by the back of the neck, and though the dog made a threatening sound, dragged him to one side and put Sol-leks back. The old dog did not like it, and showed clearly that he was afraid of Buck. François was firm, but when he turned his back Buck again moved Sol-leks out, who was not at all unwilling to go.

François was angry. "Now, by Gar, I fix you!" he cried, coming back with a heavy club in his hand.

Buck remembered the man in the red sweater, and retreated slowly. He did not try to charge in when Sol-leks was once more brought forward. But he circled just beyond the reach of the club, making angry sounds with anger and rage. And while he circled he watched the club so as to dodge it if thrown by François, for he had become wise in the way of clubs. The driver went about his work, and he called to Buck when he was ready to put him in his old place in front of Dave. Buck retreated two or three steps. François followed him up, and then he again retreated. After some time of this, François threw down the club, thinking that Buck feared a beating. But Buck was in open revolt. He wanted, not to escape a clubbing, but to have the leadership. It was his by right. He had earned it, and he would not be content with less.

Perrault took a hand. Between them they ran him about for the better part of an hour. They threw clubs at him. He dodged. They cursed him, and his fathers and mothers before him, and all his seed to come after him down to the remotest generation, and every hair on his body and drop of blood in his veins. And he answered curse with a snarl and kept out of their reach. He did not try to run away, but retreated around and around the camp, showing clearly that when his desire was met, he would come in and be good.

François sat down and scratched his head. Perrault looked at his watch and swore. Time was flying, and they should have been on the trail an hour gone. François scratched his head again. He shook it and grinned in a shy way at the courier, who shrugged his shoulders in sign that they were beaten. Then François went up to where Sol-leks stood and called to Buck. Buck laughed, as dogs laugh, yet kept his distance. François unfastened Sol-leks's traces and put him back in his old place. The team stood harnessed to the sled in an unbroken line, ready for the trail. There was no place for Buck save at the front. Once more François called, and once more Buck laughed and kept away.

"Throw down the club," Perrault commanded.

François obeyed, and then Buck walked in, laughing in triumph, and swung around into position at the head of the team. His traces were fastened, the sled broken out, and with both men running they dashed out on to the river trail.

Highly as the dog-driver had valued Buck before, with his two devils, he found, while the day was yet young, that he had valued him too low. At a jump Buck took up the duties of leadership. And where judgment was required, and quick thinking and quick acting, he showed himself the superior even of Spitz, of whom François had never seen an equal.

But it was in giving the law and making his mates live up to it, that Buck excelled. Dave and Sol-leks did not mind the change in leadership. It was none of their business. Their business was to work, and work hard, in the traces. So long as that was not interfered with, they did not care what happened. Billee, the good-natured, could lead for all they cared, so long as he kept order. The rest of the team, however, had grown unruly during the last days of Spitz, and their surprise was great now that Buck went on to beat them into shape.

Pike, who pulled at Buck's heels, and who never put an ounce more of his weight against the breast-band than he was forced to do, was quickly and repeatedly shaken for loafing. And before the first day was done he was pulling more than ever before in his life. The first night in camp, Joe, the sour one, was punished roundly—a thing that Spitz had never succeeded in doing. Buck simply smothered him by virtue of superior weight, and cut him up till he ceased snapping and began to whine for mercy.

The general tone of the team picked up immediately. It recovered its old-time solidarity, and once more the dogs leaped as one dog in the traces. At the Rink Rapids two native huskies, Teek and Koona, were added. And the speed with which Buck broke them in took away François's breath.

"Never such a dog as that Buck!" he cried. "No, never! He worth one thousand dollar, by Gar! What? What you say, Perrault?"

And Perrault nodded. He was ahead of the record then, and gaining day by day. The trail was in excellent condition, well packed and hard, and there was no new-fallen snow with which to contend. It was not too cold. The temperature dropped to fifty below zero and remained there the whole trip. The men rode and ran by turn, and the dogs were kept on the jump, with but infrequent stoppages.

The Thirty Mile River was comparatively coated with ice, and they covered in one day going out what had taken them ten days coming in. In one run they made a sixty-mile dash from the foot of Lake Le Barge to the White Horse Rapids. Across Marsh, Tagish, and Bennett (seventy miles of lakes), they flew so fast that the man whose turn it was to run was pulled behind the sled at the end of a rope. And on the last night of the second week they topped White Pass and dropped down the sea slope with the lights of Skaguay and of the shipping at their feet.

It was a record run. Each day for fourteen days they had averaged forty miles. For three days Perrault and François threw chests up and down the main street of Skaguay. They were flooded with invitations to drink. The team was always the centre of a worshipful crowd of dog-busters and mushers. Then three or four western bad men wanted to clean out the town. They were shot like pepper-boxes for their pains. Public interest turned to other idols. Next came official orders. François called Buck to him. He threw his arms around him and wept over him. And that was the last of François and Perrault. Like other men, they passed out of Buck’s life for good.

A Scotch half-breed took charge of him and his mates. With a dozen other dog-teams he started back over the weary trail to Dawson. It was no light running now, nor record time. It was heavy toil each day, with a heavy load behind. For this was the mail train, carrying word from the world to the men who sought gold under the shadow of the Pole.

Buck did not like it, but he bore up well to the work. He took pride in it after the manner of Dave and Sol-leks. He saw that his mates, whether they prided in it or not, did their fair share. It was a monotonous life, operating with machine-like regularity. One day was very like another. At a certain time each morning the cooks turned out, fires were built, and breakfast was eaten. Then some broke camp, others harnessed the dogs, and they were under way an hour or so before the darkness fell which gave warning of dawn. At night, camp was made. Some pitched the flies, others cut firewood and pine boughs for the beds, and still others carried water or ice for the cooks. Also, the dogs were fed. To them, this was the one feature of the day. It was good to loaf around, after the fish was eaten, for an hour or so with the other dogs, of which there were fivescore and odd. There were fierce fighters among them. But three battles with the fiercest brought Buck to mastery. So when he bristled and showed his teeth they got out of his way.

Best of all, perhaps, he loved to lie near the fire. His hind legs were crouched under him, his fore legs stretched out in front, his head raised, and his eyes blinking dreamily at the flames. Sometimes he thought of Judge Miller’s big house in the sun-kissed Santa Clara Valley. He thought of the cement swimming-tank, and Ysabel, the Mexican hairless, and Toots, the Japanese pug. But oftener he remembered the man in the red sweater, the death of Curly, the great fight with Spitz, and the good things he had eaten or would like to eat. He was not homesick. The Sunland was very dim and distant, and such memories had no power over him. Far more potent were the memories of his heredity. They gave things he had never seen before a seeming familiarity. The instincts (which were but the memories of his ancestors become habits) had lapsed in later days. Still later, in him, they quickened and became alive again.

Sometimes as he crouched there, blinking dreamily at the flames, it seemed that the flames were of another fire. As he crouched by this other fire he saw another and different man from the half-breed cook before him. This other man was shorter of leg and longer of arm. His muscles were stringy and knotty rather than rounded and swelling. The hair of this man was long and matted, and his head slanted back under it from the eyes. He uttered strange sounds. He seemed very much afraid of the darkness, into which he peered continually. He clutched in his hand, which hung midway between knee and foot, a stick with a heavy stone made fast to the end. He was all but naked, a ragged and fire-scorched skin hanging part way down his back. But on his body there was much hair. In some places, across the chest and shoulders and down the outside of the arms and thighs, it was matted into almost a thick fur. He did not stand erect. His trunk was inclined forward from the hips, on legs that bent at the knees. About his body there was a peculiar springiness, or resiliency, almost catlike. There was a quick alertness as of one who lived in perpetual fear of things seen and unseen.

At other times this hairy man squatted by the fire with head between his legs and slept. On such occasions his elbows were on his knees. His hands were clasped above his head as though to shed rain by the hairy arms. And beyond that fire, in the circling darkness, Buck could see many gleaming coals, two by two, always two by two. He knew these to be the eyes of great beasts of prey. And he could hear the crashing of their bodies through the undergrowth, and the noises they made in the night. And dreaming there by the Yukon bank, with lazy eyes blinking at the fire, these sounds and sights of another world would make the hair to rise along his back. It stood on end across his shoulders and up his neck, till he whimpered low and suppressedly, or growled softly. Then the half-breed cook shouted at him, “Hey, you Buck, wake up!” Whereupon the other world would vanish and the real world come into his eyes. He would get up and yawn and stretch as though he had been asleep.

It was a hard trip, with the mail behind them, and the heavy work wore them down. They were short of weight and in poor condition when they made Dawson. They should have had a ten days’ or a week’s rest at least. But in two days’ time they dropped down the Yukon bank from the Barracks, loaded with letters for the outside. The dogs were tired, the drivers grumbling, and to make matters worse, it snowed every day. This meant a soft trail, greater friction on the runners, and heavier pulling for the dogs. Yet the drivers were fair through it all, and did their best for the animals.

Each night the dogs were attended to first. They ate before the drivers ate. No man sought his sleeping-robe till he had seen to the feet of the dogs he drove. Still, their strength went down. Since the beginning of the winter they had travelled eighteen hundred miles, dragging sleds the whole weary distance. And eighteen hundred miles will tell upon life of the toughest. Buck stood it, keeping his mates up to their work and maintaining discipline, though he, too, was very tired. Billee cried and whimpered regularly in his sleep each night. Joe was sourer than ever, and Sol-leks was unapproachable, blind side or other side.

But it was Dave who suffered most of all. Something had gone wrong with him. He became more bad-tempered and easily irritated. When camp was set up, he at once made his bed, where his driver fed him. Once out of the straps and down, he did not get on his feet again until it was time to put the straps on in the morning. Sometimes, while pulling, when jerked by a sudden stop of the sled, or by straining to start it, he would cry out with pain. The driver examined him, but could find nothing. All the drivers became interested in his case. They talked it over at meal-time, and over their last pipes before going to bed, and one night they held a meeting. He was brought from his bed to the fire and was pressed and poked until he cried out many times. Something was wrong inside, but they could find no broken bones, and could not understand it.

By the time Cassiar Bar was reached, he was so weak that he was falling again and again while pulling. The Scotch half-breed called a stop and took him out of the team. He made the next dog, Sol-leks, fast to the sled. His plan was to rest Dave, letting him run free behind the sled. Sick as he was, Dave hated being taken out. He made low sounds and growled while the straps were undone, and made sad crying sounds with a broken heart when he saw Sol-leks in the position he had held and served so long. For the pride of pulling and the trail was his, and, sick to death, he could not bear that another dog should do his work.

When the sled started, he struggled in the soft snow alongside the beaten trail. He attacked Sol-leks with his teeth, rushed against him and tried to push him off into the soft snow on the other side. He tried to jump inside his straps and get between him and the sled, and all the while whined and cried and cried with grief and pain. The half-breed tried to drive him away with the whip; but he paid no attention to the sharp blow, and the man did not have the heart to strike harder. Dave refused to run quietly on the trail behind the sled, where the going was easy. He continued to struggle alongside in the soft snow, where the going was most difficult, until he was exhausted. Then he fell, and lay where he fell, crying sadly as the long line of sleds moved by.

With the last bit of his strength he managed to stagger along behind until the train made another stop. Then he struggled past the sleds to his own, where he stood alongside Sol-leks. His driver waited a moment to get a light for his pipe from the man behind. Then he returned and started his dogs. They moved out on the trail with a remarkable lack of effort, turned their heads nervously, and stopped in surprise. The driver was surprised, too; the sled had not moved. He called his friends to see the sight. Dave had bitten through both of Sol-leks’s straps, and was standing directly in front of the sled in his proper place.

He pleaded with his eyes to remain there. The driver was confused. His friends talked of how a dog could break its heart through being denied the work that killed it. They recalled cases they had known, where dogs, too old for the hard work, or injured, had died because they were cut out of the straps. Also, they held it a mercy, since Dave was to die anyway, that he should die in the straps, easy in his heart and content. So he was put in the straps again, and proudly he pulled as of old, though more than once he cried out without wanting to from the bite of his hurt inside. Several times he fell down and was dragged in the straps, and once the sled ran over him so that he walked with difficulty afterward in one of his back legs.

But he held out until camp was reached, when his driver made a place for him by the fire. Morning found him too weak to travel. At strap-up time he tried to crawl to his driver. By shaking efforts he got on his feet, staggered, and fell. Then he moved forward slowly toward where the straps were being put on his mates. He would move his front legs forward and drag up his body with a sort of jerking movement, then move his front legs forward and jerk ahead again for a few more inches. His strength left him, and the last his mates saw of him he lay gasping in the snow and yearning toward them. But they could hear him crying sadly until they passed out of sight behind a belt of river trees.

Here the train was stopped. The Scotch half-breed slowly walked back to the camp they had left. The men stopped talking. A shot from a gun rang out. The man came back quickly. The whips snapped, the bells rang happily, the sleds moved along the trail; but Buck knew, and every dog knew, what had taken place behind the belt of river trees.

CHAPTER 5. The Toil of Trace and Trail

Thirty days after it left Dawson, the Salt Water Mail arrived at Skaguay. Buck and his mates were at the front. They were in a very bad state, worn out and worn down. Buck's one hundred and forty pounds had dropped to one hundred and fifteen. The rest of his mates were lighter dogs, but they had lost more weight than he had, compared to their size. Pike, who had often pretended to have a hurt leg during his life of cheating, was now limping for real. Sol-leks was limping, and Dub had a hurt shoulder-blade.

They all had terribly sore feet. They had no energy left. Their feet fell heavily on the trail. This shook their bodies and doubled the tiredness of a day of travel. Nothing was wrong with them except that they were completely exhausted. It was not the deep tiredness that comes from a short, very hard effort, which you recover from in a few hours. It was the deep tiredness that comes from months of hard work slowly draining your strength. They had no power to recover left, no reserve strength to use. It had all been used, every last bit of it. Every muscle, every part of them, every cell was tired, dead tired. And there was a reason for it. In less than five months they had travelled twenty-five hundred miles. During the last eighteen hundred of those miles, they had had only five days of rest. When they arrived at Skaguay they were clearly almost unable to walk. They could barely keep the ropes tight, and on the downhill parts they just managed to keep out of the way of the sled.

"Mush on, poor sore feet," the driver encouraged them as they walked unsteadily down the main street of Skaguay. "This is the last. Then we get one long rest. Eh? For sure. One very good long rest."

The drivers expected a long stop with confidence. They themselves had covered twelve hundred miles with only two days of rest. By reason and common justice they deserved a period of rest. But so many men had rushed into the Klondike, and so many sweethearts, wives, and relatives had not rushed in, that the crowded mail was taking on huge proportions. Also, there were official orders. Fresh groups of Hudson Bay dogs were to take the places of those no good for the trail. The useless ones were to be got rid of, and since dogs count for little compared to money, they were to be sold.

Three days passed. By then Buck and his mates found how really tired and weak they were. Then, on the morning of the fourth day, two men from the States came along and bought them, with their harness and everything, for very little money. The men called each other "Hal" and "Charles." Charles was a middle-aged man of lightish color, with weak, watery eyes and a mustache that twisted fiercely and strongly upward. This gave a false idea of the lip it hid, which hung down loosely. Hal was a youngster of nineteen or twenty. He had a big Colt's revolver and a hunting-knife strapped about him on a belt that was covered with cartridges. This belt was the most noticeable thing about him. It showed his lack of experience—a lack of experience that was complete and impossible to describe. Both men were clearly out of place. Why such men should come to the North is part of the mystery of things that is beyond understanding.

Buck heard the bargaining. He saw the money pass between the man and the Government agent. He knew that the Scotch half-breed and the mail-train drivers were passing out of his life, following Perrault and François and the others who had gone before. When Buck and his mates were driven to the new owners' camp, Buck saw a careless and untidy setup. The tent was half stretched, the dishes were not washed, everything was in disorder. He also saw a woman. The men called her "Mercedes." She was Charles's wife and Hal's sister—a nice family party.

Buck watched them with worry as they began to take down the tent and load the sled. They made a great deal of effort in their manner, but they had no practical method. The tent was rolled into an awkward bundle three times as large as it should have been. The tin dishes were packed away without being washed. Mercedes kept getting in her men's way and kept up a constant chattering of complaint and advice. When they put a clothes-sack on the front of the sled, she suggested it should go on the back. When they had put it on the back and covered it with a couple of other bundles, she discovered articles they had missed. These could go nowhere else but in that very sack, so they unloaded again.

Three men from a neighboring tent came out and watched, grinning and winking at one another.

"You have a very smart load as it is," said one of them. "It is not me who should tell you your business, but I would not carry that tent along if I were you."

"Impossible!" cried Mercedes, throwing up her hands in delicate dismay. "How in the world could I manage without a tent?"

"It is springtime, and you will not get any more cold weather," the man replied.

She shook her head firmly, and Charles and Hal put the last small items on top of the huge load.

"Do you think it will ride?" one of the men asked.

"Why shouldn't it?" Charles demanded rather shortly.

"Oh, that is all right, that is all right," the man said quickly and meekly. "I was just wondering, that is all. It seemed a little top-heavy."

Charles turned his back and pulled the ropes down as well as he could, which was not well at all.

"And of course the dogs can hike along all day with that thing behind them," said a second of the men.

"Certainly," said Hal, with cold politeness. He took hold of the gee-pole with one hand and swung his whip from the other. "Mush!" he shouted. "Mush on there!"

The dogs jumped against the breast-bands and pulled hard for a few moments, then relaxed. They were unable to move the sled.

"The lazy animals, I will show them," he cried, preparing to hit them with the whip.

But Mercedes interfered, crying, "Oh, Hal, you must not," as she caught hold of the whip and pulled it from him. "The poor dears! Now you must promise you will not be harsh with them for the rest of the trip, or I will not go a step."

"You know very little about dogs," her brother sneered. "And I wish you would leave me alone. They are lazy, I tell you, and you have to whip them to get anything out of them. That is their way. You ask anyone. Ask one of those men."

Mercedes looked at them pleadingly. Great dislike at the sight of pain was written in her pretty face.

"They are weak as water, if you want to know," came the reply from one of the men. "Completely worn out, that is what is the matter. They need a rest."

"Rest be damned," said Hal, with his beardless lips; and Mercedes said, "Oh!" in pain and sorrow at the swear word.

But she was a clannish creature, and rushed at once to the defence of her brother. "Never mind that man," she said pointedly. "You're driving our dogs, and you do what you think best with them."

Again Hal's whip fell upon the dogs. They threw themselves against the breast-bands, dug their feet into the packed snow, got down low to it, and put forth all their strength. The sled held as though it were an anchor. After two efforts, they stood still, panting. The whip was whistling savagely, when once more Mercedes interfered. She dropped on her knees before Buck, with tears in her eyes, and put her arms around his neck.

"You poor, poor dears," she cried sympathetically, "why don't you pull hard?—then you wouldn't be whipped." Buck did not like her, but he was feeling too miserable to resist her, taking it as part of the day's miserable work.

One of the onlookers, who had been clenching his teeth to suppress hot speech, now spoke up:—

"It's not that I care a whoop what becomes of you, but for the dogs' sakes I just want to tell you, you can help them a mighty lot by breaking out that sled. The runners are froze fast. Throw your weight against the gee-pole, right and left, and break it out."

A third time the attempt was made, but this time, following the advice, Hal broke out the runners which had been frozen to the snow. The overloaded and unwieldy sled forged ahead, Buck and his mates struggling frantically under the rain of blows. A hundred yards ahead the path turned and sloped steeply into the main street. It would have required an experienced man to keep the top-heavy sled upright, and Hal was not such a man. As they swung on the turn the sled went over, spilling half its load through the loose lashings. The dogs never stopped. The lightened sled bounded on its side behind them. They were angry because of the ill treatment they had received and the unjust load. Buck was raging. He broke into a run, the team following his lead. Hal cried "Whoa! whoa!" but they gave no heed. He tripped and was pulled off his feet. The capsized sled ground over him, and the dogs dashed on up the street, adding to the gayety of Skaguay as they scattered the remainder of the outfit along its chief thoroughfare.

Kind-hearted citizens caught the dogs and gathered up the scattered belongings. Also, they gave advice. Half the load and twice the dogs, if they ever expected to reach Dawson, was what was said. Hal and his sister and brother-in-law listened unwillingly, pitched tent, and overhauled the outfit. Canned goods were turned out that made men laugh, for canned goods on the Long Trail is a thing to dream about. "Blankets for a hotel" quoth one of the men who laughed and helped. "Half as many is too much; get rid of them. Throw away that tent, and all those dishes,—who's going to wash them, anyway? Good Lord, do you think you're travelling on a Pullman?"

And so it went, the inexorable elimination of the superfluous. Mercedes cried when her clothes-bags were dumped on the ground and article after article was thrown out. She cried in general, and she cried in particular over each discarded thing. She clasped hands about knees, rocking back and forth broken-heartedly. She averred she would not go an inch, not for a dozen Charleses. She appealed to everybody and to everything, finally wiping her eyes and proceeding to cast out even articles of apparel that were imperative necessaries. And in her zeal, when she had finished with her own, she attacked the belongings of her men and went through them like a tornado.

This accomplished, the outfit, though cut in half, was still a formidable bulk. Charles and Hal went out in the evening and bought six Outside dogs. These, added to the six of the original team, and Teek and Koona, the huskies obtained at the Rink Rapids on the record trip, brought the team up to fourteen. But the Outside dogs, though practically broken in since their landing, did not amount to much. Three were short-haired pointers, one was a Newfoundland, and the other two were mongrels of indeterminate breed. They did not seem to know anything, these newcomers. Buck and his comrades looked upon them with disgust, and though he speedily taught them their places and what not to do, he could not teach them what to do. They did not take kindly to trace and trail. With the exception of the two mongrels, they were bewildered and spirit-broken by the strange savage environment in which they found themselves and by the ill treatment they had received. The two mongrels were without spirit at all; bones were the only things breakable about them.

With the newcomers hopeless and forlorn, and the old team worn out by twenty-five hundred miles of continuous trail, the outlook was anything but bright. The two men, however, were quite cheerful. And they were proud, too. They were doing the thing in style, with fourteen dogs. They had seen other sleds depart over the Pass for Dawson, or come in from Dawson, but never had they seen a sled with so many as fourteen dogs. In the nature of Arctic travel there was a reason why fourteen dogs should not drag one sled, and that was that one sled could not carry the food for fourteen dogs. But Charles and Hal did not know this. They had worked the trip out with a pencil, so much to a dog, so many dogs, so many days, Q.E.D. Mercedes looked over their shoulders and nodded comprehensively, it was all so very simple.

Late next morning Buck led the long team up the street. There was nothing lively about it, no snap or go in him and his fellows. They were starting dead weary. Four times he had covered the distance between Salt Water and Dawson, and the knowledge that, jaded and tired, he was facing the same trail once more, made him bitter. His heart was not in the work, nor was the heart of any dog. The Outsides were timid and frightened, the Insides without confidence in their masters.

Buck felt vaguely that there was no depending upon these two men and the woman. They did not know how to do anything, and as the days went by it became apparent that they could not learn. They were slack in all things, without order or discipline. It took them half the night to pitch a slovenly camp, and half the morning to break that camp and get the sled loaded in fashion so slovenly that for the rest of the day they were occupied in stopping and rearranging the load. Some days they did not make ten miles. On other days they were unable to get started at all. And on no day did they succeed in making more than half the distance used by the men as a basis in their dog-food computation.

It was certain that they would not have enough dog-food. But they made it happen sooner by feeding the dogs too much. This brought closer the day when they would feed them too little. The Outside dogs had not been trained by long hunger to live on very little. They had very big appetites. Also, the tired huskies pulled weakly. So Hal decided that the normal amount of food was too small. He doubled it. And to make things worse, Mercedes had tears in her pretty eyes and a shake in her voice. She could not persuade him to give the dogs more food. So she took fish from the sacks and fed the dogs secretly. But Buck and the huskies did not need food. They needed rest. They were making poor progress. The heavy load they pulled drained their strength badly.

Then the time of too little food came. One day Hal woke up and saw that his dog-food was half gone. They had covered only a quarter of the distance. Also, he could not get more dog-food for love or money. So he cut the normal amount even more. He tried to make each day's travel longer. His sister and brother-in-law supported him. But their heavy equipment and their own lack of skill held them back. It was easy to give the dogs less food. But it was impossible to make the dogs travel faster. And they could not start earlier in the morning, so they could not travel more hours. They did not know how to work dogs. They also did not know how to work themselves.

The first dog to go was Dub. He was a poor, clumsy thief. He was always getting caught and punished. Still, he had been a faithful worker. His hurt shoulder-blade was not treated and not rested. It went from bad to worse. At last Hal shot him with the big Colt's revolver. People in that country say that an Outside dog starves to death on the food given to a husky. So the six Outside dogs with Buck could do no better than die on half the food given to a husky. The Newfoundland went first. Then came the three short-haired pointers. The two mixed-breed dogs held on to life more stubbornly. But they died in the end.

By this time all the comforts and soft ways of the Southland had fallen away from the three people. Arctic travel lost its charm and romance. It became a reality too harsh for them. Mercedes stopped crying over the dogs. She was too busy crying over herself and arguing with her husband and brother. Arguing was the one thing they were never too tired to do. Their bad temper came from their misery. It grew with it, doubled on it, and went beyond it. The wonderful patience of the trail comes to people who work hard and suffer much. It comes to those who stay gentle in speech and kind. It did not come to these two men and the woman. They had no idea of such patience. They were stiff and in pain. Their muscles ached, their bones ached, their very hearts ached. Because of this, they spoke sharply. Hard words were first on their lips in the morning and last at night.

Charles and Hal argued whenever Mercedes gave them a chance. Each one firmly believed that he did more than his share of the work. Neither one failed to say this belief at every opportunity. Sometimes Mercedes took her husband's side. Sometimes she took her brother's side. The result was a beautiful and endless family argument. It started from a dispute about who should chop a few sticks for the fire. This dispute concerned only Charles and Hal. But soon the rest of the family was pulled in: fathers, mothers, uncles, cousins, people thousands of miles away, and some of them dead. Hal's views on art, or the kind of society plays his mother's brother wrote, should have nothing to do with chopping a few sticks of firewood. This is impossible to understand. Still, the argument was as likely to go in that direction as in the direction of Charles's political prejudices. And Charles's sister's tale-bearing tongue should have nothing to do with building a Yukon fire. This was clear only to Mercedes. She unloaded many opinions on that topic. She also unloaded opinions on a few other unpleasant traits of her husband's family. Meanwhile the fire was not built, the camp was half set up, and the dogs were not fed.

Mercedes held a special grievance—the grievance of sex. She was pretty and soft. She had been treated with chivalry all her days. But her husband and brother now treated her with anything but chivalry. It was her habit to be helpless. They complained. This challenged what she saw as her most essential right as a woman. So she made their lives unbearable. She no longer thought about the dogs. Because she was sore and tired, she insisted on riding on the sled. She was pretty and soft, but she weighed one hundred and twenty pounds. This was a heavy last straw for the load pulled by the weak and starving animals. She rode for days, until the dogs fell in the traces and the sled stood still. Charles and Hal begged her to get off and walk. They pleaded with her. They urged her. Meanwhile she wept and called on Heaven, listing their cruelty.

On one occasion they took her off the sled by main strength. They never did it again. She let her legs go limp like a spoiled child. She sat down on the trail. They went on their way, but she did not move. After they had travelled three miles, they unloaded the sled. They came back for her and put her on the sled again by main strength.

Their own misery was so great that they were callous to the suffering of their animals. Hal's theory, which he practised on others, was that one must get hardened. He had started out preaching it to his sister and brother-in-law. When that failed, he hammered it into the dogs with a club. At the Five Fingers the dog-food gave out. A toothless old squaw offered to trade them a few pounds of frozen horse-hide. She wanted the Colt's revolver that kept the big hunting-knife company at Hal's hip. This hide was a poor substitute for food. It had been stripped from the starved horses of the cattlemen six months back. In its frozen state it was more like strips of galvanized iron. When a dog wrestled it into his stomach, it thawed into thin and innutritious leathery strings. It also became a mass of short hair, irritating and indigestible.

And through it all Buck staggered along at the head of the team as in a nightmare. He pulled when he could. When he could no longer pull, he fell down and stayed down until blows from whip or club drove him to his feet again. All the stiffness and shine had gone out of his beautiful furry coat. The hair hung down, limp and dirty, or stuck together with dried blood where Hal’s club had bruised him. His muscles had wasted away to thin strings, and the flesh pads had disappeared. So each rib and every bone in his body showed clearly through the loose skin that was wrinkled in folds of emptiness. It was heartbreaking, only Buck’s heart was unbreakable. The man in the red sweater had proved that.

As it was with Buck, so was it with his mates. They were walking skeletons. There were seven all together, including him. In their very great misery they had become unable to feel the bite of the whip or the bruise of the club. The pain of the beating was dull and distant, just as the things their eyes saw and their ears heard seemed dull and distant. They were not half living, or quarter living. They were simply so many bags of bones in which sparks of life moved faintly. When a halt was made, they dropped down in the traces like dead dogs, and the spark dimmed and paled and seemed to go out. And when the club or whip fell upon them, the spark moved weakly up, and they staggered to their feet and staggered on.

There came a day when Billee, the good-natured, fell and could not rise. Hal had traded off his revolver, so he took the axe and knocked Billee on the head as he lay in the traces. Then he cut the body out of the harness and dragged it to one side. Buck saw, and his mates saw, and they knew that this thing was very close to them. On the next day Koona went, and but five of them remained: Joe, too far gone to be harmful; Pike, crippled and limping, only half conscious and not conscious enough longer to pretend to be sick; Sol-leks, the one-eyed, still faithful to the work of trace and trail, and sad in that he had so little strength with which to pull; Teek, who had not travelled so far that winter and who was now beaten more than the others because he was fresher; and Buck, still at the head of the team, but no longer enforcing discipline or trying to enforce it, blind with weakness half the time and keeping the trail by the shape of it and by the dim feel of his feet.

It was beautiful spring weather, but neither dogs nor humans were aware of it. Each day the sun rose earlier and set later. It was dawn by three in the morning, and twilight lingered till nine at night. The whole long day was full of sunshine. The ghostly winter silence had given way to the great spring murmur of awakening life. This murmur arose from all the land, full of the joy of living. It came from the things that lived and moved again, things which had been as dead and which had not moved during the long months of frost. The sap was rising in the pines. The willows and aspens were bursting out in young buds. Shrubs and vines were putting on fresh clothes of green. Crickets sang in the nights, and in the days all manner of creeping, crawling things came out into the sun. Partridges and woodpeckers were booming and knocking in the forest. Squirrels were talking, birds singing, and overhead honked the wild-fowl driving up from the south in clever wedges that split the air.

From every hill slope came the trickle of running water, the music of unseen fountains. All things were thawing, bending, snapping. The Yukon was straining to break loose the ice that held it down. It ate away from beneath; the sun ate from above. Air-holes formed, cracks sprang and spread apart, while thin sections of ice fell through bodily into the river. And amid all this bursting, tearing, throbbing of awakening life, under the bright sun and through the soft-sighing breezes, like travellers to death, staggered the two men, the woman, and the huskies.

With the dogs falling, Mercedes weeping and riding, Hal swearing harmlessly, and Charles’s eyes watering wistfully, they staggered into John Thornton’s camp at the mouth of White River. When they halted, the dogs dropped down as though they had all been struck dead. Mercedes dried her eyes and looked at John Thornton. Charles sat down on a log to rest. He sat down very slowly and with great effort because of his great stiffness. Hal did the talking. John Thornton was whittling the last touches on an axe-handle he had made from a stick of birch. He whittled and listened, gave one-word replies, and, when it was asked, brief advice. He knew the breed, and he gave his advice in the certainty that it would not be followed.

“They told us up above that the bottom was dropping out of the trail and that the best thing for us to do was to stay put,” Hal said in response to Thornton’s warning to take no more chances on the rotten ice. “They told us we couldn’t make White River, and here we are.” This last with a sneering ring of triumph in it.

“And they told you true,” John Thornton answered. “The bottom’s likely to drop out at any moment. Only fools, with the blind luck of fools, could have made it. I tell you straight, I wouldn’t risk my body on that ice for all the gold in Alaska.”

“That’s because you’re not a fool, I suppose,” said Hal. “All the same, we’ll go on to Dawson.” He uncoiled his whip. “Get up there, Buck! Hi! Get up there! Mush on!”

Thornton went on whittling. It was useless, he knew, to get between a fool and his folly; while two or three fools more or less would not alter the scheme of things.

But the team did not get up at the command. It had long since passed into the stage where blows were required to rouse it. The whip flashed out, here and there, on its merciless errands. John Thornton pressed his lips together. Sol-leks was the first to crawl to his feet. Teek followed. Joe came next, yelping with pain. Pike made painful efforts. Twice he fell over, when half up, and on the third attempt managed to rise. Buck made no effort. He lay quietly where he had fallen. The whip bit into him again and again, but he neither whined nor struggled. Several times Thornton started, as though to speak, but changed his mind. A wetness came into his eyes, and, as the whipping continued, he arose and walked uncertainly up and down.

This was the first time Buck had failed. That alone was enough to make Hal very angry. He put down the whip and picked up his usual club instead. Buck refused to move under the heavier blows that now fell on him. Like his mates, he could barely get up. But unlike them, he had decided not to get up. He had a vague feeling that something bad was coming. This feeling had been strong when he pulled in to the bank, and it had not left him. All day he had felt the thin and bad ice under his feet. It seemed he sensed disaster close by, out there ahead on the ice where his master was trying to drive him. He refused to move. He had suffered so much, and was so far gone, that the blows did not hurt much. And as they kept falling on him, the spark of life inside him flickered and went down. It was nearly out. He felt strangely numb. As though from a great distance, he knew he was being beaten. The last feelings of pain left him. He no longer felt anything, though very faintly he could hear the club hit his body. But it was no longer his body. It seemed so far away.

And then, suddenly, without warning, John Thornton sprang upon the man with the club. He let out a cry that was not clear and sounded more like an animal's cry. Hal was thrown backward, as though a falling tree had struck him. Mercedes screamed. Charles looked on wistfully and wiped his watery eyes. But he did not get up because his body was stiff.

John Thornton stood over Buck, struggling to control himself. He was shaking with rage and could not speak.

"If you strike that dog again, I'll kill you," he at last managed to say in a choking voice.

"It's my dog," Hal replied. He wiped the blood from his mouth as he came back. "Get out of my way, or I'll fix you. I'm going to Dawson."

Thornton stood between him and Buck. He showed no intention of getting out of the way. Hal drew his long hunting knife. Mercedes screamed, cried, laughed, and showed the wild loss of control of hysteria. Thornton hit Hal's knuckles with the axe handle and knocked the knife to the ground. He hit his knuckles again as he tried to pick it up. Then he bent down, picked it up himself, and with two strokes cut Buck's traces.

Hal had no fight left in him. Besides, his hands were full with his sister, or his arms, rather. And Buck was too near dead to be of further use in pulling the sled. A few minutes later they pulled out from the bank and went down the river. Buck heard them go and raised his head to see. Pike was leading, Sol-leks was at the wheel, and between them were Joe and Teek. They were limping and staggering. Mercedes was riding the loaded sled. Hal guided at the gee-pole, and Charles stumbled along in the rear.

As Buck watched them, Thornton knelt beside him. With rough, kindly hands he searched for broken bones. By the time his search had found nothing more than many bruises and a state of terrible starvation, the sled was a quarter of a mile away. Dog and man watched it crawling along over the ice. Suddenly, they saw its back end drop down, as into a rut. The gee-pole, with Hal clinging to it, jerked into the air. Mercedes's scream came to their ears. They saw Charles turn and make one step to run back. Then a whole section of ice gave way and dogs and humans disappeared. A yawning hole was all that could be seen. The bottom had dropped out of the trail.

John Thornton and Buck looked at each other.

"You poor devil," said John Thornton, and Buck licked his hand.

CHAPTER 6. For the Love of a Man

When John Thornton froze his feet the previous December, his partners made him comfortable and left him to get well. They went on up the river to get out a raft of saw-logs for Dawson. He was still limping slightly when he rescued Buck. But with the continued warm weather, even the slight limp left him. And here, lying by the river bank through the long spring days, watching the running water, listening without hurry to the songs of birds and the hum of nature, Buck slowly won back his strength.

A rest comes very good after one has travelled three thousand miles. And it must be admitted that Buck grew lazy as his wounds healed, his muscles swelled out, and the flesh came back to cover his bones. For that matter, they were all resting—Buck, John Thornton, and Skeet and Nig—waiting for the raft to come that was to carry them down to Dawson. Skeet was a little Irish setter who early made friends with Buck. He was in a dying condition and was unable to resist her first advances. She had the doctor trait which some dogs possess. And as a mother cat washes her kittens, so she washed and cleaned Buck's wounds. Regularly, each morning after he had finished his breakfast, she performed her self-appointed task. Soon he came to look for her care as much as he did for Thornton's. Nig, equally friendly, though less open in showing it, was a huge black dog, half bloodhound and half deerhound, with eyes that laughed and a endless good nature.

To Buck's surprise these dogs showed no jealousy toward him. They seemed to share the kindliness and generosity of John Thornton. As Buck grew stronger they drew him into all sorts of ridiculous games, in which Thornton himself could not keep from joining. In this way Buck played through his recovery and into a new existence. Love, genuine passionate love, was his for the first time. This he had never experienced at Judge Miller's down in the sun-kissed Santa Clara Valley. With the Judge's sons, hunting and walking, it had been a working partnership. With the Judge's grandsons, a sort of self-important guardianship. And with the Judge himself, a formal and dignified friendship. But love that was feverish and burning, that was deep worship, that was madness, it had taken John Thornton to arouse.

This man had saved his life, which was something. But further, he was the ideal master. Other men saw to the welfare of their dogs from a sense of duty and business advantage. He saw to the welfare of his as if they were his own children, because he could not help it. And he saw further. He never forgot a kind greeting or a cheering word. And to sit down for a long talk with them ("gas" he called it) was as much his delight as theirs. He had a way of taking Buck's head roughly between his hands, and resting his own head upon Buck's, of shaking him back and forth, while calling him bad names that to Buck were love names. Buck knew no greater joy than that rough embrace and the sound of murmured oaths. At each jerk back and forth it seemed that his heart would be shaken out of his body, so great was its joy. And when, released, he sprang to his feet, his mouth laughing, his eyes full of meaning, his throat vibrating with unspoken sound, and in that way remained without movement, John Thornton would say with deep respect, "God! you can almost speak!"

Buck had a way of showing love that was close to hurt. He would often seize Thornton's hand in his mouth and close so fiercely that the flesh bore the mark of his teeth for some time afterward. And as Buck understood the oaths to be love words, so the man understood this pretended bite for a caress.

For the most part, however, Buck's love was expressed in deep worship. He went wild with happiness when Thornton touched him or spoke to him, but he did not seek these signs. Unlike Skeet, who was used to push her nose under Thornton's hand and nudge and nudge till petted, or Nig, who would walk up and rest his great head on Thornton's knee, Buck was content to adore at a distance. He would lie by the hour, eager, alert, at Thornton's feet, looking up into his face, dwelling upon it, studying it, following with keenest interest each fleeting expression, every movement or change of feature. Or, as chance might have it, he would lie farther away, to the side or rear, watching the outlines of the man and the occasional movements of his body. And often, such was the deep connection in which they lived, the strength of Buck's gaze would draw John Thornton's head around. And he would return the gaze, without speech, his heart shining out of his eyes as Buck's heart shone out.

For a long time after his rescue, Buck did not like Thornton to get out of his sight. From the moment he left the tent to when he entered it again, Buck would follow at his heels. His brief masters since he had come into the Northland had bred in him a fear that no master could be permanent. He was afraid that Thornton would pass out of his life as Perrault and François and the Scotch half-breed had passed out. Even in the night, in his dreams, he was haunted by this fear. At such times he would shake off sleep and creep through the chill to the flap of the tent. There he would stand and listen to the sound of his master's breathing.

But in spite of this great love he bore John Thornton, which seemed to show a soft civilizing influence, the strain of the primitive, which the Northland had aroused in him, remained alive and active. Faithfulness and devotion, things born of fire and roof, were his. Yet he kept his wildness and cunning. He was a thing of the wild, come in from the wild to sit by John Thornton's fire, rather than a dog of the soft Southland marked with the signs of generations of civilization. Because of his very great love, he could not steal from this man. But from any other man, in any other camp, he did not hesitate an instant. And the cunning with which he stole enabled him to escape detection.

His face and body were marked with scars from the teeth of many dogs. He fought as fiercely as ever, and with more intelligence. Skeet and Nig were too kind to fight. Besides, they belonged to John Thornton. But the strange dog, no matter what kind or courage, quickly accepted Buck's power. Or he found himself fighting for his life with a terrible enemy. And Buck showed no mercy. He had learned well the law of club and tooth. He never gave up an advantage. He never backed away from an enemy he had started toward Death. He had learned from Spitz, and from the chief fighting dogs of the police and mail. He knew there was no middle way. He must be master or be mastered. To show mercy was a weakness. Mercy did not exist in the early life. It was mistaken for fear, and such wrong ideas led to death. Kill or be killed, eat or be eaten, was the law. And this command, coming from the deep past of Time, he obeyed.

He was older than the days he had seen and the breaths he had taken. He joined the past with the present. The endless time behind him beat through him in a mighty rhythm. He moved with it as the tides and seasons moved. He sat by John Thornton's fire, a broad-chested dog, with white teeth and long fur. But behind him were the shadows of all kinds of dogs, half-wolves and wild wolves. They were urgent and pushing him. They tasted the flavor of the meat he ate. They were eager for the water he drank. They smelled the wind with him. They listened with him and told him the sounds made by the wild life in the forest. They shaped his moods and directed his actions. They lay down to sleep with him when he lay down. They dreamed with him and beyond him, and became themselves the stuff of his dreams.

These shadows called to him so strongly that each day mankind and the claims of mankind slipped farther from him. Deep in the forest a call was sounding. As often as he heard this call, strangely exciting and pulling, he felt forced to turn his back on the fire and the beaten earth around it. He felt forced to plunge into the forest, and on and on, he knew not where or why. Nor did he wonder where or why, the call sounding strongly, deep in the forest. But as often as he reached the soft, continuous earth and the green shade, the love for John Thornton drew him back to the fire again.

Thornton alone held him. The rest of mankind was as nothing. Chance travelers might praise or pet him. But he was cold under it all. From a man who showed too much feeling, he would get up and walk away. When Thornton's partners, Hans and Pete, arrived on the long-expected raft, Buck refused to notice them until he learned they were close to Thornton. After that he tolerated them in a passive sort of way. He accepted gifts from them as though he gave them a gift by accepting. They were of the same large type as Thornton, living close to the earth, thinking simply and seeing clearly. Before they swung the raft into the big whirlpool by the saw-mill at Dawson, they understood Buck and his ways. They did not insist upon an closeness such as existed with Skeet and Nig.

For Thornton, however, his love seemed to grow and grow. He, alone among men, could put a pack upon Buck's back in the summer journeying. Nothing was too great for Buck to do, when Thornton commanded. One day (they had supplied themselves with food from the profits of the raft and left Dawson for the head-waters of the Tanana) the men and dogs were sitting on the top of a cliff. It fell away, straight down, to bare bed-rock three hundred feet below. John Thornton was sitting near the edge, Buck at his shoulder. A sudden wish seized Thornton, and he drew the attention of Hans and Pete to the experiment he had in mind. "Jump, Buck!" he commanded, sweeping his arm out and over the deep opening. The next instant he was struggling with Buck on the extreme edge, while Hans and Pete were dragging them back into safety.

"It's strange," Pete said, after it was over and they had caught their breath.

Thornton shook his head. "No, it is splendid, and it is terrible, too. Do you know, it sometimes makes me afraid."

"I'm not wanting to be the man that lays hands on you while he's around," Pete announced firmly, nodding his head toward Buck.

"By Jingo!" was Hans's contribution. "Not myself either."

It was at Circle City, before the year was out, that Pete's fears came true. "Black" Burton, a man evil-tempered and awful, had been picking a fight with a newcomer at the bar, when Thornton stepped in a friendly way between. Buck, as was his custom, was lying in a corner, head on feet, watching his master's every action. Burton struck out, without warning, straight from the shoulder. Thornton was sent spinning, and saved himself from falling only by clutching the rail of the bar.

Those who were looking on heard what was neither bark nor cry, but a something which is best described as a roar. They saw Buck's body rise up in the air as he left the floor for Burton's throat. The man saved his life by without thinking throwing out his arm, but was hurled backward to the floor with Buck on top of him. Buck loosed his teeth from the flesh of the arm and drove in again for the throat. This time the man succeeded only in partly blocking, and his throat was torn open. Then the crowd was upon Buck, and he was driven off. But while a surgeon checked the bleeding, he walked up and down, making angry sounds, attempting to rush in, and being forced back by an array of hostile clubs. A "miners' meeting," called on the spot, decided that the dog had sufficient reason, and Buck was released. But his reputation was made, and from that day his name spread through every camp in Alaska.

Later on, in the fall of the year, he saved John Thornton's life in quite another fashion. The three partners were guiding a long and narrow poling-boat down a bad stretch of rapids on the Forty-Mile Creek. Hans and Pete moved along the bank, holding with a thin Manila rope from tree to tree. Thornton remained in the boat, helping its descent by means of a pole, and shouting directions to the shore. Buck, on the bank, worried and anxious, kept alongside the boat, his eyes never off his master.

At a very bad spot, a shelf of rocks that was just under the water stuck out into the river. Hans let go of the rope. Thornton pushed the boat out into the stream. Hans ran down the bank with the end of the rope in his hand. He wanted to stop the boat when it had passed the rocks. The boat passed the rocks. It flew down-stream in a current as fast as a mill-race. Hans stopped it with the rope, but he stopped it too suddenly. The boat turned over and hit the bank bottom up. Thornton was thrown right out of it. He was carried down-stream toward the worst part of the rapids. It was a stretch of wild water where no swimmer could live.

Buck jumped in at once. After three hundred yards, in a wild swirl of water, he caught up with Thornton. Buck felt him grab his tail. Then Buck headed for the bank. He swam with all his great strength. But he moved slowly toward the bank. He moved down-stream very fast. From below came the deadly roar. There the wild current grew wilder. The rocks tore it into pieces and spray. The rocks pushed through like the teeth of a huge comb. The water pulled with a terrible force as it began the last steep drop. Thornton knew that the shore was impossible. He scraped hard over one rock. He hit a second and was bruised. He struck a third with crushing force. He held its slippery top with both hands and let go of Buck. Above the roar of the churning water he shouted: “Go, Buck! Go!”

Buck could not hold his place. He was swept on down-stream. He struggled desperately but could not win back. He heard Thornton’s command again. He partly rose out of the water and threw his head high. It was as if he wanted a last look. Then he turned toward the bank, as he was told. He swam with great power. Pete and Hans pulled him ashore at the very point where swimming stopped being possible and destruction began.

They knew that a man could hold on to a slippery rock in that driving current for only a few minutes. They ran as fast as they could up the bank. They went to a point far above where Thornton was hanging on. They tied the line they had used to stop the boat to Buck’s neck and shoulders. They were careful that it should not strangle him or impede his swimming. Then they launched him into the stream. He struck out boldly, but not straight enough into the stream. He discovered the mistake too late. Thornton was beside him and only about six strokes away. But Buck was being carried helplessly past.

Hans quickly stopped him with the rope, as though Buck were a boat. The rope tightened on him in the sweep of the current. He was jerked under the surface. He stayed under the surface until his body struck against the bank. Then he was pulled out. He was half drowned. Hans and Pete threw themselves upon him. They pounded the breath into him and the water out of him. He staggered to his feet and fell down. The faint sound of Thornton’s voice came to them. They could not make out the words, but they knew that he was in his extremity. His master’s voice acted on Buck like an electric shock. He sprang to his feet and ran up the bank ahead of the men. He ran to the point where he had started before.

Again the rope was tied on and he was launched. Again he struck out, but this time straight into the stream. He had miscalculated once, but he would not make that mistake a second time. Hans paid out the rope and allowed no slack. Pete kept it clear of coils. Buck held on until he was on a line straight above Thornton. Then he turned and headed down upon him with the speed of an express train. Thornton saw him coming. Buck struck him like a battering ram, with the whole force of the current behind him. Thornton reached up and closed both arms around the shaggy neck. Hans stopped the rope around the tree. Buck and Thornton were jerked under the water. They were strangling and suffocating. Sometimes one was uppermost and sometimes the other. They were dragged over the jagged bottom. They smashed against rocks and snags. Then they veered in to the bank.

Thornton came to. He was belly downward. Hans and Pete were pushing him violently back and forth across a drift log. His first glance was for Buck. Nig was setting up a howl over Buck’s limp and apparently lifeless body. Skeet was licking the wet face and closed eyes. Thornton was himself bruised and battered. When Buck had been brought around, Thornton went carefully over Buck’s body. He found three broken ribs.

“That settles it,” he announced. “We camp right here.” And camp they did, until Buck’s ribs knitted and he was able to travel.

That winter, at Dawson, Buck performed another exploit. It was not so heroic, perhaps, but it put his name many notches higher on the totem-pole of Alaskan fame. This exploit was particularly pleasing to the three men. They stood in need of the outfit which it furnished. It enabled them to make a long-desired trip into the virgin East, where miners had not yet appeared. It was brought about by a conversation in the Eldorado Saloon. In it, men became boastful of their favorite dogs. Buck, because of his record, was the target for these men. Thornton was driven stoutly to defend him. At the end of half an hour, one man stated that his dog could start a sled with five hundred pounds and walk off with it. A second bragged six hundred for his dog. And a third, seven hundred.

“Pooh! pooh!” said John Thornton. “Buck can start a thousand pounds.”

“And break it out? And walk off with it for a hundred yards?” demanded Matthewson, a Bonanza King, he of the seven hundred vaunt.

“And break it out, and walk off with it for a hundred yards,” John Thornton said coolly.

“Well,” Matthewson said, slowly and deliberately, so that all could hear, “I’ve got a thousand dollars that says he can’t. And there it is.” So saying, he slammed a sack of gold dust of the size of a bologna sausage down upon the bar.

Nobody spoke. Thornton’s bluff, if bluff it was, had been called. He could feel a flush of warm blood creeping up his face. His tongue had tricked him. He did not know whether Buck could start a thousand pounds. Half a ton! The enormousness of it appalled him. He had great faith in Buck’s strength. He had often thought him capable of starting such a load. But never, as now, had he faced the possibility of it. The eyes of a dozen men were fixed upon him, silent and waiting. Further, he had no thousand dollars. Nor did Hans or Pete.

“I have a sled standing outside now, with twenty fifty-pound sacks of flour on it,” Matthewson went on in a harsh, direct way. “So do not let that stop you.”

Thornton did not reply. He did not know what to say. He looked from face to face in a distant way. He was like a man who has lost the power to think. He was looking for something to start his mind again. The face of Jim O’Brien, a Mastodon King and an old friend, caught his eyes. It was like a signal to him. It seemed to wake him to do what he would never have dreamed of doing.

“Can you lend me a thousand?” he asked, almost in a whisper.

“Sure,” answered O’Brien. He dropped a heavy sack beside Matthewson’s. “Though I have little faith, John, that the beast can do the trick.”

The Eldorado emptied its people into the street to see the test. The tables were left empty. The dealers and helpers came out to see the result of the bet and to offer odds. Several hundred men, wearing fur and thick gloves, gathered around the sled within easy distance. Matthewson’s sled, loaded with a thousand pounds of flour, had been standing for a couple of hours. In the intense cold (it was sixty below zero), the runners had frozen fast to the hard-packed snow. Men offered odds of two to one that Buck could not move the sled. A small argument arose about the phrase “break out.” O’Brien said it was Thornton’s right to knock the runners loose. Then Buck would “break it out” from a dead stop. Matthewson insisted that the phrase included breaking the runners from the frozen grip of the snow. A majority of the men who had seen the bet being made decided in his favor. Then the odds went up to three to one against Buck.

There were no people willing to bet. Not a man believed Buck could do it. Thornton had been rushed into the bet, full of doubt. Now he looked at the sled itself, the solid fact, with the regular team of ten dogs curled up in the snow before it. The more impossible the task appeared. Matthewson became full of joy.

“Three to one!” he announced. “I’ll bet you another thousand at that rate, Thornton. What do you say?”

Thornton’s doubt was strong on his face, but his fighting spirit was aroused. It was the fighting spirit that rises above odds. It fails to see the impossible. It hears nothing except the call for battle. He called Hans and Pete to him. Their sacks were thin, and with his own the three partners could gather only two hundred dollars. In the low point of their fortunes, this sum was their total capital. Yet they laid it without waiting against Matthewson’s six hundred.

The team of ten dogs was freed from the sled, and Buck, with his own harness, was put into the sled. He had caught the excitement. He felt that in some way he must do a great thing for John Thornton. Murmurs of admiration at his splendid appearance went up. He was in perfect condition, without any extra flesh. The one hundred and fifty pounds that he weighed were pounds of courage and strength. His furry coat shone like silk. Down the neck and across the shoulders, his mane, even at rest, half stood up. It seemed to lift with every movement. It was as though too much energy made each hair alive and active. The great chest and heavy front legs were in proportion with the rest of the body. There the muscles showed in tight rolls under the skin. Men felt these muscles and said they were hard as iron. The odds went down to two to one.

“Good heavens, sir! Good heavens, sir!” said with a stutter a member of the latest ruling family, a king of the Skookum Benches. “I offer you eight hundred for him, sir, before the test, sir. Eight hundred just as he stands.”

Thornton shook his head and stepped to Buck’s side.

“You must stand away from him,” Matthewson protested. “Free play and plenty of room.”

The crowd fell silent. Only the voices of the bettors could be heard, without success offering two to one. Everybody agreed Buck was a magnificent animal. But twenty fifty-pound sacks of flour looked too large in their eyes for them to open their purses.

Thornton knelt down by Buck’s side. He took his head in his two hands and rested cheek on cheek. He did not shake him in a playful way, as he usually did. He did not murmur soft loving words. Instead, he whispered in his ear. “As you love me, Buck. As you love me,” was what he whispered. Buck whined with held-back eagerness.

The crowd watched with curiosity. The matter was growing mysterious. It seemed like magic. As Thornton got to his feet, Buck seized his gloved hand between his jaws. He pressed in with his teeth and released slowly, half unwillingly. It was the answer, not in words, but in love. Thornton stepped well back.

“Now, Buck,” he said.

Buck tightened the traces, then loosened them by several inches. It was the way he had learned.

“Gee!” Thornton’s voice rang out, sharp in the tense silence.

Buck swung to the right, ending the movement with a plunge that took up the slack. With a sudden jerk he stopped his one hundred and fifty pounds. The load shook, and from under the runners came a crisp crackling sound.

“Haw!” Thornton commanded.

Buck repeated the move, this time to the left. The crackling turned into a snapping. The sled turned, and the runners slipped and scraped several inches to the side. The sled was broken out. Men were holding their breath, deeply unaware of it.

“Now, MUSH!”

Thornton’s command cracked out like a pistol shot. Buck threw himself forward, tightening the traces with a jarring lunge. His whole body was gathered tightly together in the huge effort. The muscles twisted and knotted like living things under the silky fur. His great chest was low to the ground, his head forward and down, while his feet flew like mad. The claws cut the hard-packed snow in parallel lines. The sled swayed and trembled, half started forward. One of his feet slipped, and one man groaned aloud. Then the sled moved ahead in what seemed a rapid series of jerks. Yet it never really came to a dead stop again… half an inch… an inch… two inches… The jerks clearly became smaller. As the sled gained speed, he caught them up, until it was moving steadily along.

The men gasped and began to breathe again. They did not know that for a moment they had stopped breathing. Thornton was running behind. He encouraged Buck with short, happy words. The distance had been measured. He came near the pile of wood that marked the end of the hundred yards. A cheer began to grow and grow. It burst into a roar as Buck passed the wood and stopped at command. Every man was letting himself go, even Matthewson. Hats and gloves were flying in the air. Men were shaking hands with anyone at all. They were bubbling over in a general unclear noise.

But Thornton fell on his knees beside Buck. Head was against head. He was shaking him back and forth. Those who hurried up heard him cursing Buck. He cursed him long and strongly, and softly and with love.

“Gad, sir! Gad, sir!” said the Skookum Bench king, with broken sounds. “I’ll give you a thousand for him, sir, a thousand, sir—twelve hundred, sir.”

Thornton rose to his feet. His eyes were wet. The tears were running openly down his cheeks. “Sir,” he said to the Skookum Bench king, “no, sir. You can go to hell, sir. It’s the best I can do for you, sir.”

Buck seized Thornton’s hand in his teeth. Thornton shook him back and forth. As though moved by a common impulse, the people watching drew back to a polite distance. They were not too careless again to interrupt.

When Buck earned sixteen hundred dollars in five minutes for John Thornton, he made it possible for his master to pay off certain debts. He also made it possible for his master to travel with his partners into the East after a famous lost mine. The history of this mine was as old as the history of the country. Many men had looked for it. Few had found it. And more than a few had never returned from the search. This lost mine was full of tragedy and covered in mystery. No one knew of the first man. The oldest story stopped before it reached him. From the beginning there had been an ancient, broken-down cabin. Dying men had sworn to it and to the mine it marked. They supported their words with gold pieces that were unlike any known kind of gold in the Northland.

But no living man had taken this treasure, and the dead were dead. So John Thornton and Pete and Hans, with Buck and half a dozen other dogs, went into the East on an unknown trail. They hoped to succeed where men and dogs as good as themselves had failed. They traveled by sled seventy miles up the Yukon. Then they turned left into the Stewart River. They passed the Mayo and the McQuestion. They kept going until the Stewart itself became a small stream. It wound through the tall peaks that marked the main ridge of the continent.

John Thornton asked little of man or nature. He was not afraid of the wild. With a handful of salt and a rifle he could enter the wilderness. He could go wherever he pleased and for as long as he pleased. He was never in a hurry. In the Indian way, he hunted his dinner during the day's travel. If he did not find it, like the Indian, he kept traveling. He was sure that sooner or later he would come to it. So on this great journey into the East, plain meat was the food. Ammunition and tools mostly made up the load on the sled. And the schedule was drawn from the endless future.

To Buck it was endless delight—this hunting, fishing, and endless wandering through strange places. For weeks at a time they would keep going steadily, day after day. And for weeks on end they would camp here and there. The dogs rested and the men burned holes through frozen mud and gravel. They washed countless pans of dirt by the heat of the fire. Sometimes they went hungry. Sometimes they ate wildly, all according to how much game there was and how lucky the hunting was. Summer arrived. Dogs and men carried packs on their backs. They crossed blue mountain lakes on rafts. They went down or up unknown rivers in slender boats cut from the standing forest.

The months came and went. Back and forth they twisted through the unknown wild, where no men were and yet where men had been if the Lost Cabin were true. They crossed ridges in summer snowstorms. They shivered under the midnight sun on bare mountains between the tree line and the eternal snows. They dropped into summer valleys full of swarming flies and insects. In the shadows of glaciers they picked strawberries and flowers as ripe and fair as any the Southland could boast. In the fall of the year they entered a strange lake country, sad and silent. Wild birds had been there, but then there was no life nor sign of life—only the blowing of cold winds, the forming of ice in sheltered places, and the sad rippling of waves on lonely beaches.

And through another winter they wandered on the erased trails of men who had gone before. Once they came upon a path marked through the forest, an ancient path, and the Lost Cabin seemed very near. But the path began nowhere and ended nowhere. It remained a mystery, as the man who made it and the reason he made it remained a mystery. Another time they chanced upon the time-worn remains of a hunting lodge. Amid the torn pieces of rotted blankets John Thornton found a long-barrelled flint-lock. He knew it as a Hudson Bay Company gun from the early days in the Northwest. In those days such a gun was worth its height in beaver skins packed flat. And that was all—no hint about the man who long ago had built the lodge and left the gun among the blankets.

Spring came once more. At the end of all their wandering they found, not the Lost Cabin, but a shallow gold deposit in a broad valley. There the gold showed like yellow butter across the bottom of the washing-pan. They looked no farther. Each day they worked earned them thousands of dollars in clean dust and gold pieces, and they worked every day. The gold was put in moose-hide bags, fifty pounds to the bag. The bags were piled like firewood outside the lodge of spruce branches. Like giants they worked, days flashing after days like dreams as they piled the treasure up.

There was nothing for the dogs to do except haul in meat now and again that Thornton killed. Buck spent long hours thinking by the fire. The vision of the short-legged hairy man came to him more often now that there was little work to be done. Often, blinking by the fire, Buck wandered with him in that other world which he remembered.

The main thing in this other world seemed to be fear. Buck watched the hairy man sleeping by the fire, head between his knees and hands clasped above. He saw that the man slept restlessly, with many sudden starts and awakenings. At those times he would look fearfully into the darkness and throw more wood on the fire. They walked by the beach of a sea, where the hairy man gathered shellfish and ate them as he gathered. His eyes roved everywhere for hidden danger. His legs were ready to run like the wind at its first appearance. Through the forest they crept without a sound, Buck at the hairy man's heels. They were alert and watchful, the pair of them, ears twitching and moving and nostrils quivering. The man heard and smelled as keenly as Buck. The hairy man could jump up into the trees and travel ahead as fast as on the ground. He swung by the arms from limb to limb, sometimes a dozen feet apart. He let go and caught, never falling, never missing his grip. In fact, he seemed as much at home among the trees as on the ground. And Buck had memories of nights of watch spent beneath trees where the hairy man rested, holding on tightly as he slept.

The call still sounded deep in the forest. It was very close to the visions of the hairy man. It filled him with a great unrest and strange desires. It made him feel a vague, sweet gladness. He felt wild yearnings and stirrings for he did not know what. Sometimes he followed the call into the forest. He looked for it as if it were a real thing. He barked softly or defiantly, as his mood might lead him. He pushed his nose into the cool wood moss. Or he pushed it into the black soil where long grasses grew. He snorted with joy at the rich earth smells. Or he crouched for hours behind fungus-covered trunks of fallen trees, as if hiding. He was wide-eyed and wide-eared to everything that moved and sounded around him. Lying there, perhaps he hoped to surprise this call he could not understand. But he did not know why he did these different things. Something pushed him to do them. He did not think about them at all.

Strong impulses seized him. He would be lying in camp, resting lazily in the heat of the day. Suddenly his head would lift and his ears would stand up, alert and listening. He would jump to his feet and run away. He would go on and on for hours, through the forest paths and across the open spaces where the niggerheads grew in clumps. He loved to run down dry watercourses. He loved to creep and spy on the bird life in the woods. For a whole day he would lie in the underbrush where he could watch the partridges drumming and walking proudly up and down. But especially he loved to run in the dim twilight of the summer midnights. He listened to the quiet and sleepy murmurs of the forest. He read signs and sounds as a man may read a book. He was searching for the mysterious something that called. It called, waking or sleeping, at all times, for him to come.

One night he jumped from sleep with a start. His eyes were eager. His nostrils quivered and smelled. His mane rose in waves again and again. From the forest came the call (or one note of it, for the call had many notes). It was clear and definite as never before. It was a long, drawn-out howl, like, yet unlike, any noise made by a husky dog. And he knew it, in the old familiar way, as a sound heard before. He jumped through the sleeping camp. In quick silence he ran through the woods. As he came closer to the cry he went more slowly. There was caution in every movement. Then he came to an open place among the trees. Looking out, he saw a long, lean, timber wolf. It stood upright on its back legs, with its nose pointed to the sky.

He had made no noise, yet it stopped its howling. It tried to sense his presence. Buck walked into the open, half crouching. His body was held tightly together. His tail was straight and stiff. His feet fell with unusual care. Every movement showed both threat and an offer of friendliness. It was the warning truce that marks the meeting of wild beasts that hunt. But the wolf ran away at the sight of him. He followed, with wild jumps, in a frenzy to catch up. He ran him into a blind channel, in the bed of the creek where a pile of fallen timber blocked the way. The wolf turned around. It turned on its back legs after the fashion of Joe and of all cornered husky dogs. It snarled and its hair stood up. It snapped its teeth together in a continuous and rapid series of bites.

Buck did not attack. He circled around him and closed him in with friendly moves. The wolf was suspicious and afraid. Buck was three times his weight, while his head barely reached Buck's shoulder. Watching his chance, he darted away, and the chase began again. Time and again he was cornered, and the same thing happened. But he was in poor condition, or Buck could not so easily have caught up with him. He would run until Buck's head was even with his side. Then he would turn around at bay, only to run away again at the first chance.

But in the end Buck's persistence was rewarded. The wolf found that no harm was meant. Finally he sniffed noses with him. Then they became friendly. They played about in the nervous, half-shy way with which fierce beasts hide their fierceness. After some time of this the wolf started off at an easy run. It clearly showed he was going somewhere. He made it clear to Buck that he was to come. They ran side by side through the dark twilight, straight up the creek bed, into the gorge from which it came, and across the bleak divide where it began.

On the opposite slope of the watershed they came down into a level country. There were great stretches of forest and many streams. Through these great stretches they ran steadily, hour after hour. The sun rose higher and the day grew warmer. Buck was wildly glad. He knew he was at last answering the call. He was running by the side of his wood brother toward the place from where the call surely came. Old memories were coming upon him fast. He was stirring to them as of old he stirred to the realities of which they were the shadows. He had done this thing before, somewhere in that other and dimly remembered world. He was doing it again, now, running free in the open, the unpacked earth underfoot, the wide sky overhead.

They stopped by a running stream to drink. Stopping, Buck remembered John Thornton. He sat down. The wolf started on toward the place from where the call surely came. Then he returned to him, sniffing noses and making actions as though to encourage him. But Buck turned about and started slowly on the back track. For the better part of an hour the wild brother ran by his side, whining softly. Then he sat down, pointed his nose upward, and howled. It was a mournful howl. Buck held steadily on his way. He heard it grow faint and fainter until it was lost in the distance.

John Thornton was eating dinner when Buck dashed into camp. He sprang upon him in a frenzy of affection, knocking him over, climbing on him, licking his face, biting his hand. He was "playing the general tom-fool," as John Thornton called it. All the while he shook Buck back and forth and cursed him lovingly.

For two days and nights Buck never left the camp. He never let Thornton out of his sight. He followed him about at his work. He watched him while he ate. He saw him into his blankets at night and out of them in the morning. But after two days, the call in the forest began to sound more strongly than ever. Buck's restlessness came back to him. He was troubled by memories of the wild brother. He remembered the smiling land beyond the divide. He remembered running side by side through the wide forest stretches. Once again he began to wander in the woods. But the wild brother came no more. He listened through long nights, but the sad cry was never raised.

He began to sleep outside at night. He stayed away from camp for days at a time. Once he crossed the divide at the head of the creek. He went down into the land of timber and streams. There he wandered for a week. He looked for fresh signs of the wild brother, but found none. He killed his meat as he travelled. He travelled with the long, easy run that seems never to tire. He fished for salmon in a broad stream that emptied somewhere into the sea. By this stream he killed a large black bear. The bear had been blinded by mosquitoes while it was also fishing. It was raging through the forest, helpless and terrible. Even so, it was a hard fight. It stirred the last hidden remains of Buck's wild fierceness. Two days later, he returned to his kill. He found a dozen wolverines fighting over the meat. He scattered them like dry grass. Those that fled left two behind who would fight no more.

The hunger for blood became stronger than ever before. He was a killer, a creature that hunted. He lived on the things that lived. He did it alone, with no help, by his own strength and skill. He survived as a winner in a hostile world where only the strong survived. Because of all this, he became very proud of himself. This pride spread through his whole body like a sickness. It showed in all his movements. It was clear in the play of every muscle. It spoke as clearly as words in the way he carried himself. It made his glorious coat of fur even more glorious. But for the gray-brown on his nose and above his eyes, and for the splash of white hair that ran down the middle of his chest, he might have been mistaken for a giant wolf. He was larger than the largest of the breed. From his St. Bernard father he had inherited size and weight. But it was his shepherd mother who had given shape to that size and weight. His nose was the long wolf nose, except that it was larger than the nose of any wolf. His head was somewhat broader, but it was the wolf head on a huge scale.

His cleverness was wolf cleverness, and wild cleverness. His intelligence was shepherd intelligence and St. Bernard intelligence. All this, plus experience gained in the hardest of schools, made him as fearsome a creature as any that roamed the wild. He was a meat-eating animal living on a straight diet of meat. He was in full flower, at the high point of his life, overflowing with energy and strength. When Thornton passed a loving hand along his back, a snapping and crackling followed the hand. Each hair gave out its stored power at the touch. Every part, brain and body, nerve tissue and fibre, was tuned to the finest pitch. Between all the parts there was a perfect balance. To sights and sounds and events that required action, he responded with lightning speed. A husky dog could leap quickly to defend from attack or to attack. He could leap twice as quickly. He saw the movement, or heard the sound, and responded in less time than another dog needed just to see or hear. He perceived and decided and responded in the same instant. In fact, the three actions of perceiving, deciding, and responding came one after another. But the time between them was so tiny that they appeared to happen at once. His muscles were filled with life, and snapped into action sharply, like steel springs. Life streamed through him in a splendid flood, glad and wild, until it seemed it would burst him apart in pure joy and pour out generously over the world.

"Never was there such a dog," said John Thornton one day, as the partners watched Buck marching out of camp.

"When he was made, the mould was broke," said Pete.

"By jingo! I think so myself," Hans agreed.

They saw him marching out of camp. But they did not see the instant and terrible change that took place as soon as he was hidden by the forest. He no longer marched. At once he became a thing of the wild. He moved along softly, like a cat, a passing shadow that appeared and disappeared among the shadows. He knew how to use every cover. He could crawl on his belly like a snake. Like a snake, he could leap and strike. He could take a ptarmigan from its nest. He could kill a rabbit as it slept. He could snap in mid air the little chipmunks that fled a second too late for the trees. Fish in open pools were not too quick for him. Beaver mending their dams were not too watchful. He killed to eat, not from a wish to be cruel. But he preferred to eat what he killed himself. So a playful humor ran through his deeds. It was his delight to steal upon the squirrels. When he almost had them, he let them go. They chattered in deadly fear as they ran to the treetops.

As the fall of the year came on, the moose appeared in greater numbers. They moved slowly down to meet the winter in the lower and less harsh valleys. Buck had already dragged down a stray half-grown calf. But he wished strongly for larger and more fearsome prey. He came upon it one day on the divide at the head of the creek. A group of twenty moose had crossed over from the land of streams and timber. Chief among them was a great bull. He was in a savage temper. He stood over six feet from the ground. He was as fearsome an opponent as even Buck could desire. Back and forth the bull tossed his great flat antlers. They branched to fourteen points and spread seven feet within the tips. His small eyes burned with a vicious and bitter light. He roared with fury at the sight of Buck.

From the side of the bull, just forward of the flank, came the end of a feathered arrow. This explained why he was so angry. Buck followed an instinct from the old hunting days of the ancient world. He set out to separate the bull from the herd. It was not an easy task. He barked and danced in front of the bull, just out of reach of the great horns and the terrible wide feet. Those feet could have killed him with a single blow. The bull could not turn his back on the danger with sharp teeth and go on. So he was driven into fits of rage. At such moments he charged at Buck. Buck retreated in a smart way, drawing him on by pretending he could not escape. But when the bull was separated from his fellows, two or three of the younger bulls would charge back at Buck. This let the wounded bull return to the herd.

There is a patience of the wild. It is stubborn, never tiring and persistent as life itself. It holds the spider still in its web for endless hours. It holds the snake still in its coils. It holds the panther still in its hiding place. This patience belongs especially to life when it hunts its living food. And it belonged to Buck as he stayed close to the side of the herd. He slowed its march. He made the young bulls angry. He worried the cows with their half-grown calves. He drove the wounded bull mad with helpless rage. This went on for half a day. Buck seemed to be everywhere, attacking from all sides. He wrapped the herd in a storm of threat. He cut out his victim as fast as it could return to its mates. He wore out the patience of creatures being hunted. That patience is less than the patience of creatures that hunt.

As the day went on and the sun dropped to its bed in the northwest (the darkness had come back and the fall nights were six hours long), the young bulls went back more and more unwillingly to help their leader in trouble. The coming winter was driving them down to lower levels. It seemed they could never shake off this tireless creature that held them back. Also, it was not the life of the herd, or of the young bulls, that was threatened. Only one member's life was demanded. That was a less important interest than their own lives, and in the end they were content to pay the price.

As twilight fell the old bull stood with lowered head, watching his mates—the cows he had known, the calves he had fathered, the bulls he had mastered—as they moved on at a rapid pace through the fading light. He could not follow, for before his nose jumped the merciless fanged terror that would not let him go. He weighed three hundredweight more than half a ton. He had lived a long, strong life, full of fight and struggle, and at the end he faced death at the teeth of a creature whose head did not reach beyond his great knuckled knees.

From then on, night and day, Buck never left his prey. He never gave it a moment's rest. He never let it eat the leaves of trees or the shoots of young birch and willow. Nor did he give the wounded bull a chance to drink his burning thirst in the slender streams they crossed. Often, in desperation, the bull burst into long runs. At such times Buck did not try to stop him. He ran easily at his heels, satisfied with the way the game was played. He lay down when the moose stood still. He attacked him fiercely when he tried to eat or drink.

The great head drooped more and more under its tree of horns, and the shambling trot grew weaker and weaker. He took to standing for long periods, with nose to the ground and dejected ears dropped limply. And Buck found more time to get water for himself and to rest. At such moments, panting with red lolling tongue and with eyes fixed upon the big bull, it seemed to Buck that a change was coming over the face of things. He could feel a new stir in the land. As the moose were coming into the land, other kinds of life were coming in. Forest and stream and air seemed palpitant with their presence. The news of it came to him, not by sight, or sound, or smell, but by some other and subtler sense. He heard nothing, saw nothing, yet knew that the land was somehow different. Through it strange things were afoot and ranging. And he resolved to investigate after he had finished the business in hand.

At last, at the end of the fourth day, he pulled the great moose down. For a day and a night he remained by the kill, eating and sleeping, turn and turn about. Then, rested, refreshed and strong, he turned his face toward camp and John Thornton. He broke into the long easy lope and went on, hour after hour. He was never at a loss for the tangled way. He headed straight home through strange country with a certitude of direction that put man and his magnetic needle to shame.

As he held on he became more and more conscious of the new stir in the land. There was life abroad in it different from the life which had been there throughout the summer. No longer did this fact come to him in some subtle, mysterious way. The birds talked of it, the squirrels chattered about it, the very breeze whispered of it. Several times he stopped and drew in the fresh morning air in great sniffs. He read a message which made him leap on with greater speed. He was oppressed with a sense of calamity happening, if it were not calamity already happened. And as he crossed the last watershed and dropped down into the valley toward camp, he went with greater caution.

Three miles away he came upon a fresh trail that sent his neck hair rippling and bristling. It led straight toward camp and John Thornton. Buck hurried on, swiftly and stealthily. Every nerve was straining and tense, alert to the multitudinous details which told a story—all but the end. His nose gave him a varying description of the passage of the life on the heels of which he was travelling. He noticed the heavy silence of the forest. The bird life had flitted. The squirrels were in hiding. He saw only one—a sleek gray fellow, flattened against a gray dead limb so that he seemed a part of it, a woody growth upon the wood itself.

As Buck slid along with the darkness of a gliding shadow, his nose was jerked suddenly to the side as though a positive force had gripped and pulled it. He followed the new scent into a thicket and found Nig. He was lying on his side, dead where he had dragged himself, an arrow sticking out, head and feathers, from either side of his body.

A hundred yards farther on, Buck came upon one of the sled-dogs Thornton had bought in Dawson. This dog was thrashing about in a death-struggle, directly on the trail, and Buck passed around him without stopping. From the camp came the faint sound of many voices, rising and falling in a sing-song chant. Crawling forward to the edge of the clearing, he found Hans, lying on his face, covered with arrows like a porcupine. At the same instant Buck looked out where the spruce-bough lodge had been and saw what made his hair jump straight up on his neck and shoulders. A gust of overpowering rage swept over him. He did not know that he growled, but he growled aloud with a terrible fierceness. For the last time in his life he allowed passion to take the place of cunning and reason, and it was because of his great love for John Thornton that he lost his head.

The Yeehats were dancing around the broken pieces of the spruce-bough lodge when they heard a terrible roar. They saw an animal rushing at them, a kind they had never seen before. It was Buck, a living storm of anger, throwing himself at them wildly to destroy. He jumped at the first man (it was the chief of the Yeehats). He tore the man's throat wide open until the torn jugular shot out a fountain of blood. He did not stop to bite the victim. He tore in passing, and with the next jump he tore wide the throat of a second man. No one could stop him. He jumped about in their very middle, tearing, ripping, destroying, in constant and terrible motion that beat the arrows they shot at him. In fact, his movements were so unbelievably fast, and the Indians were so closely mixed together, that they shot one another with the arrows. One young hunter threw a spear at Buck in mid air. It went through the chest of another hunter with such force that the point broke through the skin of the back and stood out beyond. Then panic took the Yeehats, and they ran in terror to the woods, shouting as they ran that the Evil Spirit had come.

And truly Buck was the Devil in the flesh, raging at their heels and pulling them down like deer as they ran through the trees. It was a terrible day for the Yeehats. They scattered far and wide over the country. It was not until a week later that the last of the survivors gathered together in a lower valley and counted their losses. As for Buck, he grew tired of the chase and returned to the empty camp. He found Pete where he had been killed in his blankets in the first moment of surprise. Thornton's desperate struggle was freshly written on the earth, and Buck smelled every detail of it down to the edge of a deep pool. By the edge, head and front feet in the water, lay Skeet, faithful to the last. The pool itself, muddy and changed in color from the sluice boxes, completely hid what it contained, and it contained John Thornton. For Buck followed his track into the water, and from it no track led away.

All day Buck stayed by the pool or walked without rest about the camp. He knew death, as a stopping of movement, as a passing out and away from the lives of the living, and he knew John Thornton was dead. It left a great empty space in him, somewhat like hunger, but a space that ached and ached, and that food could not fill. At times, when he paused to look at the dead bodies of the Yeehats, he forgot the pain of it. At such times he felt a great pride in himself, a pride greater than any he had yet felt. He had killed man, the noblest game of all, and he had killed in the face of the law of club and fang. He smelled the bodies with interest. They had died so easily. It was harder to kill a husky dog than them. They were no match at all, were it not for their arrows and spears and clubs. From then on he would not be afraid of them except when they carried in their hands their arrows, spears, and clubs.

Night came on, and a full moon rose high over the trees into the sky. It lit the land until it lay washed in a strange day. And with the coming of the night, as Buck stayed by the pool and mourned, he became aware of a stirring of new life in the forest other than that which the Yeehats had made. He stood up, listening and smelling. From far away came a faint, sharp yelp, followed by a chorus of similar sharp yelps. As the moments passed the yelps grew closer and louder. Again Buck knew them as things heard in that other world which stayed in his memory. He walked to the middle of the open space and listened. It was the call, the many-noted call, sounding more inviting and powerful than ever before. And as never before, he was ready to obey. John Thornton was dead. The last tie was broken. Man and the claims of man no longer held him.

The wolf pack had been hunting their living meat, as the Yeehats were hunting it, on the sides of the migrating moose. At last they had crossed over from the land of streams and timber and invaded Buck's valley. Into the clearing where the moonlight streamed, they poured in a silver flood. And in the middle of the clearing stood Buck, still as a statue, waiting for them to come. They were awed, he stood so still and large, and a short pause fell, until the boldest one jumped straight for him. Like a flash Buck struck, breaking the neck. Then he stood, without movement, as before, the stricken wolf rolling in pain behind him. Three others tried it in quick succession. One after the other they drew back, bleeding from cut throats or shoulders.

This was enough to throw the whole pack forward, in disorder, crowded together, blocked and confused by its eagerness to pull down the prey. Buck's wonderful quickness and agility stood him in good stead. Turning on his hind legs, and snapping and cutting, he was everywhere at once. He showed a front that was apparently unbroken, so swiftly did he turn and guard from side to side. But to stop them from getting behind him, he was forced back, down past the pool and into the creek bed, until he came up against a high gravel bank. He worked along to a right angle in the bank which the men had made in the course of mining. In this angle he came to bay, protected on three sides and with nothing to do but face the front.

And he faced it so well that at the end of half an hour the wolves drew back, defeated. The tongues of all were out and hanging, the white fangs showing cruelly white in the moonlight. Some were lying down with heads raised and ears pointed forward. Others stood on their feet, watching him. And still others were lapping water from the pool. One wolf, long and lean and gray, came forward cautiously, in a friendly manner, and Buck recognized the wild brother with whom he had run for a night and a day. He was whining softly, and, as Buck whined, they touched noses.

Then an old wolf came forward. It was thin and had scars from many fights. Buck pulled back his lips and showed his teeth, almost ready to growl. But then he touched noses with the old wolf. After that, the old wolf sat down, pointed its nose at the moon, and began the long wolf cry. The others sat down and cried too. And now the call came to Buck in clear, certain sounds. He sat down and cried too. When this was over, he came out of his corner and the pack crowded around him. They sniffed him in a half-friendly, half-wild way. The leaders raised the cry of the pack and jumped away into the woods. The wolves followed behind, crying together. And Buck ran with them, side by side with the wild brother, crying as he ran.

And here the story of Buck may well end. After a few years, the Yeehats noticed a change in the breed of timber wolves. Some were seen with patches of brown on head and nose, and with a line of white running down the chest. But more surprising than this, the Yeehats tell of a Ghost Dog that runs at the head of the pack. They are afraid of this Ghost Dog. It is more clever than they are. It steals from their camps in hard winters, robs their traps, kills their dogs, and defies their bravest hunters.

No, the tale grows worse. Some hunters fail to return to the camp. Other hunters were found by their tribesmen with throats cut cruelly open. Around them in the snow were wolf prints bigger than the prints of any wolf. Each fall, when the Yeehats follow the moose, there is a certain valley they never enter. And some women become sad when word goes around the fire of how the Evil Spirit came to choose that valley as a place to live.

In the summers, however, there is one visitor to that valley of whom the Yeehats do not know. It is a great wolf with a beautiful coat. He is like all other wolves, and yet not like them. He crosses alone from the smiling forest land and comes down into an open space among the trees. Here a yellow stream flows from old moose-hide sacks and sinks into the ground. Long grasses grow through it, and dead plant matter covers it and hides its yellow from the sun. And here he thinks for a time, crying once, long and sadly, before he leaves.

But he is not always alone. When the long winter nights come and the wolves follow their food into the lower valleys, he may be seen running at the head of the pack. He runs through the pale moonlight or the shining northern lights. He leaps huge above his fellows. His great throat sounds out as he sings a song of the younger world, which is the song of the pack.
