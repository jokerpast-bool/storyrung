FOREWORD.

Between the far away past history of the world, and the time near to us, there was a period when the wisdom of ancient times was dead and gone. Our own days of light had not yet come. In that period there lay a great black gap in human history. It was a gap of ignorance, of superstition, of cruelty, and of wickedness.

We call that time the dark or middle ages.

Few records remain to us of that dreadful period in the history of our world. We know of it only through broken and disjointed pieces that have been passed down to us through the generations.

Yet even though the life of the world then was so wicked and black, there still remained a few good men and women here and there. Most of them lived in peaceful and quiet monasteries, far from the thunder and the glare of the world's bloody battle. They knew the right and the truth and lived according to what they knew. They preserved and tenderly cared for the truths that the dear Christ taught, and lived and died for in Palestine so long ago.

This tale that I am about to tell is of a little boy who lived and suffered in those dark middle ages. It tells how he saw both the good and the bad of men. It tells how, by gentleness and love and not by strife and hatred, he came at last to stand above other men and to be looked up to by all. And if you follow the story to the end, I hope you may find it a pleasure, as I have done. I hope you may enjoy walking through those dark ancient castles. I hope you may enjoy lying with little Otto and Brother John in the high belfry-tower. I hope you may enjoy sitting with them in the peaceful quiet of the sunny old monastery garden. Of all the story, I love best those early peaceful years that little Otto spent in the dear old White Cross on the Hill.

Poor little Otto's life was a stony and a thorny pathway. It is well for all of us nowadays that we walk it in fancy and not in truth.

I. The Dragon’s House.

Up from the gray rocks, rising sheer and bold and bare, stood the walls and towers of Castle Drachenhausen. A great gate-way had a heavy iron-pointed portcullis hanging suspended in the dim arch above. It yawned blackly upon the bascule or falling drawbridge. That drawbridge spanned a chasm between the blank stone walls and the roadway. The roadway ran winding down the steep rocky slope to the little valley just beneath. There in the lap of the hills around stood the wretched straw-thatched huts of the peasants belonging to the castle. These miserable serfs were half timid, half fierce. They tilled their poor patches of ground, wrenching from the hard soil barely enough to keep body and soul together. Among those vile hovels played the little children like foxes about their dens. Their wild, fierce eyes peered out from under a mat of tangled yellow hair.

Beyond these squalid huts lay the rushing, foaming river. A high, rude, stone bridge crossed it where the road from the castle met the water. Beyond the river stretched the great, black forest. Within its gloomy depths the savage wild beasts made their lair. In winter time the howling wolves chased their flying prey across the moonlit snow. They ran under the net-work of the black shadows from the naked boughs above.

The watchman stood in the cold, windy bartizan or watch-tower that clung to the gray walls above the castle gateway. He looked from his narrow window, where the wind piped and hummed. He looked across the tree-tops that rolled in endless billows of green. He looked over hill and over valley to the blue and distant slope of the Keiserberg. There, on the mountain side, glimmered far away the walls of Castle Trutz-Drachen.

The gaping gateway led through massive stone walls. Within stood three great cheerless brick buildings. They were so forbidding that even the yellow sunlight could not light them into brightness. They looked down with row upon row of windows upon three sides of the bleak, stone courtyard. Back of and above them clustered a jumble of other buildings, tower and turret. One high-peaked roof overtopped another.

The great house in the centre was the Baron's Hall. The part to the left was called the Roderhausen. Between the two stood a huge square pile. It rose dizzily up into the clear air high above the rest. It was the great Melchior Tower.

At the top clustered a jumble of buildings hanging high aloft in the windy space. There was a crooked wooden belfry, a tall, narrow watch-tower, and a rude wooden house. That house clung partly to the roof of the great tower and partly to the walls.

From the chimney of this crazy hut a thin thread of smoke would now and then rise into the air. Folk lived far up in that empty, airy desert. Oftentimes wild, uncouth little children were seen playing on the edge of the dizzy height. Sometimes they sat with their bare legs hanging down over the sheer depths as they gazed below at what was going on in the court-yard. There they sat, just as little children in the town might sit upon their father's door-step. The sparrows might fly around the feet of the little town children. In the same way, the circling flocks of rooks and daws flew around the feet of these air-born creatures.

It was Schwartz Carl and his wife and little ones who lived far up there in the Melchior Tower. The tower overlooked the top of the hill behind the castle and so down into the valley upon the further side. There, day after day, Schwartz Carl kept watch upon the gray road. It ran like a ribbon through the valley from the rich town of Gruenstaldt to the rich town of Staffenburgen. Merchant caravans passed from the one to the other. The lord of Drachenhausen was a robber baron.

Dong! Dong! The great alarm bell would suddenly ring out from the belfry high up upon the Melchior Tower. Dong! Dong! Till the rooks and daws whirled clamoring and screaming. Dong! Dong! Till the fierce wolf-hounds in the rocky kennels behind the castle stables howled dismally in answer. Dong! Dong!--Dong! Dong!

A great noise and confusion and hurry would follow in the castle courtyard below. Men shouted and called to one another. Armor rang, and horses' feet clattered on the hard stone. The iron-pointed gate would be slowly raised with the creaking and groaning of the winch. Then the bridge would fall with a clank and rattle and clash of iron chains. Then horse and man would thunder over it. They clattered away down the winding, stone pathway until the great forest swallowed them, and they were gone.

Then for a while peace would fall upon the castle courtyard. The cock would crow. The cook would scold a lazy maid. And Gretchen, leaning out of a window, would sing a bit of a song, just as though it were a peaceful farm-house, instead of a den of thieves.

Maybe it would be evening before the men returned once more. Perhaps one would have a bloody cloth tied about his head. Perhaps one would carry his arm in a sling. Perhaps one—maybe more than one—would be left behind, never to return again. He would soon be forgotten by all except some poor woman who would weep silently in the loneliness of her daily work.

Nearly always the adventurers would bring back pack-horses loaded with bundles of goods. Sometimes, besides these, they would return with a poor soul. His hands were tied behind his back and his feet beneath the horse's body. His fur coat and his flat cap were wofully crooked. For a while he would disappear in some dark cell of the dungeon-keep. Then an envoy would come from the town with a fat purse. His ransom would be paid, the dungeon would let him out, and he would be allowed to go on his way again.

One man always rode beside Baron Conrad in his expeditions and adventures. He was a short, deep-chested, broad-shouldered man. His strong arms were so long that when he stood his hands hung nearly to his knees.

His rough, close-cut hair came so low upon his brow that only a strip of forehead showed between it and his thick, black eyebrows. One eye was blind. The other twinkled and gleamed like a spark under the roof of his brows. Many folk said that the one-eyed Hans had drunk beer with the Hill-man, who had given him the strength of ten. For he could bend an iron spit like a hazel twig. He could lift a barrel of wine from the floor to his head as easily as though it were a basket of eggs.

As for the one-eyed Hans, he never said that he had not drunk beer with the Hill-man. For he liked the credit that such reports gave him with the other folk. And so, like a half savage mastiff, faithful to death to his master, but to him alone, he went his sullen way and lived his sullen life within the castle walls. He was half respected, half feared by the other inmates. For it was dangerous trifling with the one-eyed Hans.

II. How the Baron went Forth to Shear.

Baron Conrad and Baroness Matilda sat together at their morning meal. Below their raised seats stretched the long, heavy wooden table, loaded with coarse food. There was black bread, boiled cabbage, bacon, eggs, a great chine from a wild boar, sausages, such as we eat nowadays, and flagons and jars of beer and wine. Along the board sat ranged in the order of the household the followers and retainers. Four or five slatternly women and girls served the others as they fed noisily at the table. They moved here and there behind the men with wooden or pewter dishes of food. Now and then they laughed at the jests that passed or joined in the talk. A huge fire blazed and crackled and roared in the great open fireplace. Before it were stretched two fierce, shaggy, wolfish-looking hounds. Outside, the rain beat upon the roof or ran trickling from the eaves. Every now and then a chill draught of wind would breathe through the open windows of the great black dining-hall and set the fire roaring.

Along the dull-gray wall of stone hung pieces of armor, and swords and lances, and great branching antlers of the stag. Overhead arched the rude, heavy, oaken beams, blackened with age and smoke. Underfoot was a chill pavement of stone.

Upon Baron Conrad's shoulder leaned the pale, slender, yellow-haired Baroness. She was the only one in all the world with whom the fierce lord of Drachenhausen softened to gentleness. She was the only one upon whom his savage brows looked kindly. To her his harsh voice softened with love.

The Baroness was talking to her husband in a low voice, as he looked down into her pale face, with its gentle blue eyes.

"And wilt thou not, then," said she, "do that one thing for me?"

"Nay," he growled, in his deep voice. "I cannot promise thee never more to attack the towns-people in the valley over yonder. How else could I live an' I did not take from the fat town hogs to fill our own larder?"

"Nay," said the Baroness. "Thou couldst live as some others do, for all do not rob the burgher folk as thou dost. Alas! mishap will come upon thee some day, and if thou shouldst be slain, what then would come of me?"

"Prut," said the Baron. "Thy foolish fears." But he laid his rough, hairy hand softly upon the Baroness' head and stroked her yellow hair.

"For my sake, Conrad," whispered the Baroness.

A pause followed. The Baron sat looking thoughtfully down into the Baroness' face. A moment more, and he might have promised what she besought. A moment more, and he might have been saved all the bitter trouble that was to follow. But it was not to be.

Suddenly a harsh sound broke the quietness of all into a confusion of noises. Dong! Dong!—it was the great alarm-bell from Melchior's Tower.

The Baron started at the sound. He sat for a moment or two with his hand clinched upon the arm of his seat as though about to rise. Then he sunk back into his chair again.

All the others had risen tumultuously from the table. Now they stood looking at him, awaiting his orders.

"For my sake, Conrad," said the Baroness again.

Dong! Dong! rang the alarm-bell. The Baron sat with his eyes bent upon the floor, scowling blackly.

The Baroness took his hand in both of hers. "For my sake," she pleaded. The tears filled her blue eyes as she looked up at him. "Do not go this time."

From the courtyard outside came the sound of horses' feet clashing against the stone pavement. Those in the hall stood watching and wondering at this strange delay of the Lord Baron. Just then the door opened and one came pushing past the rest. It was the one-eyed Hans. He came straight to where the Baron sat. Leaning over, he whispered something into his master's ear.

“For my sake,” begged the Baroness again. But the decision was made. The Baron pushed back his chair heavily and stood up. “Forward!” he roared in a very loud voice. A great shout answered him as he walked loudly down the hall and out of the open door.

The Baroness covered her face with her hands and cried.

“Never mind, little bird,” said old Ursela, the nurse, in a gentle voice. “He will come back to you again as he has come back to you before.”

But the poor young Baroness kept crying with her face buried in her hands. He had not done the thing she had asked.

A white young face with yellow hair looked out into the courtyard from a window above. But if Baron Conrad of Drachenhausen saw it from under the bars of his shining helmet, he gave no sign.

“Forward,” he cried again.

Down came the bridge with a loud noise. They rode away with noisy feet and ringing armor through the gray curtain of falling rain.

The day had passed and the evening had come. The Baroness and her women sat beside a big fire. All were talking and laughing except two—the fair young Baroness and old Ursela. One sat listening, listening, listening. The other sat with her chin resting in the palm of her hand, silently watching her young mistress. The night was falling gray and chill when suddenly the clear notes of a horn rang from outside the castle walls. The young Baroness started, and a pink light flashed up into her pale cheeks.

“Yes, good,” said old Ursela. “The red fox has come back to his den again. I am sure he brings a fat town goose in his mouth. Now we will have fine clothes to wear, and you another gold chain to hang about your pretty neck.”

The young Baroness laughed happily at the old woman’s words. “This time,” she said, “I will choose a string of pearls like the one my aunt used to wear. I had it about my neck when Conrad first saw me.”

Minute after minute passed. The Baroness sat nervously playing with a bracelet of golden beads about her wrist. “How long he stays,” she said.

“Yes,” said Ursela. “But it is not cousin wish that holds him by the coat.”

As she spoke, a door banged in the hallway outside. The ring of iron footsteps sounded on the stone floor. Clank! Clank! Clank!

The Baroness stood up, her face all bright. The door opened. Then the flush of joy faded away and her face grew white, white, white. One hand held the back of the bench where she had been sitting. The other hand pressed tightly against her side.

It was Hans the one-eyed who stood in the doorway. Black trouble sat on his brow. All were looking at him, waiting.

“Conrad,” whispered the Baroness at last. “Where is Conrad? Where is your master?” Even her lips were white as she spoke.

The one-eyed Hans said nothing.

Just then came the noise of men’s voices in the corridor. There was the shuffle and struggle of feet carrying a heavy load. Nearer and nearer they came, and one-eyed Hans stood aside. Six men came struggling through the doorway, carrying a bed for the sick and hurt. On it lay the great Baron Conrad. The flaming torch thrust into the iron bracket against the wall flashed up with the draft of air from the open door. The light fell upon the white face and the closed eyes. It showed on his body armor a great red stain that was not the stain of rust.

Suddenly Ursela cried out in a sharp, high voice, “Catch her, she falls!”

It was the Baroness.

Then the old woman turned fiercely upon the one-eyed Hans. “You fool!” she cried. “Why did you bring him here? You have killed your lady!”

“I did not know,” said the one-eyed Hans, in a dull way.

III. How the Baron came Home Shorn.

But Baron Conrad was not dead. For days he lay upon his hard bed. Sometimes he muttered unclear words beneath his red beard. Sometimes he raved fiercely with the fever of his wound. But one day he woke again to the things about him.

He turned his head first to one side and then to the other. There sat Schwartz Carl and the one-eyed Hans. Two or three other servants stood by a great window that looked out into the courtyard beneath. They were joking and laughing together in low voices. One lay upon the heavy oak bench that stood along by the wall, sleeping noisily.

“Where is your lady?” said the Baron presently. “And why is she not with me at this time?”

The man that lay upon the bench started up at the sound of his voice. Those at the window came hurrying to his bed. But Schwartz Carl and the one-eyed Hans looked at one another, and neither of them spoke. The Baron saw the look and read a certain meaning in it. It brought him to his elbow, though only to sink back upon his pillow again with a groan.

“Why do you not answer me?” said he at last, in a hollow voice. Then to the one-eyed Hans, “Have you no tongue, fool, that you stand there staring like a fish? Answer me, where is your mistress?”

“I—I do not know,” stammered poor Hans.

For a while the Baron lay silently looking from one face to the other. Then he spoke again. “How long have I been lying here?” said he.

“A week, my lord,” said Master Rudolph, the steward. He had come into the room and now stood among the others at the bed.

“A week,” repeated the Baron in a low voice. Then to Master Rudolph, “And has the Baroness been often beside me in that time?” Master Rudolph hesitated. “Answer me,” said the Baron in a hard voice.

“Not—not often,” said Master Rudolph, slowly and with doubt.

The Baron lay silent for a long time. At last he passed his hands over his face and held them there for a minute. Then suddenly, before anyone knew what he was about to do, he rose upon his elbow and then sat upright upon the bed. The green wound broke out again. A dark red spot grew and spread upon the linen coverings. His face was drawn and tired with the pain of his moving. His eyes were wild and red. Great drops of sweat gathered and stood upon his forehead as he sat there swaying slightly from side to side.

“My shoes,” said he, in a rough, low voice.

Master Rudolph stepped forward. “But, my Lord Baron,” he began, and then stopped short. The Baron shot him such a look that his tongue stood still in his head.

Hans saw that look out of his one eye. Down he dropped upon his knees. He felt under the bed and brought forth a pair of soft leather shoes. He slipped them upon the Baron’s feet and then tied the straps above the top of his foot.

“Your shoulder,” said the Baron. He stood up slowly. The pain made him hold Hans so hard that the man winced again. For a moment he stood as if he was gathering strength. Then he started forward with determination to do the task he had set for himself.

At the door he stopped for a moment as if his weakness had overcome him. There Master Nicholas, his cousin, met him. The steward had sent one of the servants to tell the old man what the Baron was about to do.

“You must go back again, Conrad,” said Master Nicholas. “You are not fit to be outside.”

The Baron did not answer him a word. He glared at him with his red eyes and pressed his teeth together. Then he started forward again on his way.

He went down the long hall, slowly and with great effort. The others followed silently behind him. Then he went up the steep winding stairs, step by step, stopping now and then to lean against the wall. So he reached a long and dark passageway. It was lit only by the light of a little window at the far end.

He stopped at the door of one of the rooms that opened into this passageway. He stood for a moment, then he pushed it open.

No one was inside but old Ursela. She sat singing softly over a fire with a bundle on her knees. She did not see the Baron or know that he was there.

“Where is your lady?” he said, in a hollow voice.

Then the old nurse looked up with a start. “Jesu bless us,” she cried, and crossed herself.

“Where is your lady?” said the Baron again, in the same rough voice. Then, not waiting for an answer, “Is she dead?”

The old woman looked at him for a minute, blinking her wet eyes. Then she suddenly broke into a long, high cry. The Baron needed to hear no more.

As if in answer to the old woman’s cry, a thin, high sound came from the bundle in her lap.

At the sound, the red blood rushed up into the Baron’s face. “What is that you have there?” he said, pointing to the bundle on the old woman’s knees.

She pulled back the covers. There lay a poor, weak, little baby. It raised its faint, thin cry once again.

“It is your son,” said Ursela, “that the dear Baroness left behind her when the holy angels took her to Paradise. She blessed him and called him Otto before she left us.”

IV. The White Cross on the Hill.

Here the clear waters of the River Rhine run smoothly around a point of land that sticks out. The water holds a picture of the blue sky and white clouds floating above. St. Michaelsburg rises from the banks of the stream, which are covered with tall grass. It sweeps up with a smooth swell until it cuts sharp and clear against the sky. Short vineyards covered its dark soil. Field and garden and orchard crowned its top, where lay the Monastery of St. Michaelsburg--“The White Cross on the Hill.” There within the white walls, where the warm yellow sunlight slept, all was peaceful and quiet. The quiet was broken only now and then by the crowing of the rooster or the loud noise of a hen, the lowing of cows or the crying of goats, a single voice in prayer, the faint sound of distant singing, or the deep ring of the monastery bell from the high bell tower. The tower looked over the hill and valley and the smooth, far-winding stream. No other sounds broke the silence. In this peaceful place no one ever heard the clash of armor, the ring of iron-shod hooves, or the rough call to arms.

Not all men were wicked and cruel and fierce in that dark, far-away age. Not all were robbers and terrifying tyrants, even in that time when men’s hands were against their neighbors, and war and stealing lived in place of peace and justice.

Abbot Otto, of St. Michaelsburg, was a gentle, patient, pale-faced old man. His white hands were soft and smooth. No one would have thought that they could have known the hard touch of sword handle and spear. And yet, in the days of the Emperor Frederick--the grandson of the great Red-beard--no one stood higher in skill at arms than he. But all at once--for why, no man could tell--a change came over him. In the flower of his youth and fame and growing power, he gave up everything in life. He entered the quiet shelter of that white monastery on the hillside, so far away from the noise and conflict of the world in which he had lived.

Some said that it was because the lady he had loved had loved his brother. They said that when they were married, Otto of Wolbergen had left the church with a broken heart.

But such stories are old songs that have been sung before.

Clatter! clatter! Jingle! jingle! It was a fully armed knight who came riding up the steep hill road. The road wound from left to right and right to left among the vineyards on the slopes of St. Michaelsburg. His polished helmet and chest armor blazed in the noon sunlight, for no knight in those days dared to ride the roads except in full armor. In front of him, the lone knight carried a bundle wrapped in the folds of his rough gray cloak.

It was a very sick man that rode up the heights of St. Michaelsburg. His head hung upon his breast from the faintness of tiredness and pain. For it was the Baron Conrad.

He had left his bed of sickness that morning. He had saddled his horse in the gray dawn with his own hands. He had ridden away into the misty twilight of the forest without anyone knowing except the gatekeeper. The gatekeeper winked and blinked in the confusion of his broken sleep. He had opened the gates to the sick man, hardly knowing what he was doing, until he saw his master far away, clattering down the steep bridle path.

Eight leagues had he ridden that day with neither a stop nor a rest. But now at last the end of his journey had come. He pulled his horse to a stop under the shade of the great wooden gateway of St. Michaelsburg.

He reached up to the knotted rope and gave it a pull. From inside came the answering ring of the bell at the gate. After a while a little door opened in the great wooden gates. The gentle, wrinkled face of old Brother Benedict, who kept the gate, looked out at the strange visitor in iron armor. He also looked at the great black war-horse. The horse was streaked and wet with the sweat of the journey. It was marked with spots of foam. A few words passed between them. Then the little window was closed again. Inside, the shuffling sound of feet in sandals grew fainter and fainter. Brother Benedict was carrying the message from Baron Conrad to Abbot Otto. The figure in armor was left alone. He sat there as silent as a statue.

Soon the footsteps sounded again. There was a noise of chains knocking together. The key turned in the lock. The bolts were pulled back with a rough sound. Then the gate swung slowly open. Baron Conrad rode into the shelter of the White Cross. The hooves of his war-horse hit the stones of the courtyard inside. The wooden gate swung slowly shut behind him.

Abbot Otto stood by the table when Baron Conrad entered the high room from the far end. The light from the window behind the old man fell in broken rays upon him. It seemed to frame his thin gray hairs with a golden glory. His white, delicate hand rested upon the table beside him. Upon the table were some sheets of paper covered with rows of ancient Greek writing. He had been working to read them.

Clank! clank! clank! Baron Conrad walked across the stone floor. Then he stopped short in front of the good old man.

"What do you seek here, my son?" said the Abbot.

"I seek sanctuary for my son and your brother's grandson," said the Baron Conrad. He threw back the folds of his cloak and showed the face of the sleeping baby.

For a while the Abbot said nothing. He stood looking dreamily at the baby. After a while he looked up. "And the child's mother," said he--"what does she have to say at this?"

"She has nothing to say," said Baron Conrad, hoarsely. Then he stopped short in his speech. "She is dead," said he, at last, in a husky voice, "and is with God's angels in paradise."

The Abbot looked intently in the Baron's face. "So!" said he, under his breath. Then for the first time he noticed how white and drawn was the Baron's face. "Are you sick yourself?" he asked.

"Ay," said the Baron, "I have come from death's door. But that is no matter. Will you take this little baby into sanctuary? My house is a vile, rough place. It is not fit for such as he, and his mother is with the blessed saints in heaven." And once more Conrad of Drachenhausen's face began twitching with the pain of his thoughts.

"Yes," said the old man, gently, "he shall live here." He stretched out his hands and took the baby. "Would," said he, "that all the little children in these dark times might be thus brought to the house of God. There they would learn mercy and peace, instead of rapine and war."

For a while he stood looking down in silence at the baby in his arms. But his mind was far away upon other things. At last he roused himself with a start. "And you," said he to the Baron Conrad--"has not your heart been chastened and softened by this? Surely you will not go back to your old life of rapine and extortion?"

"Nay," said Baron Conrad, gruffly, "I will rob the city swine no longer. That was the last thing that my dear one asked of me."

The old Abbot's face lit up with a smile. "I am right glad that your heart was softened. I am glad that you are willing at last to cease from war and violence."

"Nay," cried the Baron, roughly, "I said nothing of ceasing from war. By heaven, no! I will have revenge!" He clashed his iron foot upon the floor. He clinched his fists and ground his teeth together. "Listen," said he, "and I will tell you how my troubles happened. A fortnight ago I rode out upon an expedition against a caravan of fat burghers in the valley of Gruenhoffen. They outnumbered us many to one. But city swine such as they are not of the stuff to stand against our kind for a long time. Nevertheless, the men-at-arms who guarded the caravan stayed us with pike and cross-bow. They stood behind a tree which they had felled in front of a high bridge. The others had driven the pack-horses off. So by the time we had forced the bridge they were a league or more away. We pushed after them as hard as we were able. But when we came up with them we found that they had been joined by Baron Frederick of Trutz-Drachen. For three years and more the burghers of Gruenstadt have been paying him a tribute for his protection against others. Then again they made a stand, and this time the Baron Frederick himself was with them. But though the dogs fought well, we were forcing them back. We might have got the better of them, had not my horse stumbled upon a sloping stone. He fell and rolled over upon me. While I lay there with my horse upon me, Baron Frederick ran me down with his lance. He gave me that foul wound that came so near to slaying me--and did slay my dear wife. Nevertheless, my men were able to bring me out from that press and away. We had bitten the Trutz-Drachen dogs so deep that they were too sore to follow us. So they let us go our way in peace. But when those fools of mine brought me to my castle they bore me lying upon a litter to my wife's chamber. There she beheld me. Thinking me dead, she swooned a death-swoon. She only lived long enough to bless her new-born baby and name it Otto, for you, her father's brother. But, by heavens! I will have revenge, root and branch, upon that vile tribe, the Roderburgs of Trutz-Drachen. Their great-grandsire built that castle in scorn of Baron Casper in the old days. Their grandsire slew my father's grandsire. Baron Nicholas slew two of our kindred. And now this Baron Frederick gives me that foul wound and kills my dear wife through my body." Here the Baron stopped short. Then of a sudden he shook his fist above his head. He cried out in his hoarse voice: "I swear by all the saints in heaven, either the red cock shall crow over the roof of Trutz-Drachen or else it shall crow over my house! The black dog shall sit on Baron Frederick's shoulders or else he shall sit on mine!" Again he stopped. He fixed his blazing eyes upon the old man. "Do you hear that, priest?" said he, and broke into a great boisterous laugh.

Abbot Otto sighed heavily. But he did not try further to persuade the other man to think differently.

"You are wounded," he said at last, in a gentle voice. "At least stay here with us until you are healed."

"No," said the Baron, roughly. "I will stay no longer than to hear you promise to care for my child."

"I promise," said the Abbot. "But put aside your armor, and rest."

"No," said the Baron. "I go back again today."

At this the Abbot cried out in surprise: "Surely you, a wounded man, would not take that long journey without a proper rest! Think! Night will come upon you before you can reach home again, and the forests are full of wolves."

The Baron laughed. "Those are not the wolves I fear," he said. "Do not urge me further. I must return tonight. But if you wish to do me a kindness, you can give me some food to eat and a small bottle of your golden Michaelsburg. Beyond these, I ask no further favor of any man, whether he is a priest or an ordinary man."

"What comfort I can give you, you shall have," said the Abbot, in his patient voice. Then he left the room to give the necessary orders, and he took the baby with him.

First confusion took its place, and then dull alarm. “But, dear heaven,” she said, “where then has the peddler man gone?”

A moment or two of silence followed her speech. “Perhaps,” said one of the others, in a voice quiet with awe, “perhaps it was the evil one himself to whom you opened the door.”

Again there was a quiet, breathless pause. It was the girl who had let Hans in at the back gate who next spoke.

“Yes,” said she, in a voice trembling with fear at what she had done, “yes, it must have been the evil one, for now I remember he had only one eye.” The four girls crossed themselves, and their eyes grew big and round with fear.

Suddenly a shower of mortar came rattling down the chimney. “Ach!” cried the four, as with one voice. Bang! the door was slammed shut and away they ran like a flock of frightened rabbits.

When Jacob, the watchman, came that way an hour later, on his evening round of the castle, he found a peddler’s bag lying in the middle of the floor. He turned it over with his pike-staff and saw that it was full of beads and trinkets and ribbons.

“How came this here?” said he. And then, without waiting for the answer which he did not expect, he threw it over his shoulder and marched away with it.

X. How Hans Brought Terror to the Kitchen.

Hans found himself in a fine mess in the chimney. The soot got into his one eye and made it water. It got into his nose and made him sneeze. It got into his mouth and his ears and his hair. But still he struggled on, up and up. “For every chimney has a top,” said Hans to himself, “and I am sure to climb out somewhere or other.” Suddenly he came to a place where another chimney joined the one he was climbing. Here he stopped to consider the matter at his leisure. “See now,” he muttered, “if I still go upward I may come out at the top of some tall chimney-stack with no way of getting down outside. Now, below here there must be a fireplace somewhere, for a chimney does not start from nothing at all. Yes, good! We will go down a while and see what we make of that.”

It was a crooked, zigzag road that he had to travel, and rough and hard as well. His one eye stung and smarted, and his knees and elbows were rubbed raw. Still, One-eyed Hans had been in worse trouble than this in his life.

Down he went and down he went, further than he had climbed upward before. “Sure, I must be near some place or other,” he thought.

As though in instant answer to his thoughts, he heard the sudden sound of a voice so close beneath him that he stopped short in his downward climbing. He stood as still as a mouse, with his heart in his mouth. A few inches more and he would have been discovered—what would have happened then would have been no hard matter to foretell.

Hans braced his back against one side of the chimney and his feet against the other. Then he leaned forward and looked down between his knees. The gray light of the coming evening glimmered in a wide stone fireplace just below him. Within the fireplace two people were moving about upon the broad hearth, a great, fat woman and a boy with rough hair. The woman held a spit with two newly tied fowls upon it, so that One-eyed Hans knew that she must be the cook.

"You ugly toad," said the woman to the boy. "Did I not tell you to make a fire an hour ago? Now there is not even a spark to cook the birds with. And they must be prepared for the lord Baron's supper. Where have you been all this time?"

"It does not matter," said the boy, in a sour voice, as he put the sticks ready for lighting. "It does not matter. I was not running after Long Jacob, the bowman, trying to catch him for a sweetheart, as you have been doing."

The reply was quick and ready. The cook raised her hand. "Smack!" she hit him, and a loud cry from the kitchen boy followed.

"Yes, good," thought Hans, as he looked down at them. "I am glad that the boy's ear was not on my head."

"Now give me no more of your talk," said the woman. "Just do the work you were told to do." Then: "How did all this black soot get here? I would like to know."

"How should I know?" said the kitchen boy through his nose. "Perhaps you would blame that on me too?"

"That is my doing," Hans whispered to himself. "But if they light the fire, what happens to me then?"

"See now," said the cook. "I am going to make the cakes ready. If I come back and find that you have not built the fire, I will warm your other ear for you."

"So," thought Hans, "then it will be my time to come down the chimney. There will be only one of them here."

The next moment he heard the door close. He knew that the cook had gone to make the cakes ready, as she had said. As he looked down, he saw that the boy was bending over the bundle of sticks. He was blowing on the small piece of burning material he had brought in, until it became a flame. The dry sticks began to make small cracking sounds and burn brightly. "Now is my time," said Hans to himself. He pressed his elbows against each side of the chimney and straightened his legs so that he might fall clear. His movements loosened a little shower of soot. It fell rattling onto the sticks, which were now beginning to burn brightly. The boy raised his face and looked up. Hans let go of the chimney. Crash! He fell and landed on his feet in the middle of the burning sticks. The kitchen boy fell backward onto the floor. He lay on his back with a face as white as dough. His eyes and mouth were wide open. He stared without a word at the terrible black figure standing in the middle of the flames and smoke. Then his scattered thoughts came back to him. "It is the evil one," he roared. Then he turned onto his side. He half rolled and half scrambled to the door. Then he jumped out and banged the door behind him. He flew down the passageway, yelling with fear, and never once dared to look behind him.

All the time One-eyed Hans was brushing away the sparks that stuck to his clothes. He was as black as ink from head to foot with the soot from the chimney.

"So far all is good," he muttered to himself. "But if I go wandering about in my sooty shoes, I will leave black tracks to follow me. So there is nothing to do but go barefoot."

He bent down and pulled the pointed soft leather shoes from his feet. He threw them onto the now burning sticks. There they twisted and turned and wrinkled, and at last burst into flame. Meanwhile Hans lost no time. He must find a hiding place, and quickly, if he still hoped to escape. A great bread trough stood in the corner of the kitchen. It was a box shaped like a hopper, with a flat lid. It was the best hiding place that the room offered. Without further thought Hans ran to it. As he passed the table, he snatched up a loaf of black bread and a bottle half full of old wine. He had had nothing to eat since that morning. He climbed into the great bread trough and pulled the lid down over him. He curled himself up as snugly as a mouse in its nest.

For a while the kitchen was silent. At last the sound of voices was heard at the door, whispering together in low tones. Suddenly the door was thrown open. A tall, lean fellow with a long thin face, dressed in rough cloth, strode into the room. He stood there glaring around him with a boldness that was half fear. Three or four women and the trembling kitchen boy crowded together in a frightened group behind him.

The man was Long Jacob, the bowman. But after all, his boldness was all wasted. Not a thread or a hair was to be seen. There was only the crackling fire throwing its cheerful red glow upon the wall of the room. The room was now quickly growing dark in the falling gray of the twilight outside.

The fat cook's fear began quickly to turn into anger.

"You little devil," she cried. "It is one of your tricks." She made a dive for the kitchen boy. He ducked around the skirts of one of the other women and so escaped for the time. But Long Jacob wrinkled up his nose and sniffed. "No," said he. "I think there is some truth in the tale that the boy told. For here is a vile smell of burned horn that the black one has left behind him."

It was the smell from the soft leather shoes that Hans had burned.

The silence of night had fallen over the Castle of Trutz-Drachen. Not a sound was heard but the squeaking of mice running behind the wood panels, the dull dripping of moisture from the eaves, or the sighing of the night wind around the gables and through the bare windows of the castle.

The lid of the great dough trough was softly raised. A face, black with soot, peeped cautiously out from under it. Then little by little a figure as black as the face rose up. One-eyed Hans stepped out onto the floor, stretching and rubbing himself.

"I think I must have slept," he muttered. "Hui, I am as stiff as a new leather doublet. And now, what next is to become of me? I hope my luck may yet stick to me, in spite of this foul black soot!"

Along the middle of the front of the great hall of the castle ran a long stone gallery. It opened at one end onto the courtyard by a high flight of stone steps. A man-at-arms in breastplate and steel cap, bearing a long pike, paced up and down the length of this gallery. Now and then he stopped, leaned over the edge, and gazed up into the starry sky above. Then, with a long drawn yawn, he lazily turned back to the monotonous watch again.

A dark figure came out from an arched doorway at the lower part of the long straight building. It was a little below the end gallery. But the guard saw nothing of it, because his back was turned. The figure crawled along by the dark wall. It was as silent and as careful as a cat. Now and then it stopped. Then it crept slowly forward again toward the gallery. There the armed man walked up and down in a steady rhythm. It was One-eyed Hans, and his feet were bare.

Little by little, foot by foot, the black figure crawled along the angle of the wall. Little by little, foot by foot, it came nearer and nearer to the long straight row of stone steps. Those steps led to the covered gallery. At last it crouched at the lowest step of the flight. Just then the guard on watch came to the very end of the gallery. He stood there leaning on his spear. If he had looked down below, he could not have missed seeing One-eyed Hans lying there without moving. But he was gazing far away over the steep black roofs beyond. He never saw the presence no one suspected. Minute after minute passed. One stood there looking out into the night, and the other lay crouching by the wall. Then with a weary sigh the guard turned. He began to walk slowly back again toward the farther end of the gallery.

At once the still figure below rose. It moved silently and swiftly up the flight of steps.

Two rough stone pillars stood on either side of the end of the gallery. Like a shadow the black figure slipped behind one of these. It pressed itself flat against the wall. There it stood straight and still as the shadows around it.

Down the long gallery came the watchman. His sword made a loud ringing sound in the silence as he walked: tramp, tramp, tramp! ring, clank, jingle.

Within three feet of the still figure behind the pillar he turned. He began to walk back his steady steps. At once the other left the shadow of the post. It crept quickly and quietly after him. The guard took one step, two steps. For a moment the black figure behind him seemed to crouch and draw itself together. Then like a flash it leaped forward upon its victim.

A dark cloth fell upon the man's face. In an instant he was thrown back and down with a dull crash upon the stones. Then followed a fierce and silent struggle in the darkness. But strong and solid as the man was, he was no match for the almost superhuman strength of One-eyed Hans. The cloth which he had thrown over his head was tied tightly and firmly. Then the man was forced upon his face. In spite of his fierce struggles, his arms were tied around and around with strong fine cord. Next his feet were tied in the same way, and the task was done. Then Hans stood upon his feet and wiped the sweat from his dark forehead. "Listen, brother," he whispered. As he spoke he bent down and pressed something cold and hard against the neck of the other. "Do you know the feel of this? It is a broad knife. If you manage to loosen that cloth from your mouth and make any cry, it shall be put into your throat."

So saying, he pushed the knife back again into its cover. Then he bent down and picked up the other. He threw him across his shoulder like a sack. He ran down the steps as lightly as though his load was nothing at all. He carried his burden to the arched doorway whence he had come a little while before. There he first took all his prisoner's weapons from him. Then Hans sat the man up in the angle of the wall. "So, brother," said he, "now we can talk with more ease than we could up there. I will tell you honestly why I am here. It is to find where the young Baron Otto of Drachenhausen is kept. If you can tell me, well and good. If not, I must cut your throat and find me one who knows more. Now, can you tell me what I would learn, brother?"

The other nodded faintly in the darkness.

"That is good," said Hans. "Then I will loosen your cloth until you have told me. Only bear in mind what I said about my knife."

Thereupon he untied his prisoner, and the fellow slowly rose to his feet. He shook himself and looked all about him in a heavy, confused way. He looked as though he had just awakened from a dream.

His right hand slid secretly down to his side, but the knife cover was empty.

"Come, brother!" said Hans, impatiently. "Time is passing. Once lost, it can never be found again. Show me the way to the young Baron Otto or--." And he rubbed the shining blade of his knife on his hard palm.

The fellow needed no further asking. He turned and led the way. Together they were swallowed up in the wide shadows. Again the hush of night-time lay upon the Castle of Trutz-Drachen.

XI. How Otto was Saved.

Little Otto was lying upon the hard couch in his cell. He was tossing in restless and feverish sleep. Suddenly a heavy hand was laid upon him. A voice whispered in his ear, "Baron, Baron Otto, wake, rouse yourself. I am come to help you. I am One-eyed Hans."

Otto was awake in an instant. He raised himself upon his elbow in the darkness. "One-eyed Hans," he breathed. "One-eyed Hans; who is One-eyed Hans?"

"True," said the other, "you do not know me. I am your father's trusted servant. I am the only one except his own blood and kin who has stayed with him in this hour of trouble. Yes, all are gone but me alone. So I have come to help you away from this vile place."

"Oh, dear, good Hans! if only you can!" cried Otto. "If only you can take me away from this wicked place. Alas, dear Hans! I am weary and sick to death." And poor little Otto began to weep silently in the darkness.

"Yes, yes," said Hans, gruffly, "it is no place for a little child to be. Can you climb, my little master? Can you climb a knotted rope?"

"No," said Otto, "I can never climb again! See, Hans;" and he threw back the covers from off him.

"I cannot see," said Hans, "it is too dark."

"Then feel, dear Hans," said Otto.

Hans bent over the poor little white figure glimmering palely in the darkness. Suddenly he drew back with a snarl like an angry wolf. "Oh! the black, bloody wretches!" he cried, hoarsely. "And have they done that to you, a little child?"

"Yes," said Otto, "the Baron Henry did it." And then again he began to cry.

"There, there," said Hans, roughly. "Stop crying. You will get away from here even if you are not able to climb. I myself will help you. Your father is already waiting below the window here. You will soon be with him. There, there, cry no more."

“How,” cried the Baron Henry, “are you afraid of one man? Then follow me!” and he spurred forward to the bridge-head. He turned his horse and glared round upon the dull faces of his followers, until his eyes seemed fairly to burn with anger beneath the bars of his face guard.

Baron Conrad gave a roar of laughter. “How now,” he cried; “are you all afraid of one man? Is there none among you that dares come forward and meet me? I know you, Baron Henry! you are not afraid to cut off the hand of a little child. Do you not now have the courage to face the father?”

Baron Henry ground his teeth with rage as he glared around upon the faces of his soldiers. Suddenly his eye lit upon one of them. “Ha! Carl Spigler,” he cried, “you have your cross-bow with you;--shoot me down that dog! No,” he said, “you can do him no harm under his armor; shoot the horse upon which he sits.”

Baron Conrad heard the speech. “Oh! you coward villain!” he cried, “stop; do not shoot the good horse. I will get down and fight you on foot.” Thereupon, armed as he was, he jumped with a clash from his horse and turning the animal’s head, gave it a slap upon the side. The good horse first trotted and then walked to the further end of the bridge, where it stopped and began cropping at the grass that grew beside the road.

“Now then!” cried Baron Henry, fiercely, “now then, you cannot fear him, villains! Down with him! forward!”

Slowly the soldiers spurred their horses forward upon the bridge and toward that one figure that, holding tightly the great two-handed sword, stood there alone guarding the passage.

Then Baron Conrad swung the great blade above his head. It caught the sunlight and flashed again. He did not wait for the attack. When the first of the advancing riders had come within a few feet of him, he jumped with a shout upon them. The man pushed at him with his long spear, and the Baron went staggering a few feet back. But he recovered himself at once and jumped forward again. The great sword flashed in the air, whistling. It fell, and the nearest man dropped his spear with a loud noise. With a loud, unclear cry, he grabbed the hair of his horse with both hands. Again the blade whistled in the air, and this time it was stained with red. Again it fell, and with another high cry the man fell head first under the horse's feet. The next moment they were upon him. Each one tried to strike at the one figure, to ride him down, or to push him down with their spears. There was no room now to swing the long blade. But holding the handle in both hands, Baron Conrad pushed with it as though it were a spear. He stabbed at horse or man, it did not matter. Crowded upon the narrow road of the bridge, those who attacked had not only to guard themselves against the terrible strokes of that great sword. They also had to keep their wounded horses, which were rearing and mad with fear, from falling with them into the water beneath.

Presently someone raised the cry, “Back! back!” And those nearest the Baron began pulling in their horses. “Forward!” roared Baron Henry, from the middle of the crowd. But in spite of his command, and even the blows that he gave, those behind were pushed back by those in front, struggling and shouting. The bridge was cleared again, except for three figures that lay still upon the road, and one who leaned panting against the wall of the bridge, his armor dimmed and stained.

The Baron Henry raged like a crazy man. He ground his teeth together and rode back a little way. Then he turned, lowered his long weapon, and suddenly kicked his horse with his spurs. The next instant he came rushing down upon his lone enemy.

Baron Conrad swung his sword in the air as he saw the other coming at him like a flash of lightning. He jumped aside, and the long weapon passed close to him. As it passed he struck, and the iron point flew off the shaft at the blow and fell ringing upon the stone road of the bridge.

Baron Henry pulled in his horse until it sat back on its rear legs. Then he slowly backed it down the bridge, still facing his enemy, and still holding the wooden stump of the weapon in his hand. At the end of the bridge he threw it from him.

“Another one!” he cried, in a rough, low voice. One was silently handed to him and he took it, his hand shaking with rage. Again he rode to a little distance and turned his horse. Then he drove his steel spurs into its shaking side and came rushing down upon the other again. Once more the terrible sword swung in the air and fell, but this time the weapon was pulled to one side and the blow fell without harm. The next instant, with a pull of the reins, the horse struck full and fair against the man.

Conrad of Drachenhausen was thrown backward and downward, and the cruel iron feet crushed over his fallen body as horse and man rushed past him to the far end of the bridge. A shout went up from those who stood watching. The next moment the fallen figure rose and staggered without sight to the side of the bridge, and stood leaning against the stone wall.

At the further end of the bridge Baron Henry had turned his horse. Once again he lowered his weapon, and again he drove down upon his bruised and wounded enemy. This time the weapon struck full and fair, and those who watched saw the steel point go through the iron chest-plate and then break off short, leaving the sharp point inside the wound.

Baron Conrad sank to his knees, and the Roderburg, rising on his horse above him, drew his sword to finish the work he had begun.

Then those who stood looking on saw a wonderful thing happen. The wounded man rose suddenly to his feet, and before his enemy could strike he jumped, with a great and bitter cry of pain and despair, upon him as he sat in the saddle above.

Henry of Trutz-Drachen grasped at his horse’s neck hair, but the attack was so fierce, so sudden, and so unexpected that before he could save himself he was dragged to one side and fell crashing in his armor upon the stone road of the bridge.

“The dragon! the dragon!” roared Baron Conrad, in a voice like thunder, and with the strength of despair he dragged his fallen foe toward the open side of the bridge.

“Forward!” cried the chief of the Trutz-Drachen men, and down they rode upon the struggling knights to save their master in this new danger. But they were too late.

There was a pause at the edge of the bridge, for Baron Henry had got to his feet. Stunned and confused as he was by the suddenness of his fall, he was now struggling fiercely and desperately. For a moment they stood swaying backward and forward, held in each other’s arms, the blood from the wounded man’s chest staining the armor of both. The moment passed, and then, with a shower of stones and cement from beneath their iron-shod heels, they fell over and dropped. There was a loud splash in the water below. The soldiers came hurrying up and looked with fearful faces over the low wall of the bridge. They saw the turning circles of water sweep down with the current of the stream, a few bubbles rise to the surface, and then—nothing. For the smooth river flowed on as silently as ever.

Presently a loud voice broke through the fearful hush that followed. It came from William of Roderburg, Baron Henry’s relative.

“Forward!” he cried. A murmur of voices from the others was all the answer that he received. “Forward!” cried the young man again. “The boy and those with him are not so far away that we might still catch up with them.”

Then one of the men spoke up in answer—a man with a lined, weather-beaten face and short gray hair. “No,” said he. “Our Lord Baron is gone, and this is no quarrel of ours. Here are four of us that are wounded and three I fear that are dead. Why should we follow further only to suffer more blows for no gain?” A low sound of agreement rose from those that stood around, and William of Roderburg saw that nothing more was to be done by the Trutz-Dragons that day.

XIV. How Otto Saw the Great Emperor.

Through weakness and sickness and faintness, Otto had lain half unconscious through all that long journey under the hot May sun. It was like a dreadful nightmare. He had heard on and on and on that steady beating of running horse feet upon the ground. He had felt that last kiss that his father had given him upon his cheek. Then the ride went on again, until all faded away into a dull mist and he knew no more. When next he woke it was with the sharp smell of burned vinegar in his nose and with the feeling of a cool cloth bathing his brow. He opened his eyes and then closed them again, thinking he must have been in a dream. For he lay in his old room at the peaceful monastery of the White Cross on the hill. The good Father Abbot sat near by, looking at his face with the old absent student look. Brother John sat in the deep window seat also looking at him, and Brother Theodore, the doctor of the monastery, sat beside him bathing his head. Beside these old familiar faces were the faces of those who had been with him in that long flight: the One-eyed Hans, old Master Nicholas his relative, and the others. So he closed his eyes, thinking that maybe it was all a dream. But the sharp beating pain of the poor stump at his wrist soon taught him that he was still awake.

“Am I then really home in St. Michaelsburg again?” he murmured, without opening his eyes.

Brother Theodore began making a loud sound through his nose. There was a pause. "Yes," said the old Abbot at last. His gentle voice shook as he spoke. "Yes, my dear little child, you are back again in your own home. You have not been long out in the great world. But truly you have had a hard and bitter test of it."

"But they will not take me away again, will they?" said Otto quickly. He opened his blue eyes.

"No," said the Abbot, gently. "Not until you are healed in body. And not until you are ready and willing to go."

Three months and more had passed, and Otto was well again. Now he was riding into the old and strange town of Nurnburg. One-eyed Hans went with him. So did those faithful few who had stayed with the Baron Conrad through his last few bitter days. The Emperor Rudolph was there at that time. He was waiting for King Ottocar of Bohemia to come there. The King was to answer the imperial summons before the Council. Otto was travelling to the court.

As they rode in through the gates of the town, Otto looked up at the high-peaked houses. Their gables hung over the street, and he had never seen the like of them before. He stared with his round blue eyes at seeing them so crowded together along the length of the street. But most of all he wondered at the number of people who passed here and there. They pushed each other in their hurry. He also wondered at the tradesmen's booths opening upon the street, with the wonderful goods hanging within. There was armor at the smiths, shining ornaments at the goldsmiths, and rich fabrics of silks and satins at the mercers. He had never seen anything so rich and grand in all of his life. Little Otto had never been in a town before.

"Oh! look," he cried, "at that wonderful lady. See, holy father! Sure the Emperor's wife can be no finer than that lady."

The Abbot smiled. "No, Otto," said he. "That is but a townswoman's wife or daughter. The ladies at the Emperor's court are far grander than such as she."

"So!" said Otto, and then fell silent with wonder.

And now, at last the great moment had come. Little Otto with his own eyes was to see the mighty Emperor. This Emperor ruled over all the powerful kingdoms of Germany and Austria, and Italy and Bohemia, and other kingdoms and states and lands. His heart beat so that he could hardly speak. For a moment, the good Abbot who held him by the hand stopped outside of the doorway with its woven wall hangings. He whispered some last instructions into his ear. Then they entered the apartment.

It was a long, stone-paved room. The floor was covered with rich rugs. The walls were hung with woven cloth pictures. In them were shown knights and ladies in gardens full of leaves, and kings and warriors at battle. A long row of high windows with glass extended along the length of the apartment. They flooded it with the soft light of the autumn day. At the further end of the room, far away, stood a group of nobles. They stood by a great carved fireplace where the remains of a fire burned slowly. They wore gorgeous dress of velvet and silks, and shining golden chains hung about their necks.

One figure stood alone in front of the great open fireplace. His hands were held together behind him. His look was bent in thought upon the floor. He was dressed only in a simple gray robe without ornament or decoration. A plain leather belt was tied around his waist. From it hung a sword with a bone handle encased in a brown leather cover. A noble stag-hound lay close behind him. It was curled up upon the floor, enjoying the grateful warmth of the fire.

As the Father Abbot and Otto drew near, he raised his head and looked at them. It was a plain, homely face that Otto saw. It had a wrinkled forehead and a long mouth drawn down at the corners. It was the face of a good, honest townsman burdened with the cares of a prosperous trade. "Who can he be," thought Otto, "and why does the poor man stand there among all the great nobles?"

But the Abbot walked straight up to him and kneeled upon the floor. Little Otto, full of wonder, did the same. It was the great Emperor Rudolph.

"Who have we here," said the Emperor. He bent his brow upon the Abbot and the boy.

"Sire," said Abbot Otto, "we have humbly asked you by petition, in the name of your late servant, Baron Conrad of Vuelph of Drachenhausen. We ask for justice to this his son, the Baron Otto. As you may see, sire, he has been cruelly wounded at the hands of Baron Henry of Roderburg of Trutz-Drachen. He has moreover been robbed of his lands, his castle burnt, and his household made prisoner."

The Emperor frowned until the shaggy eyebrows nearly hid the keen gray twinkle of the eyes beneath. "Yes," said he, "I do remember me of that petition. I have given it consideration both in private and in council." He turned to the group of listening nobles. "Look," said he, "at this little child marred by the inhumanity and the cruelty of those robber villains. By heavens! I will put down their lawless violence, if I have to give every castle from the north to the south to the flames and to the sword." Then he turned to Otto again. "Poor little child," said he, "your wrongs shall be righted. So far as they are able, those cruel Roderburgs shall pay you coin for coin, and grain for grain, for what you have lost. Until such payment has been paid, the family of the man who did this deed shall be held as security."

Little Otto looked up in the kind, rugged face above him. "No, Lord Emperor," said he, in his strange, quiet way. "There are but two in the family--the mother and the daughter. I have promised to marry the little girl when she and I are old enough. So, if you please, I would not have harm happen to her."

The Emperor continued to look down at the kneeling boy. At last he gave a short, dry laugh. "So be it," said he. "Your plan is not without its wisdom. Perhaps it is all for the best that the affair should be ended thus peacefully. The estates of the Roderburgs shall be held in trust for you until you are of age. Otherwise it shall be as you have proposed. The little maiden shall be taken into care under our own protection. And as to you--are you willing that I should take you under my own charge in the place of your father, who is dead?"

"Aye," said Otto, simply, "I am willing, for it seems to me that you are a good man."

The nobles who stood near smiled at the boy's speech. As for the Emperor, he laughed loudly. "I give you thanks, my Lord Baron," said he. "There is no one in all my court who has paid me greater courtesy than that."

So comes the end of our tale.

But perhaps you may like to know what happened afterward. No one likes to leave a story without tying a knot in it.

Eight years had passed. Otto grew up to become a man in the Emperor's court. He was with him through war and peace.

But he himself never drew a sword or struck a blow. The right hand that hung at his side was of pure silver. The hard, cold fingers never closed. People called him "Otto of the Silver Hand." But perhaps there was another reason for that name. The pure, simple wisdom that the old monks of the White Cross on the hill had taught him stayed with him through all the honors that the Emperor gave to his favorite. As he grew older, his words were listened to and weighed by those high in Council, and even by the Emperor himself.

And now for the end of all.

One day Otto stood nervously at the doorway of a room in the imperial castle. He hesitated before he entered. And yet there was nothing so very terrible inside. There was only one poor girl whose heart beat faster than his. Poor little Pauline, whom he had not seen since that last day in the black cell at Trutz-Drachen.

At last he pushed aside the curtains and entered the room.

She was sitting on a rough bench beside the window. She looked at him with her great, dark eyes.

He stopped short and stood for a moment confused and silent. He had no thought in his mind but of the little girl he had last seen. For a moment he stood confused before the fair young woman with her great, beautiful dark eyes.

She, on her part, saw a tall, slender young man with curling, golden hair. One hand was white and delicate. The other was of pure and shining silver.

He came to her and took her hand and set it to his lips. All she could do was gaze with her great, dark eyes upon the hero of whom she had heard so many talk. The favorite of the Emperor. The wise young Otto of the Silver Hand.

Afterword

The ruins of Drachenhausen were rebuilt. The walls were as sound as ever, though empty and open to the sky. But it was no longer the den of a robber baron. Beneath the shield over the great gate was carved a new motto of the Vuelphs. It was a motto that the Emperor Rudolph himself had given:

"Manus argentea quam manus ferrea melior est."
