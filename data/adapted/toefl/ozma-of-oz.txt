Author's Note

My friends the children are responsible for this new "Oz Book." They were also responsible for the last one, which was called The Land of Oz. Their sweet little letters ask to know "more about Dorothy." And they ask: "What became of the Cowardly Lion?" and "What did Ozma do afterward?" They mean, of course, after she became the Ruler of Oz. And some of them suggest plots to me. They say: "Please have Dorothy go to the Land of Oz again." Or, "Why not make Ozma and Dorothy meet, and have a good time together?" Indeed, if I could do all that my little friends ask, I would have to write dozens of books to satisfy their demands. And I wish I could, for I enjoy writing these stories just as much as the children say they enjoy reading them.

Well, here is "more about Dorothy," and about our old friends the Scarecrow and the Tin Woodman, and about the Cowardly Lion, and Ozma, and all the rest of them. And here, also, is a good deal about some new folks that are strange and unusual. One little friend read this story before it was printed. She said to me: "Billina is REAL OZZY, Mr. Baum, and so are Tiktok and the Hungry Tiger."

If this judgment is fair and correct, and the little folks find this new story "real Ozzy," I shall be very glad indeed that I wrote it. But perhaps I shall get some more of those very welcome letters from my readers. They will tell me just how they like "Ozma of Oz." I hope so, anyway.

L. FRANK BAUM.

MACATAWA, 1907.

1. The Girl in the Chicken Coop

The wind blew hard and shook the water of the ocean. It sent small waves across its surface. Then the wind pushed the edges of the small waves until they became waves. And it pushed the waves around until they became huge rolling waves. The huge waves rolled terribly high: higher even than the tops of houses. Some of them, indeed, rolled as high as the tops of tall trees, and seemed like mountains. And the deep gaps between the great waves were like deep valleys.

All this wild rushing and splashing of the waters of the big ocean, which the bad wind caused without any good reason at all, resulted in a terrible storm. And a storm on the ocean is likely to play many strange tricks and do a lot of damage.

At the time the wind began to blow, a ship was sailing far out upon the waters. When the waves began to tumble and toss and to grow bigger and bigger, the ship rolled up and down. It tipped to one side--first one way and then the other. And it was pushed around so roughly that even the sailors had to hold fast to the ropes and railings. They did this to keep themselves from being swept away by the wind or thrown face-first into the sea.

And the clouds were so thick in the sky that the sunlight could not get through them. So the day grew dark as night, which added to the terrors of the storm.

The Captain of the ship was not afraid, because he had seen storms before. He had sailed his ship through them in safety. But he knew that his passengers would be in danger if they tried to stay on deck. So he put them all into the cabin and told them to stay there until after the storm was over. He told them to keep brave hearts and not be scared, and all would be well with them.

Now, among these passengers was a little Kansas girl named Dorothy Gale. She was going with her Uncle Henry to Australia, to visit some relatives they had never before seen. Uncle Henry, you must know, was not very well. He had been working so hard on his Kansas farm that his health had given way and left him weak and nervous. So he left Aunt Em at home to watch after the hired men and to take care of the farm. Meanwhile he traveled far away to Australia to visit his cousins and have a good rest.

Dorothy was eager to go with him on this journey. Uncle Henry thought she would be good company and help cheer him up. So he decided to take her along. The little girl was quite an experienced traveler, for she had once been carried by a huge storm as far away from home as the marvelous Land of Oz. And she had met with a good many adventures in that strange country before she managed to get back to Kansas again. So she was not easily frightened, whatever happened. And when the wind began to make a long, loud sound and whistle, and the waves began to tumble and toss, our little girl did not mind the loud noise the least bit.

"Of course we will have to stay in the cabin," she said to Uncle Henry and the other passengers. "And keep as quiet as possible until the storm is over. For the Captain says if we go on deck we may be blown off the ship."

No one wanted to risk such an accident as that, you may be sure. So all the passengers stayed crowded together in the dark cabin. They listened to the loud crying of the storm and the sharp sounds of the masts and ropes. And they tried to keep from bumping into one another when the ship tipped to one side.

Dorothy had almost fallen asleep when she was aroused with a start. She found that Uncle Henry was missing. She could not imagine where he had gone. And as he was not very strong she began to worry about him. She began to fear he might have been thoughtless enough to go on deck. In that case he would be in great danger unless he instantly came down again.

The fact was that Uncle Henry had gone to lie down in his little sleeping bed. But Dorothy did not know that. She only remembered that Aunt Em had told her to take good care of her uncle. So at once she decided to go on deck and find him. This was even though the storm was now worse than ever. The ship was moving up and down in a really terrible way. Indeed, the little girl found it was very hard just to climb the stairs to the deck. As soon as she got there the wind hit her so fiercely that it almost tore away the skirts of her dress. Yet Dorothy felt a kind of happy excitement in fighting the storm. She held tight to the railing and looked around through the dark. She thought she saw the dim shape of a man holding on to a tall pole not far away from her. This might be her uncle, so she called as loudly as she could:

"Uncle Henry! Uncle Henry!"

But the wind screamed and cried so wildly that she hardly heard her own voice. The man certainly failed to hear her, for he did not move.

Dorothy decided she must go to him. So she ran forward during a quiet moment in the storm. She ran to where a big square box for chickens had been tied to the deck with ropes. She reached this place in safety. But as soon as she seized tight hold of the wooden bars of the big box in which the chickens were kept, the wind suddenly doubled its fury. It was as if it was angry because the little girl dared to resist its power. With a scream like that of an angry giant it tore away the ropes that held the box. It lifted the box high into the air, with Dorothy still holding on to the bars. Around and over it spun, this way and that. A few moments later the chicken box dropped far away into the sea. There the big waves caught it and slid it up-hill to a foaming top and then down-hill into a deep valley. It was as if it were nothing more than a toy to keep them happy.

Dorothy had a good ducking, you may be sure. But she did not lose her presence of mind even for a second. She kept tight hold of the strong bars. As soon as she could get the water out of her eyes she saw that the wind had ripped the cover from the box. The poor chickens were flapping away in every direction. The wind blew them until they looked like feather dusters without handles. The bottom of the box was made of thick boards. So Dorothy found she was holding on to a sort of raft, with sides of bars, which easily held up her weight. She coughed the water out of her throat and got her breath again. Then she managed to climb over the bars and stand upon the firm wooden bottom of the box, which supported her easily enough.

"Why, I've got a ship of my own!" she thought. She was more amused than frightened at her sudden change of condition. Then the box climbed up to the top of a big wave. She looked eagerly around for the ship from which she had been blown.

It was far, far away by this time. Perhaps no one on board had yet missed her, or knew of her strange adventure. Down into a valley between the waves the box swept her. When she climbed another top the ship looked like a toy boat, it was such a long way off. Soon it had entirely disappeared in the dark. Then Dorothy gave a sigh of regret at parting with Uncle Henry. She began to wonder what was going to happen to her next.

Just now she was tossing on the open water of a big ocean. There was nothing to keep her floating but a miserable wooden hen box. It had a board bottom and barred sides. Through the bars the water constantly splashed and wetted her through to the skin! And there was nothing to eat when she became hungry--as she was sure to do before long. There was no fresh water to drink and no dry clothes to put on.

"Well, I declare!" she exclaimed, with a laugh. "You are in a pretty fix, Dorothy Gale, I can tell you! And I have not the least idea how you are going to get out of it!"

As if to add to her troubles the night was now creeping on. The gray clouds overhead changed to very black darkness. But the wind, as if satisfied at last with its bad tricks, stopped blowing this ocean. It hurried away to another part of the world to blow something else. So the waves, not being shaken any more, began to quiet down and behave themselves.

It was lucky for Dorothy, I think, that the storm died down. Otherwise, brave though she was, I fear she might have died. Many children, in her place, would have wept and given way to despair. But Dorothy had met so many adventures and come safely through them. So it did not occur to her at this time to be especially afraid. She was wet and uncomfortable, it is true. But, after sighing that one sigh I told you of, she managed to recall some of her usual cheerfulness. She decided to patiently wait for whatever her fate might be.

By and by the black clouds rolled away. They showed a blue sky overhead, with a silver moon shining sweetly in the middle of it. Little stars winked merrily at Dorothy when she looked their way. The box did not toss around any more. It rode the waves more gently--almost like a cradle rocking. So the floor upon which Dorothy stood was no longer swept by water coming through the bars. Seeing this, and being quite exhausted by the excitement of the past few hours, the little girl decided that sleep would be the best thing to restore her strength. It was also the easiest way in which she could pass the time. The floor was damp and she was herself wringing wet. But fortunately this was a warm climate and she did not feel at all cold.

So she sat down in a corner of the box. She leaned her back against the bars. She nodded at the friendly stars before she closed her eyes, and was asleep in half a minute.

2. The Yellow Hen

A strange noise awoke Dorothy. She opened her eyes to find that day had dawned and the sun was shining brightly in a clear sky. She had been dreaming that she was back in Kansas again. She was playing in the old farm yard with the calves and pigs and chickens all around her. At first, as she rubbed the sleep from her eyes, she really imagined she was there.

"Kut-kut-kut, ka-daw-kut! Kut-kut-kut, ka-daw-kut!"

Ah! Here was the strange noise that had woken her again. Surely it was a hen making a loud sound! But her wide-open eyes first saw the blue waves of the ocean through the narrow openings of the small wooden box. The sea was now calm and peaceful. Her thoughts flew back to the past night, so full of danger and trouble. She also began to remember that she was a lost child of the storm. She was floating on a dangerous and unknown sea.

"Kut-kut-kut, ka-daw-w-w--kut!"

"What is that?" cried Dorothy, jumping to her feet.

"Why, I have just laid an egg, that is all," replied a small but sharp and clear voice. Looking around her, the little girl found a yellow hen sitting in the opposite corner of the box.

"Dear me!" she exclaimed in surprise. "Have you been here all night, too?"

"Of course," answered the hen. She moved her wings and opened her mouth wide in a tired way. "When the box blew away from the ship, I held on tight to this corner with my claws and mouth. I knew if I fell into the water I would surely drown. Indeed, I nearly drowned as it was, with all that water washing over me. I was never so wet before in my life!"

"Yes," agreed Dorothy, "it was quite wet for a time, I know. But do you feel comfortable now?"

"Not very. The sun has helped to dry my feathers, as it has your dress. I feel better since I laid my morning egg. But what will become of us, I would like to know, floating on this big pond?"

"I would like to know that, too," said Dorothy. "But tell me, how does it happen that you are able to talk? I thought hens could only make their usual sounds."

"Why, as for that," answered the yellow hen, thinking carefully, "I have made hen sounds all my life. I never spoke a word before this morning, as far as I can remember. But when you asked a question a minute ago, it seemed the most natural thing in the world to answer you. So I spoke, and I seem to keep on speaking, just as you and other human beings do. Strange, is it not?"

"Very," replied Dorothy. "If we were in the Land of Oz, I would not think it so strange, because many of the animals can talk in that fairy country. But out here in the ocean must be a good long way from Oz."

"How is my grammar?" asked the yellow hen, worriedly. "Do I speak quite properly, in your judgment?"

"Yes," said Dorothy, "you do very well, for a beginner."

"I am glad to know that," continued the yellow hen, in a quiet, trusting tone. "Because, if one is going to talk, it is best to talk correctly. The red rooster has often said that my sounds were quite perfect. And now it is a comfort to know I am talking properly."

"I am beginning to get hungry," remarked Dorothy. "It is breakfast time, but there is no breakfast."

"You may have my egg," said the yellow hen. "I do not care for it, you know."

"Do you not want to sit on it until it hatches?" asked the little girl in surprise.

"No, indeed. I never care to hatch eggs unless I have a nice warm nest in some quiet place, with thirteen eggs under me. That is thirteen, you know, and it is a lucky number for hens. So you may as well eat this egg."

"Oh, I could not possibly eat it unless it was cooked," exclaimed Dorothy. "But I am much obliged for your kindness, just the same."

"Do not mention it, my dear," answered the hen calmly, and began cleaning her feathers.

For a moment Dorothy stood looking out over the wide sea. She was still thinking of the egg, though. So presently she asked:

"Why do you lay eggs when you do not expect to hatch them?"

"It is a habit I have," replied the yellow hen. "It has always been my pride to lay a fresh egg every morning, except when I am losing my old feathers. I never feel like making my morning sound until the egg is properly laid. And without the chance to make that sound, I would not be happy."

"It is strange," said the girl, thinking deeply. "But as I am not a hen, I cannot be expected to understand that."

"Certainly not, my dear."

Then Dorothy fell silent again. The yellow hen was some company, and a bit of comfort, too. But it was terribly lonely out on the big ocean, nevertheless.

After a time the hen flew up and sat upon the topmost piece of wood of the box. This was a little above Dorothy's head when she was sitting upon the bottom, as she had been doing for some moments past.

"Why, we are not far from land!" exclaimed the hen.

"Where? Where is it?" cried Dorothy, jumping up in great excitement.

"Over there a little way," answered the hen, nodding her head in a certain direction. "We seem to be moving toward it, so that before noon we ought to find ourselves upon dry land again."

"I shall like that!" said Dorothy with a little sigh. Her feet and legs were still wetted now and then by the sea-water that came through the open spaces.

"So shall I," answered her companion. "There is nothing in the world so miserable as a wet hen."

The land, which they seemed to be quickly approaching, since it grew more clear every minute, was quite beautiful as viewed by the little girl in the floating hen-box. Next to the water was a broad beach of white sand and small stones. Farther back were several rocky hills. Beyond these appeared a strip of green trees that marked the edge of a forest. But there were no houses to be seen, nor any sign of people who might live in this unknown land.

"I hope we shall find something to eat," said Dorothy, looking eagerly at the pretty beach toward which they moved. "It is long past breakfast time now."

"I am a little hungry myself," declared the yellow hen.

"Why do you not eat the egg?" asked the child. "You do not need to have your food cooked, as I do."

"Do you take me for a person who eats her own kind?" cried the hen, angrily. "I do not know what I have said or done that leads you to insult me!"

"I beg your pardon, I am sure, Mrs.--Mrs.--by the way, may I ask your name, ma'am?" asked the little girl.

"My name is Bill," said the yellow hen, somewhat roughly.

"Bill! Why, that is a boy's name."

"What difference does that make?"

"You are a lady hen, are you not?"

"Of course. But when I was first hatched out, no one could tell whether I was going to be a hen or a rooster. So the little boy at the farm where I was born called me Bill. He made a pet of me because I was the only yellow chicken in the whole brood. When I grew up, and he found that I did not crow and fight, as all the roosters do, he did not think to change my name. Every creature in the barn-yard, as well as the people in the house, knew me as 'Bill.' So Bill I have always been called, and Bill is my name."

"But it is all wrong, you know," said Dorothy, seriously. "And, if you do not mind, I shall call you 'Billina.' Putting the 'eena' on the end makes it a girl's name, you see."

"Oh, I do not mind it at all," answered the yellow hen. "It does not matter at all what you call me, as long as I know the name means ME."

"Very well, Billina. MY name is Dorothy Gale--just Dorothy to my friends and Miss Gale to strangers. You may call me Dorothy, if you like. We are getting very near the shore. Do you think it is too deep for me to walk the rest of the way?"

"Wait a few minutes longer. The sunshine is warm and pleasant, and we are in no hurry."

"But my feet are all wet and soft," said the girl. "My dress is dry enough, but I will not feel real comfortable till I get my feet dried."

She waited, however, as the hen advised. Before long the big wooden box rubbed gently on the sandy beach and the dangerous voyage was over.

It did not take the lost travelers long to reach the shore, you may be sure. The yellow hen flew to the sands at once, but Dorothy had to climb over the high boards. Still, for a country girl, that was not much of a task. As soon as she was safe on land Dorothy took off her wet shoes and stockings and spread them upon the sun-warmed beach to dry.

Then she sat down and watched Billina. The hen was picking away with her sharp bill in the sand and small stones. She scratched them up and turned them over with her strong claws.

"What are you doing?" asked Dorothy.

"Getting my breakfast, of course," said the hen quietly, picking away.

"What do you find?" asked the girl, with interest.

"Oh, some fat red ants, and some sand-bugs, and once in a while a tiny crab. They are very sweet and nice, I tell you."

"How terrible!" cried Dorothy, in a shocked voice.

"What is terrible?" asked the hen. She lifted her head to look with one bright eye at her companion.

"Why, eating live things, and bad bugs, and crawling ants. You ought to be ASHAMED of yourself!"

"Goodness me!" answered the hen, in a puzzled tone. "How strange you are, Dorothy! Live things are much fresher and more healthy than dead ones. And you humans eat all sorts of dead creatures."

"We do not!" said Dorothy.

"You do, indeed," answered Billina. "You eat lambs and sheep and cows and pigs and even chickens."

"But we cook them," said Dorothy, with pride.

"What difference does that make?"

"A good deal," said the girl, in a more serious tone. "I cannot just explain the difference, but it is there. And, anyway, we never eat such terrible things as BUGS."

"But you eat the chickens that eat the bugs," answered the yellow hen, with an odd laugh. "So you are just as bad as we chickens are."

This made Dorothy thoughtful. What Billina said was true enough, and it almost took away her wish for breakfast. As for the yellow hen, she continued to pick away at the sand, and seemed quite happy with her food.

Finally, down near the water's edge, Billina stuck her bill deep into the sand. Then she drew back and shook.

"Ow!" she cried. "I struck metal that time, and it nearly broke my beak."

"It was probably a rock," said Dorothy, without care.

"Nonsense. I know a rock from metal, I think," said the hen. "There is a different feel to it."

"But there could not be any metal on this wild, empty seashore," said the girl. "Where is the place? I will dig it up, and prove to you I am right."

Billina showed her the place where she had hit her bill, as she said it. Dorothy dug away the sand until she felt something hard. Then she put in her hand and pulled the thing out. She found it was a large golden key--rather old, but still bright and of perfect shape.

"What did I tell you?" cried the hen, with a laugh of victory. "Can I tell metal when I hit it, or is the thing a rock?"

"It is metal, sure enough," answered the child. She looked with thought at the strange thing she had found. "I think it is pure gold, and it must have lain hidden in the sand for a long time. How do you think it came there, Billina? And what do you think this mysterious key opens?"

"I cannot say," replied the hen. "You ought to know more about locks and keys than I do."

Dorothy looked around. There was no sign of any house in that part of the country. She reasoned that every key must fit a lock and every lock must have a purpose. Perhaps the key had been lost by somebody who lived far away, but had wandered on this very shore.

Thinking about these things the girl put the key in the pocket of her dress. Then she slowly put on her shoes and stockings, which the sun had fully dried.

"I believe, Billina," she said, "I will have a look around, and see if I can find some breakfast."

3. Letters in the Sand

Walking a little way back from the water's edge, toward the group of trees, Dorothy came to a flat stretch of white sand. It seemed to have strange signs marked upon its surface, just as one would write upon sand with a stick.

"What does it say?" she asked the yellow hen, who walked along beside her in a rather proud way.

"How should I know?" answered the hen. "I cannot read."

"Oh! Can you not?"

"Certainly not; I have never been to school, you know."

"Well, I have," said Dorothy; "but the letters are big and far apart, and it is hard to spell out the words."

But she looked at each letter carefully, and finally found that these words were written in the sand:

"BEWARE THE WHEELERS!"

"That is rather strange," said the hen, when Dorothy had read aloud the words. "What do you think the Wheelers are?"

"People that wheel, I think. They must have wheelbarrows, or baby-carriages or hand-carts," said Dorothy.

"Perhaps they are automobiles," suggested the yellow hen. "There is no need to beware of baby-carriages and wheelbarrows; but automobiles are dangerous things. Several of my friends have been run over by them."

"It cannot be automobiles," answered the girl, "for this is a new, wild country, without even trolley-cars or telephones. The people here have not been discovered yet, I am sure; that is, if there ARE any people. So I do not believe there CAN be any automobiles, Billina."

"Perhaps not," said the yellow hen. "Where are you going now?"

"Over to those trees, to see if I can find some fruit or nuts," answered Dorothy.

She walked across the sand, going around the foot of one of the little rocky hills that stood near, and soon reached the edge of the forest.

At first she was very sad. The nearer trees were all punita, or cotton-wood or eucalyptus. They had no fruit or nuts at all. But after a while, when she was almost without hope, the little girl came upon two trees. They seemed ready to give her plenty of food.

One tree was quite full of square paper boxes. They grew in groups on all the branches. On the biggest and ripest boxes the word "Lunch" could be read in neat raised letters. This tree seemed to bear all the year around. There were lunch-box flowers on some of the branches. On others there were tiny little lunch-boxes that were still quite green. They were clearly not fit to eat until they had grown bigger.

The leaves of this tree were all paper napkins. It looked very pleasing to the hungry little girl.

But the tree next to the lunch-box tree was even more wonderful. It bore large numbers of tin dinner-pails. They were so full and heavy that the thick branches bent under their weight. Some were small and dark-brown in color. The larger ones were a dull tin color. But the really ripe ones were pails of bright tin. They shone and sparkled beautifully in the rays of sunshine that touched them.

Dorothy was delighted. Even the yellow hen admitted that she was surprised.

The little girl stood on tip-toe and picked one of the nicest and biggest lunch-boxes. Then she sat down on the ground and eagerly opened it. Inside she found a ham sandwich, a piece of sponge-cake, a pickle, a slice of new cheese and an apple, all nicely wrapped in white papers. Each thing had a separate stem. So she had to pick them off the side of the box. But Dorothy found them all delicious. She ate every bit of the lunch in the box before she finished.

"A lunch is not exactly breakfast," she said to Billina, who sat beside her and watched with interest. "But when one is hungry one can eat even supper in the morning, and not complain."

"I hope your lunch-box was perfectly ripe," said the yellow hen in a worried tone. "So much sickness is caused by eating green things."

"Oh, I'm sure it was ripe," said Dorothy. "All of it, that is, except the pickle. And a pickle just has to be green, Billina. But everything tasted perfectly splendid. I would rather have it than a church picnic. And now I think I'll pick a dinner-pail to have when I get hungry again. Then we'll start out and explore the country, and see where we are."

"Don't you have any idea what country this is?" asked Billina.

"None at all. But listen: I'm quite sure it's a fairy country. Otherwise things like lunch-boxes and dinner-pails would not be growing on trees. Besides, Billina, you are a hen. You would not be able to talk in any civilized country, like Kansas, where no fairies live at all."

"Perhaps we're in the Land of Oz," said the hen, thinking carefully.

"No, that can't be," answered the little girl. "Because I've been to the Land of Oz, and it's all surrounded by a terrible desert that no one can cross."

"Then how did you get away from there again?" asked Billina.

"I had a pair of silver shoes that carried me through the air. But I lost them," said Dorothy.

"Ah, indeed," remarked the yellow hen in a tone of doubt.

"Anyhow," the girl went on, "there is no seashore near the Land of Oz. So this must surely be some other fairy country."

While she was speaking she chose a bright and pretty dinner-pail that seemed to have a thick handle. She picked it from its branch. Then, with the yellow hen beside her, she walked out of the shadow of the trees toward the sea-shore.

They were part way across the sands when Billina suddenly cried in a voice of terror:

"What's that?"

Dorothy turned around quickly. She saw coming out of a path that led from between the trees the most peculiar person her eyes had ever seen.

It had the form of a man, except that it walked, or rather rolled, on all fours. Its legs were the same length as its arms. This gave them the appearance of the four legs of a beast. Yet it was no beast that Dorothy had discovered. The person was dressed most beautifully in embroidered garments of many colors. It wore a straw hat set at a cheerful angle on the side of its head. But it differed from human beings in this way: instead of hands and feet, round wheels grew at the end of its arms and legs. By means of these wheels it rolled very swiftly over the level ground. Later Dorothy found that these odd wheels were made of the same hard substance as our finger-nails and toe-nails. She also learned that creatures of this strange race were born in this unusual way. But when our little girl first caught sight of the first individual of a race that was going to cause her a lot of trouble, she thought the brightly dressed person was on roller-skates. She thought the skates were attached to his hands as well as to his feet.

"Run!" screamed the yellow hen, flapping away in great fear. "It's a Wheeler!"

"A Wheeler?" exclaimed Dorothy. "What can that be?"

"Don't you remember the warning in the sand: 'Beware the Wheelers'? Run, I tell you--run!"

So Dorothy ran. The Wheeler gave a sharp, wild cry and came after her in full chase.

Looking over her shoulder as she ran, the girl now saw a great procession of Wheelers coming out of the forest. There were dozens and dozens of them. They were all dressed in splendid, tight-fitting garments. They were all rolling swiftly toward her and making their wild, strange cries.

"They're sure to catch us!" panted the girl, who was still carrying the heavy dinner-pail she had picked. "I can't run much farther, Billina."

"Climb up this hill,--quick!" said the hen. Dorothy found she was very near to the heap of loose and jagged rocks they had passed on their way to the forest. The yellow hen was already flapping among the rocks. Dorothy followed as best she could, half climbing and half tumbling up the rough and uneven steep.

She was none too soon. The foremost Wheeler reached the hill a moment after her. But while the girl climbed up the rocks, the creature stopped short with howls of rage and disappointment.

Dorothy now heard the yellow hen laughing in her cackling, henny way.

"Don't hurry, my dear," cried Billina. "They can't follow us among these rocks, so we're safe enough now."

Dorothy stopped at once and sat down on a broad boulder, for she was all out of breath.

The rest of the Wheelers had now reached the foot of the hill. But it was clear that their wheels would not roll on the rough and sharp rocks. So they could not follow Dorothy and the hen to the place where they had hidden. But they moved all around the little hill. So the child and Billina were trapped and could not come down without being caught.

Then the creatures shook their front wheels at Dorothy in a threatening way. It seemed they could speak as well as make their terrible cries. Several of them shouted:

"We will get you in time, do not fear! And when we do get you, we will tear you into little bits!"

"Why are you so cruel to me?" asked Dorothy. "I am a stranger in your country, and I have done you no harm."

"No harm!" cried one who seemed to be their leader. "Did you not pick our lunch-boxes and dinner-pails? Do you not have a stolen dinner-pail still in your hand?"

"I only picked one of each," she answered. "I was hungry, and I did not know the trees were yours."

"That is no excuse," answered the leader, who was dressed in a very fine suit. "It is the law here that whoever picks a dinner-pail without our permission must die at once."

"Stay where we are," advised the yellow hen. "We are safe from the Wheelers until we starve to death, in any case. And before that time comes, a good many things can happen."

4. Tiktok the Machine Man

After an hour or so, most of the band of Wheelers rolled back into the forest. Only three of them stayed to guard the hill. These curled themselves up like big dogs and pretended to go to sleep on the sands. But neither Dorothy nor Billina were fooled by this trick. So they remained safe among the rocks and paid no attention to their clever enemies.

Finally the hen, flying over the mound, exclaimed: "Why, here is a path!"

But when she followed it to the foot of the mound, she found that several big pieces of rock had been placed directly across the end of the way. This stopped anyone outside from seeing it. It also stopped the Wheelers from using it to climb up the mound.

Then Dorothy walked back up the path. She followed it until she came to the very top of the hill. There a single round rock stood that was bigger than any of the others around it. "It looks something like a door, does it not?"

"What looks like a door?" asked the child.

"Why, that crack in the rock, just facing you," replied Billina. Her little round eyes were very sharp and seemed to see everything. "It runs up one side and down the other, and across the top and the bottom."

"What does?"

"Why, the crack. So I think it must be a door of rock, although I do not see any hinges."

"Oh, yes," said Dorothy. Now she saw the crack in the rock for the first time. "And is this not a key-hole, Billina?" She pointed to a round, deep hole at one side of the door.

"Of course. If we only had the key, now, we could unlock it and see what is there," replied the yellow hen. "Maybe it is a treasure room full of diamonds and rubies, or heaps of shining gold, or--"

"That reminds me," said Dorothy, "of the golden key I picked up on the shore. Do you think that it would fit this key-hole, Billina?"

"Try it and see," suggested the hen.

So Dorothy searched in the pocket of her dress and found the golden key. She put it into the hole of the rock and turned it. A sudden sharp snap was heard. Then, with a serious creak that made shivers run down the child's back, the face of the rock fell outward, like a door on hinges. It showed a small dark room just inside.

"Good gracious!" cried Dorothy, shrinking back as far as the narrow path would let her.

For, standing within the narrow room of rock, was the form of a man--or, at least, it seemed like a man, in the dim light. He was only about as tall as Dorothy herself. His body was round as a ball and made out of polished copper. Also his head and limbs were copper. These were joined or hinged to his body in a strange way, with metal caps over the joints, like the armor worn by knights in days of old. He stood perfectly still. Where the light struck upon his form, it shone as if made of pure gold.

"Do not be frightened," called Billina, from her perch. "It is not alive."

"I see it is not," replied the girl, drawing a long breath.

"It is only made out of copper, like the old kettle in the barn-yard at home," continued the hen. She turned her head first to one side and then to the other, so that both her little round eyes could examine the object.

"Once," said Dorothy, "I knew a man made out of tin. He was a woodman named Nick Chopper. But he was as alive as we are, because he was born a real man. 5. Dorothy Opens the Dinner Pail

"Now Tiktok," said Dorothy, "the first thing to be done is to find a way for us to escape from these rocks. The Wheelers are down below, you know, and threaten to kill us."

"There is no rea-son to be a-fraid of the Wheel-ers," said Tiktok. The words came more slowly than before.

"Dear me!" said Dorothy, in a frightened voice. "What can be the matter?"

"He has run down, I suppose," said the hen, calmly. "You could not have wound him up very tight."

"I did not know how much to wind him," replied the girl. "But I will try to do better next time."

"The key."

Dorothy looked, and the hen helped her. By and by the girl found the clock-key. It had fallen into a crack of the rock.

At once she wound up Tiktok's voice. She took care to give the key as many turns as it would go around. She found this quite a task, as you may imagine if you have ever tried to wind a clock. But the machine man's first words were to assure Dorothy that he would now run for at least twenty-four hours.

"You did not wind me much, at first," he calmly said. "And I told you that long story about King Evoldo. So it is no wonder that I ran down."

She next rewound the action clock-work. Then Billina advised her to carry the key to Tiktok in her pocket, so it would not get lost again.

"Why, they are nothing to be frightened at," said the machine. "They try to make folks believe that they are very terrible. "No," said Tiktok.

"And you will not find such a thing among these rocks, either," declared the yellow hen.

"Wind up my think-works tightly, and I will try to think of some other plan," said Tiktok.

In the cover she found a small tank that was full of very nice lemon drink. It was covered by a cup. The cup might also, when removed, be used to drink the lemon drink from. Within the box were three slices of turkey, two slices of cold tongue, some lobster salad, four slices of bread and butter, a small sweet egg pie, an orange and nine large strawberries, and some nuts and dried grapes. Strangely enough, the nuts in this dinner-box grew already cracked. So Dorothy had no trouble in picking out their meats to eat.

She spread the feast upon the rock beside her and began her dinner. First she offered some of it to Tiktok. He declined because, as he said, he was merely a machine. "Of course not," he answered. "They belong to the royal family of Ev. "The firm of Smith & Tinker, in the town of Evna, where the royal palace stands," answered Tiktok.

"Did they make many of you?" asked the child.

"No; I am the only automatic mechanical man they ever completed," he replied. "They were very wonderful creators of new machines, were my makers, and quite artistic in all they did."

"I am sure of that," said Dorothy. "Do they live in the town of Evna now?"

"They are both gone," replied the machine. "Mr. Smith was an artist, as well as a creator of machines. He painted a picture of a river which was so natural that, as he was reaching across it to paint some flowers on the opposite bank, he fell into the water and was drowned."

"The Princess will not like it," said the maid, hesitating.

"I do not care whether she likes it or not," replied Billina. She moved her wings with a loud noise and flew straight at the maid's face. The little servant ducked her head at once. The hen reached Dorothy's side in safety.

"Very well," sighed the maid. "If you are all ruined because of this stubborn hen, do not blame me for it. It is not safe to make Princess Langwidere angry."

"Tell her we are waiting, if you please," Dorothy requested, with dignity. "Billina is my friend, and must go wherever I go."

Without more words the maid led them to a drawing-room with rich furniture. It was lit with soft rainbow colors that came in through beautiful stained-glass windows.

"Remain here," she said. "What names shall I give the Princess?"

"I am Dorothy Gale, of Kansas," replied the child. "And this gentleman is a machine named Tiktok, and the yellow hen is my friend Billina."

The little servant bowed and left. She went through several passages and climbed two marble stairways before she came to the rooms of her mistress.

Princess Langwidere's sitting-room had great mirrors on its walls, which reached from the ceiling to the floor. The ceiling was also made of mirrors, and the floor was of polished silver that reflected every object on it. So when Langwidere sat in her easy chair and played soft music on her mandolin, her form was mirrored hundreds of times, in walls and ceiling and floor. Whichever way the lady turned her head she could see and admire her own features. This she loved to do. Just as the maid entered she was saying to herself:

"This head with the reddish-brown hair and light brown eyes is quite attractive. I must wear it more often than I have done lately, although it may not be the best of my collection."

"You have company, Your Highness," announced the maid, bowing low.

"Who is it?" asked Langwidere, opening her mouth wide with tiredness.

"Dorothy Gale of Kansas, Mr. Tiktok and Billina," answered the maid.

"What a strange lot of names!" murmured the Princess, beginning to be a little interested. "What are they like? Is Dorothy Gale of Kansas pretty?"

"She might be called so," the maid replied.

"And is Mr. Tiktok attractive?" continued the Princess.

"That I cannot say, Your Highness. But he seems very bright. Will Your Gracious Highness see them?"

"Oh, I may as well, Nanda. But I am tired admiring this head, and if my visitor has any claim to beauty I must take care that she does not surpass me. So I will go to my cabinet and change to No. 17, which I think is my best appearance. Do you not agree?"

"Your No. 17 is extremely beautiful," answered Nanda, with another bow.

Again the Princess opened her mouth wide with tiredness. Then she said:

"Help me to rise."

So the maid helped her to get to her feet, although Langwidere was the stronger of the two. Then the Princess slowly walked across the silver floor to her cabinet, leaning heavily at every step upon Nanda's arm.

Now I must explain to you that the Princess Langwidere had thirty heads--as many as there are days in the month. But of course she could only wear one of them at a time, because she had only one neck. These heads were kept in what she called her "cabinet," which was a beautiful dressing-room that lay just between Langwidere's sleeping-room and the mirrored sitting-room. Each head was in a separate cupboard lined with velvet. The cupboards ran all around the sides of the dressing-room. They had doors with fine carved work, gold numbers on the outside and mirrors with jewels in their frames on the inside.

When the Princess got out of her crystal bed in the morning she went to her cabinet. She opened one of the velvet-lined cupboards and took the head it contained from its golden shelf. Then, with the help of the mirror inside the open door, she put on the head--as neat and straight as could be--and afterward called her maids to dress her for the day. She always wore a simple white costume, that suited all the heads. For, being able to change her face whenever she liked, the Princess had no interest in wearing a variety of gowns, as other ladies do who are forced to wear the same face constantly.

Of course the thirty heads were very different, no two formed alike, but all being of extreme loveliness. There were heads with golden hair, brown hair, rich reddish-brown hair and black hair; but none with gray hair. The heads had eyes of blue, of gray, of light brown, of brown and of black; but there were no red eyes among them, and all were bright and handsome. The noses were Grecian, Roman, turned-up and Oriental, representing all types of beauty. The mouths were of different sizes and shapes, showing white teeth like pearls when the heads smiled. As for small hollows in the cheeks, they appeared in cheeks and chins, wherever they might be most charming, and one or two heads had small brown spots upon the faces to contrast the better with the brightness of their skin.

One key unlocked all the velvet cupboards containing these treasures--a strange key carved from a single blood-red ruby--and this was fastened to a strong but slender chain which the Princess wore around her left wrist.

When Nanda had supported Langwidere to a position in front of cupboard No. 17, the Princess unlocked the door with her ruby key. She handed head No. 9, which she had been wearing, to the maid. Then she took No. 17 from its shelf and fitted it to her neck. It had black hair and dark eyes and a lovely skin white as a pearl, and when Langwidere wore it she knew she was remarkably beautiful in appearance.

There was only one trouble with No. 17. The temper that went with it (and which was hidden somewhere under the smooth black hair) was fiery, harsh and proud in the extreme. It often led the Princess to do unpleasant things which she regretted when she came to wear her other heads.

But she did not remember this today. She went to meet her guests in the drawing-room with a feeling of certainty that she would surprise them with her beauty.

However, she was greatly disappointed to find that her visitors were merely a small girl in a cotton dress, a copper man that would only go when wound up, and a yellow hen that was sitting happily in Langwidere's best work-basket. In the basket there was a china egg used for mending socks. (It may surprise you to learn that a princess ever does such a common thing as mend socks. But, if you will stop to think, you will realize that a princess is sure to wear holes in her socks, the same as other people; only it is not considered quite polite to mention the matter.)

"Oh!" said Langwidere, slightly lifting the nose of No. 17. "I thought someone of importance had called."

"Then you were right," declared Dorothy. "I am a good deal of importance myself, and when Billina lays an egg she has the proudest cackle you ever heard. As for Tiktok, he is the--"

"Stop--Stop!" ordered the Princess. Her splendid eyes flashed with anger. "How dare you bother me with your silly talk?"

"Why, you awful thing!" said Dorothy. She was not used to being treated so badly.

The Princess looked at her more closely.

"Tell me," she said again, "are you of royal blood?"

"Better than that, ma'am," said Dorothy. "I came from Kansas."

"Huh!" cried the Princess, with hate in her voice. "You are a foolish child, and I will not let you bother me. Run away, you little goose, and trouble someone else."

Dorothy was so angry that for a moment she could find no words to reply. But she rose from her chair. She was about to leave the room when the Princess stopped her. The Princess had been studying the girl's face. She said, more gently:

"Come nearer to me."

Dorothy obeyed, without a thought of fear. She stood before the Princess while Langwidere examined her face with careful attention.

"You are rather attractive," said the lady, after a moment. "Not at all beautiful, you understand. But you have a certain style of good looks that is different from that of any of my thirty heads. So I believe I'll take your head and give you No. 26 for it."

"It will do you no good to refuse," continued the Princess. "I need your head for my collection, and in the Land of Ev my will is law. I never have cared much for No. 26, and you will find that it is very little worn. Besides, it will do you just as well as the one you are wearing, for all practical purposes."

"I do not know anything about your No. 26, and I do not want to," said Dorothy, firmly. "I am not used to taking cast-off things, so I'll just keep my own head."

"You refuse?" cried the Princess, with a frown.

"Of course I do," was the reply.

"Then," said Langwidere, "I shall lock you up in a tower until you decide to obey me. Nanda," turning to her maid, "call my army."

Nanda rang a silver bell. At once a big fat colonel in a bright red uniform entered the room. Ten lean soldiers followed him. They all looked sad and discouraged. They greeted the princess in a very sad way.

"Carry that girl to the North Tower and lock her up!" cried the Princess, pointing to Dorothy.

"To hear is to obey," answered the big red colonel. He caught the child by her arm. But at that moment Tiktok raised his dinner-pail. He hit it so hard against the colonel's head that the big officer sat down upon the floor with a sudden bump. He looked both dazed and very much surprised.

"Help!" he shouted. The ten lean soldiers jumped to assist their leader.

There was great excitement for the next few moments. Tiktok had knocked down seven of the army. They were lying in every direction upon the carpet. Then suddenly the machine paused, with the dinner-pail raised for another blow. It remained perfectly still.

"My ac-tion has run down," he called to Dorothy. "Wind me up, quick."

She tried to obey. But the big colonel had by this time managed to get upon his feet again. So he grabbed fast hold of the girl and she was helpless to escape.

"This is too bad," said the machine. "I ought to have run six hours lon-ger, at least. But I sup-pose my long walk and my fight with the Wheel-ers made me run down fast-er than us-u-al."

"Well, it can't be helped," said Dorothy, with a sigh.

"Will you exchange heads with me?" demanded the Princess.

"No, indeed!" cried Dorothy.

"Then lock her up," said Langwidere to her soldiers. They led Dorothy to a high tower at the north of the palace and locked her securely within.

The soldiers afterward tried to lift Tiktok. But they found the machine so solid and heavy that they could not move it. So they left him standing in the center of the drawing-room.

"People will think I have a new statue," said Langwidere. "So it won't matter in the least, and Nanda can keep him well polished."

"What shall we do with the hen?" asked the colonel. He had just discovered Billina in the work-basket.

"Put her in the chicken-house," answered the Princess. "Someday I'll have her fried for breakfast."

"She looks rather tough, Your Highness," said Nanda, doubtfully.

"That is a base slander!" cried Billina, struggling wildly in the colonel's arms. "But the breed of chickens I come from is said to be poison to all princesses."

"Then," remarked Langwidere, "I will not fry the hen, but keep her to lay eggs. And if she does not do her duty I'll have her drowned in the horse trough."

7. Ozma of Oz to the Rescue

Nanda brought Dorothy bread and water for her supper. She slept upon a hard stone couch with a single pillow and a silken coverlet.

In the morning she leaned out of the window of her prison in the tower. She wanted to see if there was any way to escape. The room was not so very high up, when compared with our modern buildings. But it was far enough above the trees and farm houses to give her a good view of the surrounding country.

To the east she saw the forest, with the sands beyond it and the ocean beyond that. There was even a dark speck upon the shore. She thought it might be the chicken-coop in which she had arrived at this strange country.

Then she looked to the north. She saw a deep but narrow valley lying between two rocky mountains. A third mountain shut off the valley at the further end.

Westward the fertile Land of Ev suddenly ended a little way from the palace. The girl could see miles and miles of sandy desert that stretched further than her eyes could reach. It was this desert, she thought, with much interest, that alone separated her from the wonderful Land of Oz. She remembered sorrowfully that she had been told no one had ever been able to cross this dangerous waste but herself. Once a cyclone had carried her across it. A magical pair of silver shoes had carried her back again. But now she had neither a cyclone nor silver shoes to assist her. Her condition was sad indeed. For she had become the prisoner of a disagreeable princess. This princess insisted that she must exchange her head for another one that she was not used to. It might not fit her at all.

Really, there seemed no hope of help for her from her old friends in the Land of Oz. Thoughtfully she gazed from her narrow window. On all the desert not a living thing was stirring.

8. The Hungry Tiger

The first thing Dorothy did was to rush into the embrace of the Scarecrow. His painted face beamed with delight as he pressed her form to his straw-padded bosom. Then the Tin Woodman embraced her--very gently, for he knew his tin arms might hurt her if he squeezed too roughly.

These greetings having been exchanged, Dorothy took the key to Tiktok from her pocket and wound up the machine man's action. Now he could bow properly when introduced to the rest of the company. "She's a yellow hen who is another friend of mine," answered the girl, anxiously. "I wonder what has become of her?"

"She is in the chicken house, in the back yard," said the Princess. "My drawing-room is no place for hens."

"How dreadful!" said Dorothy.

"Is it not so?" replied the Hungry Tiger, licking his lips with his long red tongue. "Fat babies! Do they not sound delicious? But I have never eaten any, because my conscience tells me it is wrong. "In that you are mistaken," was the reply. "I am a good beast, perhaps, but a very bad tiger. It is the nature of tigers to be cruel and fierce. 9. The Royal Family of Ev

The Tin Woodman was the first to address the meeting.

11. The Nome King

After a while, they came near the mountain that blocked their path. It was the farthest edge of the Kingdom of Ev. The way grew dark and sad because the high peaks on both sides shut out the sunshine. It was very silent too. There were no birds to sing or squirrels to talk. The trees were far behind them, and only bare rocks remained.

Ozma and Dorothy were a little frightened by the silence. All the others were quiet and serious except the Sawhorse. As it ran along with the Scarecrow on its back, it hummed a strange song. This was the chorus:

"Would a wooden horse in a woodland go? Yes, yes! I sigh, he would, although If he had not had a wooden head He would climb the mountain top instead."

But no one paid any attention to this. They were now close to the Nome King's lands, and his wonderful underground palace could not be very far away.

Suddenly they heard a shout of mocking laughter and stopped short. They would have to stop in a minute anyway. The huge mountain blocked their way forward, and the path ran close up to a wall of rock and ended.

"Who was that laughing?" asked Ozma.

There was no reply. But in the dark they could see strange shapes move quickly across the face of the rock. Whatever these creatures were, they looked very like the rock itself. They were the color of rocks, and their shapes were as rough and uneven as if they had been broken off the side of the mountain. They stayed close to the steep cliff facing our friends. They moved up and down and this way and that, without any steady pattern, which was quite confusing. They seemed not to need places to rest their feet. They held on to the surface of the rock as a fly does to a window. They were never still for a moment.

"Do not mind them," said Tiktok, as Dorothy moved back. "They are on-ly the Nomes."

"And what are Nomes?" asked the girl, half frightened.

"They are rock fair-ies, and serve the Nome King," replied the machine. "But they will do us no harm. You must call for the King, be-cause with-out him you can ne-ver find the en-trance to the pal-ace."

The Tin Woodman at once chased his army and cried "halt!" When they had stopped running, he asked: "Where are you going?"

"I--I find I've forgotten the brush for my whiskers," said a general, shaking with fear. "S-s-so we are g-going back to get it!"

"Never!" cried all the other officers, hitting their chests angrily.

"For my part," said the private, meekly, "I must obey my officers. So when I am told to run, I run. And when I am told to fight, I fight."

"I demand that the Nome King appear to us!"

There was no reply. The Nomes on the mountain just laughed at them.

"You must not give orders to the Nome King," said Tiktok. "You do not rule him, as you do your own people."

So Ozma called again. She said:

"I ask the Nome King to appear to us."

Only laughing answers came back to her. The dark Nomes kept moving here and there on the rocky cliff.

"I'm not afraid to beg him," said Dorothy. "I'm only a little girl from Kansas. We have more dignity at home than we know what to do with. I will call the Nome King."

"Do," said the Hungry Tiger. "And if he makes a mess of you, I will gladly eat you for breakfast tomorrow morning."

"PLEASE Mr. Nome King, come here and see us."

The Nomes started to laugh again. But a low growl came from the mountain. In a flash they had all vanished from sight and were silent.

Then a door in the rock opened, and a voice cried:

"Enter!"

"Isn't it a trick?" asked the Tin Woodman.

"Never mind," replied Ozma. "We came here to rescue the poor Queen of Ev and her ten children. We must take some risks to do so."

"The Nome King is honest and good natured," said Tiktok. "You can trust him to do what is right."

So Ozma led the way, hand in hand with Dorothy. They passed through the arched doorway of rock and entered a long passage. It was lit by jewels set in the walls, with lamps behind them. There was no one to lead them, or to show them the way. But all the party pushed through the passage until they came to a round, domed cave that was grandly furnished.

In the center of this room was a throne carved out of a solid block of rock. It was rough and uneven in shape but glittering with great rubies and diamonds and emeralds on every part of its surface. And upon the throne sat the Nome King.

This important monarch of the Underground World was a little fat man. He was dressed in gray-brown garments that were the exact color of the rock throne in which he was seated. His bushy hair and flowing beard were also colored like the rocks, and so was his face. He wore no crown of any sort. His only ornament was a broad, jewel-studded belt that encircled his fat little body. As for his features, they seemed kindly and good humored. His eyes were turned merrily upon his visitors as Ozma and Dorothy stood before him, with their followers ranged in close order behind them.

"Why, he looks just like Santa Claus--only he isn't the same color!" whispered Dorothy to her friend. But the Nome King heard the speech, and it made him laugh aloud.

"'He had a red face and a round little belly That shook when he laughed like a bowl full of jelly!'"

said the monarch, in a pleasant voice. And they could all see that he really did shake like jelly when he laughed.

Both Ozma and Dorothy were much relieved to find the Nome King so jolly. A minute later he waved his right hand, and the girls each found a cushioned stool at her side.

"Sit down, my dears," said the King. "And tell me why you have come all this way to see me, and what I can do to make you happy."

While they seated themselves, the Nome King picked up a pipe. He took a glowing red coal out of his pocket and placed it in the bowl of the pipe. Then he began puffing out clouds of smoke that curled in rings above his head. Dorothy thought this made the little monarch look more like Santa Claus than ever. But Ozma now began speaking, and every one listened intently to her words.

"Your Majesty," said she, "I am the ruler of the Land of Oz. I have come here to ask you to release the good Queen of Ev and her ten children, whom you have enchanted and hold as your prisoners."

"Oh, no; you are mistaken about that," replied the King. "They are not my prisoners, but my slaves, whom I purchased from the King of Ev."

"But that was wrong," said Ozma.

"According to the laws of Ev, the king can do no wrong," answered the monarch. He was eying a ring of smoke he had just blown from his mouth. "So he had a perfect right to sell his family to me in exchange for a long life."

"You cheated him, though," declared Dorothy. "For the King of Ev did not have a long life. He jumped into the sea and was drowned."

"That was not my fault," said the Nome King. He crossed his legs and smiled contentedly. "I gave him the long life, all right. But he destroyed it."

"Then how could it be a long life?" asked Dorothy.

"Easily enough," was the reply. "Now suppose, my dear, that I gave you a pretty doll in exchange for a lock of your hair. And suppose that after you had received the doll you smashed it into pieces and destroyed it. Could you say that I had not given you a pretty doll?"

"No," answered Dorothy.

"And could you, in fairness, ask me to return to you the lock of hair, just because you had smashed the doll?"

"No," said Dorothy, again.

"Of course not," the Nome King returned. "Nor will I give up the Queen and her children because the King of Ev destroyed his long life by jumping into the sea. They belong to me and I shall keep them."

"But you are treating them cruelly," said Ozma, who was much distressed by the King's refusal.

"In what way?" he asked.

"By making them your slaves," said she.

16. Purple, Green, and Gold

The yellow hen stepped high and with an air of vast importance. She walked slowly over the rich velvet carpets of the splendid palace. She examined everything she met with her sharp little eyes.

Billina had a right to feel important. She alone shared the Nome King's secret and knew how to tell the objects that were transformations from those that had never been alive. She was very sure that her guesses would be correct. But before she began to make them she was curious to see all the magnificence of this underground palace. It was perhaps one of the most splendid and beautiful places in any fairyland.

As she went through the rooms she counted the purple decorations. Although some were small and hidden in strange places, Billina spied them all. She found the entire ten scattered about the various rooms. The green decorations she did not bother to count, for she thought she could find them all when the time came.

Finally, she made a survey of the entire palace and enjoyed its splendor. Then the yellow hen returned to one of the rooms where she had noticed a large purple footstool. She placed a claw upon this and said "Ev." At once the footstool vanished and a lovely lady stood before her. She was tall and slender and most beautifully robed.

The lady's eyes were round with surprise for a moment. She could not remember her transformation. She could not imagine what had brought her back to life.

"Good morning, ma'am," said Billina, in her sharp voice. "You're looking quite well, considering your age."

"Who speaks?" demanded the Queen of Ev. She drew herself up proudly.

"Why, my name is Bill, by rights," answered the hen. She was now sitting on the back of a chair. "Although Dorothy has put scollops on it and made it Billina. But the name does not matter. I have saved you from the Nome King, and you are a slave no longer."

"Then I thank you for the kind favor," said the Queen, with a graceful courtesy. "But, my children--tell me, I beg of you--where are my children?" and she held her hands together in anxious asking.

She flew down from her perch and walked into the next room. The Queen followed. As she passed a low table a small green grasshopper caught her eye. 17. The Scarecrow Wins the Fight

After Billina had entered the palace Dorothy and Evring sat down to wait for the success or failure of her mission. The Nome King sat on his throne and smoked his long pipe for a while in a cheerful and contented mood.

When they came to the gulf, Ozma's magic carpet carried them all over in safety. Now they began to pass the trees, where birds were singing. The breeze that came to them from the farms of Ev smelled sweet with flowers and new-cut hay. The sunshine fell full upon them. It warmed them and drove away from their bodies the cold and wet of the underground kingdom of the Nomes.

"I would be quite content," said the Scarecrow to Tiktok, "if only the Tin Woodman were with us. But it breaks my heart to leave him behind."

"He was a fine fellow," replied Tiktok, "although his material was not very durable."

"Oh, tin is an excellent material," the Scarecrow said quickly. "And if anything ever happened to poor Nick Chopper, he was always easily repaired with solder. Besides, he did not have to be wound up, and was not likely to get out of order."

"I sometimes wish," said Tiktok, "that I was stuffed with straw, as you are. It is hard to be made of copper."

"I have no reason to complain of my lot," replied the Scarecrow. "A little fresh straw, now and then, makes me as good as new. But I can never be the polished gentleman that my poor departed friend, the Tin Woodman, was."

You may be sure the royal children of Ev and their Queen mother were delighted to see their beloved country again. When the towers of the palace of Ev came into view, they could not stop cheering at the sight. Little Evring, riding in front of Dorothy, was so overjoyed that he took a curious tin whistle from his pocket. He blew a shrill blast that made the Sawhorse leap and jump in sudden alarm.

"What is that?" asked Billina, who had been forced to flap her wings to keep her seat upon the head of the frightened Sawhorse.

"That's my whistle," said Prince Evring, holding it out on his hand.

It was in the shape of a little fat pig, made of tin and painted green. The whistle was in the tail of the pig.

"Where did you get it?" asked the yellow hen, closely examining the toy with her bright eyes.

"Why, I picked it up in the Nome King's palace, while Dorothy was making her guesses, and I put it in my pocket," answered the little Prince.

Billina laughed, or at least she made the peculiar cackle that served her as a laugh.

"No wonder I could not find the Tin Woodman," she said. "And no wonder the magic belt did not make him appear, or the King could not find him, either!"

"What do you mean?" questioned Dorothy.

"Why, the Prince had him in his pocket," cried Billina, cackling again.

"I did not!" protested little Evring. "I only took the whistle."

"Well, then, watch me," returned the hen. She reached out a claw, touched the whistle and said "Ev."

"Good afternoon," said the Tin Woodman, taking off his funnel cap and bowing to Dorothy and the Prince. "I think I must have been asleep for the first time since I was made of tin, for I do not remember our leaving the Nome King."

"You have been enchanted," answered the girl, throwing an arm around her old friend and hugging him tight in her joy. "But it is all right now."

"I want my whistle!" said the little Prince, beginning to cry.

"Hush!" cautioned Billina. "The whistle is lost, but you may have another when you get home."

The Scarecrow had fairly thrown himself upon the chest of his old comrade. He was so surprised and delighted to see him again. Tiktok squeezed the Tin Woodman's hand so earnestly that he dented some of his fingers. Then they had to make way for Ozma to welcome the tin man. The army caught sight of him and set up a cheer. Everybody was delighted and happy.

For the Tin Woodman was a great favorite with all who knew him. His sudden recovery, after they had thought he was lost to them forever, was indeed a pleasant surprise.

Before long the cavalcade arrived at the royal palace. A great crowd of people had gathered there to welcome their Queen and her ten children. There was much shouting and cheering. The people threw flowers in their path, and every face wore a happy smile.

They found the Princess Langwidere in her mirrored room. She was admiring one of her handsomest heads. It had rich chestnut hair, dreamy walnut eyes and a shapely hickorynut nose. She was very glad to be relieved of her duties to the people of Ev. The Queen graciously permitted her to keep her rooms and her cabinet of heads as long as she lived.

Then the Queen took her eldest son out upon a balcony that overlooked the crowd of subjects gathered below. She said to them:

"Here is your future ruler, King Evardo Fifteenth. He is fifteen years of age. He has fifteen silver buckles on his jacket and is the fifteenth Evardo to rule the land of Ev."

The people shouted their approval fifteen times. Even the Wheelers, some of whom were present, loudly promised to obey the new King.

So the Queen placed a big crown of gold, set with rubies, upon Evardo's head. She threw an ermine robe over his shoulders and proclaimed him King. He bowed gratefully to all his subjects and then went away to see if he could find any cake in the royal pantry.

Ozma of Oz and her people, as well as Dorothy, Tiktok and Billina, were splendidly entertained by the Queen mother. She owed all her happiness to their kind offices. That evening the yellow hen was publicly presented with a beautiful necklace of pearls and sapphires, as a token of esteem from the new King.

20. The Emerald City

Dorothy decided to accept Ozma's invitation to return with her to the Land of Oz. There was no greater chance of her getting home from Ev than from Oz. The little girl was anxious to see once more the country where she had encountered such wonderful adventures. By this time Uncle Henry would have reached Australia in his ship. He had probably given her up for lost. So he could not worry any more than he did if she stayed away from him a while longer. So she would go to Oz.

They bade good-bye to the people of Ev. The King promised Ozma that he would ever be grateful to her and render the Land of Oz any service that might lie within his power.

And then they approached the edge of the dangerous desert. Ozma threw down the magic carpet, which at once unrolled far enough for all of them to walk upon it without being crowded.

Tiktok said he was Dorothy's faithful follower because he belonged to her. So he was allowed to join the group. Before they started, the girl wound up his machinery as far as possible. Then the copper man stepped off as quickly as any one of them.

Ozma also invited Billina to visit the Land of Oz. The yellow hen was glad enough to go where new sights and scenes waited for her.

They began the trip across the desert early in the morning. They stopped only long enough for Billina to lay her daily egg. Before sunset they saw the green slopes and hills with many trees of the beautiful Land of Oz. They entered it in the Munchkin territory. The King of the Munchkins met them at the border and welcomed Ozma with great respect. He was very pleased by her safe return. For Ozma of Oz ruled the King of the Munchkins, the King of the Winkies, the King of the Quadlings and the King of the Gillikins just as those kings ruled their own people. And this supreme ruler of the Land of Oz lived in a great town of her own, called the Emerald City. It was in the exact center of the four kingdoms of the Land of Oz.

The Munchkin king entertained them at his palace that night. In the morning they set out for the Emerald City. They travelled over a road of yellow brick that led straight to the gates covered with jewels. Everywhere the people came out to greet their beloved Ozma. They also called out happily to the Scarecrow, the Tin Woodman and the Cowardly Lion, who were popular favorites. Dorothy too remembered some of the people. They had helped her on the occasion of her first visit to Oz. They were well pleased to see the little Kansas girl again. They showered her with compliments and good wishes.

At one place, where they stopped to rest themselves, Ozma accepted a bowl of milk from the hands of a pretty dairy-maid. Then she looked at the girl more closely, and exclaimed:

"Why, it is Jinjur--is it not!"

"Yes, your Highness," was the reply, as Jinjur dropped a low bow. And Dorothy looked with wonder at this lively appearing person. She had once gathered an army of women and driven the Scarecrow from the throne of the Emerald City. She had even fought a battle with the powerful army of Glinda the Sorceress.

"I have married a man who owns nine cows," said Jinjur to Ozma. "And now I am happy and contented and willing to lead a quiet life and mind my own business."

"Where is your husband?" asked Ozma.

"He is in the house, nursing a black eye," replied Jinjur, calmly. "The foolish man would insist upon milking the red cow when I wanted him to milk the white one. But he will know better next time, I am sure."

Then the group moved on again. They crossed a broad river on a ferry and passed many fine farm houses that were dome shaped and painted a pretty green color. Then they came in sight of a large building that was covered with flags and bright cloth.

"I do not remember that building," said Dorothy. "What is it?"

"That is the College of Art and Athletic Perfection," replied Ozma. "I had it built quite recently, and the Woggle-Bug is its president. It keeps him busy, and the young men who attend the college are no worse off than they were before. You see, in this country are a number of youths who do not like to work, and the college is an excellent place for them."

And now they came in sight of the Emerald City. The people flocked out to greet their lovely ruler. There were several bands and many officers and officials of the realm. There was also a crowd of citizens in their holiday clothes.

Thus the beautiful Ozma was escorted by a brilliant procession to her royal city. The cheering was so great that she was obliged to constantly bow to the right and left to acknowledge the greetings of her subjects.

That evening there was a grand reception in the royal palace. The most important persons of Oz attended. Jack Pumpkinhead, who was a little overripe but still active, read an address. He congratulated Ozma of Oz upon the success of her generous mission to rescue the royal family of a neighboring kingdom.

Then magnificent gold medals set with precious stones were presented to each of the twenty-six officers. The Tin Woodman was given a new axe studded with diamonds. The Scarecrow received a silver jar of face powder. Dorothy was presented with a pretty small crown and made a Princess of Oz. Tiktok received two bracelets set with eight rows of very clear and sparkling emeralds.

Afterward they sat down to a splendid feast. Ozma put Dorothy at her right and Billina at her left. The hen sat upon a golden perch and ate from a jeweled platter. Then were placed the Scarecrow, the Tin Woodman and Tiktok, with baskets of lovely flowers before them, because they did not require food. The twenty-six officers were at the lower end of the table. The Lion and the Tiger also had seats, and were served on golden platters that held a half a bushel at one time.

The wealthiest and most important citizens of the Emerald City were proud to wait upon these famous travelers. They were assisted by a lively little maid named Jellia Jamb. The Scarecrow pinched her pink cheeks and seemed to know her very well.

During the feast Ozma grew thoughtful, and suddenly she asked:

"Where is the private?"

"Oh, he is sweeping out the buildings for soldiers," replied one of the generals, who was busy eating a leg of a turkey. "But I have ordered him a dish of bread and sweet syrup to eat when his work is done."

"Let him be sent for," said the girl ruler.

While they waited for this command to be obeyed, she asked:

"Have we any other privates in the armies?"

"Oh, yes," replied the Tin Woodman, "I believe there are three, altogether."

The private now entered. He greeted his officers and the royal Ozma very respectfully.

"What is your name, my man?" asked the girl.

"Omby Amby," answered the private.

"Then, Omby Amby," said she, "I promote you to be Captain General of all the armies of my kingdom. And especially to be Commander of my Body Guard at the royal palace."

"It is very expensive to hold so many offices," said the private, hesitating. "I have no money with which to buy uniforms."

"You shall be supplied from the royal treasury," said Ozma.

Then the private was given a seat at the table. The other officers welcomed him warmly, and the feasting and fun were resumed.

Suddenly Jellia Jamb exclaimed:

"There is nothing more to eat! The Hungry Tiger has eaten everything!"

"But that is not the worst of it," declared the Tiger, sadly. "Somewhere or somehow, I have actually lost my appetite!"

Dorothy spent several very happy weeks in the Land of Oz as the guest of the royal Ozma. Ozma was glad to please and interest the little Kansas girl. Dorothy met many new people and saw many old friends again. Everywhere she went, Dorothy found herself among friends.

One day, however, she sat in Ozma's private room. She noticed a picture hanging on the wall. The picture kept changing its appearance. At one time it showed a meadow. At another time it showed a forest, a lake or a village.

"How strange!" she exclaimed. She watched the changing scenes for a few moments.

"Yes," said Ozma. "That is really a wonderful invention in magic. If I wish to see any part of the world or any person living, I only need to express the wish. Then it is shown in the picture."

"May I use it?" asked Dorothy, eagerly.

"Of course, my dear."

"Then I would like to see the old Kansas farm, and Aunt Em," said the girl.

Instantly the well remembered farmhouse appeared in the picture. Aunt Em could be seen quite clearly. She was washing dishes by the kitchen window. She seemed quite well and contented. The hired men and the teams were in the harvest fields behind the house. The corn and wheat seemed to the child to be in excellent condition. On the side porch Dorothy's pet dog, Toto, was lying fast asleep in the sun. To her surprise, old Speckles was running around with a group of twelve new chickens following after her.

"Everything seems all right at home," said Dorothy, with a sigh of relief. "Now I wonder what Uncle Henry is doing."

The scene in the picture at once moved to Australia. There, in a pleasant room in Sydney, Uncle Henry was seated in an easy chair. He was smoking his pipe in a serious way. He looked sad and lonely. His hair was now quite white. His hands and face were thin and wasted.

"Oh!" cried Dorothy, in an anxious voice. "I am sure Uncle Henry is not getting any better. It is because he is worried about me. Ozma, dear, I must go to him at once!"

"How can you?" asked Ozma.

"I do not know," replied Dorothy. "But let us go to Glinda the Good. I am sure she will help me. She will advise me how to get to Uncle Henry."

Ozma readily agreed to this plan. She had the Sawhorse tied to a pretty green and pink carriage. The two girls rode away to visit the famous sorceress.

Glinda received them kindly. She listened to Dorothy's story with attention.

"I have the magic belt, you know," said the little girl. "If I put it around my waist and commanded it to take me to Uncle Henry, would it not do it?"

"I think so," replied Glinda, with a smile.

"And then," continued Dorothy, "if I ever wanted to come back here again, the belt would bring me."

"In that you are wrong," said the sorceress. "The belt has magical powers only while it is in some fairy country, such as the Land of Oz, or the Land of Ev. Indeed, my little friend, suppose you wore it and wished yourself in Australia, with your uncle. The wish would surely be fulfilled, because it was made in fairyland. But you would not find the magic belt around you when you arrived at your destination."

"What would become of it?" asked the girl.

"It would be lost, as were your silver shoes when you visited Oz before. No one would ever see it again. It seems too bad to destroy the use of the magic belt in that way, does it not?"

"Then," said Dorothy, after a moment of thought, "I will give the magic belt to Ozma. She can use it in her own country. And she can wish me transported to Uncle Henry without losing the belt."

"That is a wise plan," replied Glinda.

So they rode back to the Emerald City. On the way, they arranged a plan. Every Saturday morning Ozma would look at Dorothy in her magic picture, wherever the little girl might chance to be. And if she saw Dorothy make a certain signal, then Ozma would know that the little Kansas girl wanted to revisit the Land of Oz. By means of the Nome King's magic belt, Ozma would wish that she might instantly return.

This having been agreed upon, Dorothy said good-bye to all her friends. Tiktok wanted to go to Australia too. But Dorothy knew that the machine man would never do for a servant in a civilized country. The chances were that his machinery would not work at all. So she left him in Ozma's care.

Billina, on the contrary, preferred the Land of Oz to any other country. She refused to accompany Dorothy.

"The bugs and ants that I find here are the finest tasting in the world," declared the yellow hen. "And there are plenty of them. So here I shall end my days. And I must say, Dorothy, my dear, that you are very foolish to go back into that stupid, dull world again."

"Uncle Henry needs me," said Dorothy, simply. Everyone except Billina thought it was right that she should go.

All Dorothy's friends of the Land of Oz, both old and new, gathered in a group in front of the palace. They came to bid her a sad good-bye and to wish her long life and happiness. After much hand shaking, Dorothy kissed Ozma once more. Then she handed her the Nome King's magic belt, saying:

"Now, dear Princess, when I wave my cloth, please wish me with Uncle Henry. I am very sorry to leave you--and the Scarecrow--and the Tin Woodman--and the Cowardly Lion--and Tiktok--and--and everybody--but I do want my Uncle Henry! So good-bye, all of you."

Then the little girl stood on one of the big green stones which decorated the courtyard. She looked once again at each of her friends. Then she waved her cloth.

"No," said Dorothy, "I was not drowned at all. And I have come to nurse you and take care of you, Uncle Henry. And you must promise to get well as soon as possible."

Uncle Henry smiled and held his little niece close in his lap.

"I am better already, my darling," said he.
