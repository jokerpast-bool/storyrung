CHAPTER 1. ON THE GREAT ALKALI PLAIN.

ON THE GREAT ALKALI PLAIN.

In the central part of the great North American Continent there lies a dry and ugly desert. For many long years it served as a wall against the advance of civilization. From the Sierra Nevada to Nebraska, and from the Yellowstone River in the north to the Colorado in the south, is a region of emptiness and silence. Nature is not always in one mood throughout this grim district. It includes snow-capped and high mountains, and dark and sad valleys. There are fast-flowing rivers which rush through rough canyons. And there are enormous plains, which in winter are white with snow, and in summer are grey with the salty alkali dust. They all keep, however, the common features of barrenness, unfriendliness, and suffering.

There are no inhabitants of this land of despair. A band of Pawnees or of Blackfeet may sometimes cross it in order to reach other hunting grounds. But the toughest of the braves are glad to lose sight of those awful plains, and to find themselves once more upon their prairies. The coyote hides among the low bushes. The buzzard flies heavily through the air. And the clumsy grizzly bear walks heavily through the dark ravines, and picks up such food as it can among the rocks. These are the only dwellers in the wilderness.

In the whole world there can be no more dreary view than that from the northern slope of the Sierra Blanco. As far as the eye can reach stretches the great flat plain-land, all covered with patches of alkali, and cut by clumps of the small chaparral bushes. On the far edge of the horizon lies a long chain of mountain peaks, with their rough tops marked with snow. In this great stretch of country there is no sign of life, nor of anything belonging to life. There is no bird in the steel-blue heaven, no movement upon the dull, grey earth—above all, there is absolute silence. Listen as one may, there is no shadow of a sound in all that mighty wilderness. Nothing but silence—complete and heart-subduing silence.

It has been said there is nothing belonging to life upon the broad plain. That is hardly true. Looking down from the Sierra Blanco, one sees a pathway traced out across the desert, which winds away and is lost in the far distance. It is rutted with wheels and trodden down by the feet of many adventurers. Here and there there are scattered white objects which shine in the sun, and stand out against the dull deposit of alkali. Come close, and examine them! They are bones: some large and rough, others smaller and more delicate. The former have belonged to oxen, and the latter to men. For fifteen hundred miles one may trace this terrible caravan route by these scattered remains of those who had fallen by the wayside.

Looking down on this very scene, there stood upon the fourth of May, eighteen hundred and forty-seven, a solitary traveller. His appearance was such that he might have been the very spirit or demon of the region. An observer would have found it difficult to say whether he was nearer to forty or to sixty. His face was thin and worn, and the brown parchment-like skin was drawn tightly over the projecting bones. His long, brown hair and beard were all marked and mixed with white. His eyes were sunken in his head, and burned with an unnatural light. The hand which held his rifle was hardly more fleshy than that of a skeleton. As he stood, he leaned upon his weapon for support. Yet his tall figure and the massive frame of his bones suggested a wiry and strong constitution. His thin face, however, and his clothes, which hung so loosely over his shrivelled limbs, showed what it was that gave him that old and weak appearance. The man was dying—dying from hunger and from thirst.

He had toiled painfully down the ravine, and on to this little hill, in the vain hope of seeing some signs of water. Now the great salt plain stretched before his eyes, and the distant belt of wild mountains, without a sign anywhere of plant or tree, which might show the presence of water. In all that broad landscape there was no gleam of hope. North, and east, and west he looked with wild questioning eyes. Then he realised that his wanderings had come to an end, and that there, on that barren rock, he was about to die. “Why not here, as well as in a soft bed, twenty years from now,” he muttered, as he seated himself in the shelter of a large rock.

Before sitting down, he had put upon the ground his useless rifle, and also a large bundle tied up in a grey shawl, which he had carried hanging over his right shoulder. It appeared to be somewhat too heavy for his strength, for in lowering it, it came down on the ground with some little force. Instantly there broke from the grey parcel a little moaning cry. From it there pushed out a small, scared face, with very bright brown eyes, and two little spotted, dimpled fists.

“You’ve hurt me!” said a childish voice in reproach.

“Have I though,” the man answered with regret, “I didn’t go for to do it.” As he spoke he unwrapped the grey shawl and freed a pretty little girl of about five years of age. Her dainty shoes and smart pink frock with its little linen apron all showed a mother’s care. The child was pale and wan, but her healthy arms and legs showed that she had suffered less than her companion.

“How is it now?” he answered anxiously, for she was still rubbing the tangled golden curls which covered the back of her head.

“Kiss it and make it well,” she said, with perfect seriousness, showing the injured part up to him. “That’s what mother used to do. Where’s mother?”

“Mother’s gone. I guess you’ll see her before long.”

“Gone, eh!” said the little girl. “Funny, she didn’t say good-bye. She ‘most always did if she was just goin’ over to Auntie’s for tea, and now she’s been away three days. Say, it’s awful dry, ain’t it? Ain’t there no water, nor nothing to eat?”

“No, there ain’t nothing, dearie. You’ll just need to be patient awhile, and then you’ll be all right. Put your head up agin me like that, and then you’ll feel bullier. It ain’t easy to talk when your lips is like leather, but I guess I’d best let you know how the cards lie. What’s that you’ve got?”

“Pretty things! fine things!” cried the little girl with excitement, holding up two glittering fragments of mica. “When we goes back to home I’ll give them to brother Bob.”

“You’ll see prettier things than them soon,” said the man with confidence. “You just wait a bit. I was going to tell you though—you remember when we left the river?”

“Oh, yes.”

“Well, we thought we would find another river soon, you see. But something was wrong. The compasses, or the map, or something, and it did not show up. The water ran out. Just a little drop left for people like you and—and—”

“And you could not wash yourself,” his companion interrupted seriously. She stared up at his dirty face.

“No, and not drink either. And Mr. Bender, he was the first to go. Then Indian Pete, and then Mrs. McGregor, and then Johnny Hones, and then, my dear, your mother.”

“Then mother is dead too,” cried the little girl. She dropped her face in her apron and cried bitterly.

“Yes, they all went except you and me. Then I thought there was some chance of water in this direction. So I lifted you over my shoulder and we walked together. It does not seem as though we have made things better. There is a very small chance for us now!”

“Do you mean that we are going to die too?” asked the child. She stopped her crying and raised her tear-marked face.

“I guess that is about the size of it.”

“Why did you not say so before?” she said, laughing happily. “You gave me such a fright. Why, of course, now as long as we die we will be with mother again.”

“Yes, you will, my dear.”

“And you too. I will tell her how very good you have been. I will bet she meets us at the door of Heaven with a big pitcher of water, and a lot of buckwheat cakes, hot, and toasted on both sides, like Bob and me were fond of. How long will it be first?”

“I do not know—not very long.” The man’s eyes were fixed upon the northern horizon. In the blue vault of the heaven there had appeared three little specks. They increased in size every moment, so rapidly did they approach. They quickly resolved themselves into three large brown birds. They circled over the heads of the two wanderers, and then settled upon some rocks which overlooked them. They were buzzards, the vultures of the west, whose coming is the forerunner of death.

“Cocks and hens,” cried the little girl happily. She pointed at their bad-omen forms, and clapped her hands to make them rise. “Say, did God make this country?”

“Of course He did,” said her companion. He was rather startled by this unexpected question.

“He made the country down in Illinois, and He made the Missouri,” the little girl continued. “I guess somebody else made the country in these parts. It is not nearly so well done. They forgot the water and the trees.”

“What would you think of offering up prayer?” the man asked shyly.

“It is not night yet,” she answered.

“It does not matter. It is not quite regular, but He will not mind that, you bet. You say over those ones that you used to say every night in the wagon when we were on the Plains.”

“Why do you not say some yourself?” the child asked, with wondering eyes.

“I do not remember them,” he answered. “I have not said any since I was half the height of that gun. I guess it is never too late. You say them out, and I will stand by and come in on the choruses.”

“Then you will need to kneel down, and me too,” she said. She laid the shawl out for that purpose. “You have got to put your hands up like this. It makes you feel kind of good.”

It was a strange sight had there been anything but the buzzards to see it. Side by side on the narrow shawl knelt the two wanderers, the little prattling child and the reckless, hardened adventurer. Her chubby face, and his haggard, skinny face were both turned up to the cloudless heaven in heartfelt request to that dread being with whom they were face to face. The two voices—the one thin and clear, the other deep and harsh—united in the request for mercy and forgiveness. The prayer finished, they resumed their seat in the shadow of the boulder until the child fell asleep, nestling upon the broad breast of her protector. He watched over her sleep for some time, but Nature proved to be too strong for him. For three days and three nights he had allowed himself neither rest nor sleep. Slowly the eyelids drooped over the tired eyes, and the head sunk lower and lower upon the breast, until the man’s gray beard was mixed with the gold hair of his companion, and both slept the same deep and dreamless sleep.

Had the wanderer remained awake for another half hour a strange sight would have met his eyes. Far away on the extreme edge of the alkali plain there rose up a little spray of dust, very slight at first, and hardly to be distinguished from the mists of the distance, but gradually growing higher and broader until it formed a solid, well-defined cloud. This cloud continued to increase in size until it became evident that it could only be raised by a great multitude of moving creatures. In more fertile spots the observer would have come to the conclusion that one of those great herds of bisons which graze upon the prairie land was approaching him. This was obviously impossible in these dry wilds. As the whirl of dust drew nearer to the solitary bluff upon which the two castaways were resting, the canvas-covered tilts of wagons and the figures of armed horsemen began to show up through the haze, and the apparition revealed itself as being a great caravan upon its journey for the West. But what a caravan! When the head of it had reached the base of the mountains, the rear was not yet visible on the horizon. Right across the enormous plain stretched the straggling array, wagons and carts, men on horseback, and men on foot. Innumerable women who staggered along under burdens, and children who toddled beside the wagons or peeped out from under the white coverings. This was evidently no ordinary party of immigrants, but rather some nomad people who had been compelled from stress of circumstances to seek themselves a new country. There rose through the clear air a confused clattering and rumbling from this great mass of humanity, with the creaking of wheels and the neighing of horses. Loud as it was, it was not sufficient to rouse the two tired wayfarers above them.

At the head of the column there rode a score or more of grave ironfaced men, clad in sombre homespun garments and armed with rifles. On reaching the base of the bluff they halted, and held a short council among themselves.

“The wells are to the right, my brothers,” said one, a hard-lipped, clean-shaven man with grizzly hair.

“To the right of the Sierra Blanco—so we shall reach the Rio Grande,” said another.

“Fear not for water,” cried a third. “He who could draw it from the rocks will not now abandon His own chosen people.”

“Amen! Amen!” responded the whole party.

They were about to start their journey again. Then one of the youngest men had the sharpest eyes. He gave a cry and pointed up at the rough rock above them. From the top of it, a little bit of pink cloth moved in the wind. It stood out hard and bright against the gray rocks behind. When they saw it, everyone pulled their horses to a stop. They took their guns off their shoulders. New riders came at a fast run to strengthen the front group. The word "Redskins" was on every lip.

"There cannot be many Injuns here," said the older man who seemed to be in command. "We have passed the Pawnees. There are no other tribes until we cross the great mountains."

"Shall I go forward and see, Brother Stangerson?" asked one of the group.

"And I," "and I," cried a dozen voices.

"Leave your horses below and we will wait for you here," the Elder answered. In a moment the young men had got off their horses. They tied them and were climbing the steep slope. It led up to the thing that had made them curious. They moved fast and without a sound. They had the confidence and skill of trained trackers. The watchers from the plain below could see them move quickly from rock to rock. Then their figures stood out against the line of the sky. The young man who had first given the alarm was leading them. Suddenly his followers saw him throw up his hands. He seemed overcome with surprise. When they joined him, they were affected in the same way by the sight that met their eyes.

On the little flat top of the empty hill there stood a single giant rock. Against this rock lay a tall man. He had a long beard and a hard face, but he was extremely thin. His calm face and regular breathing showed that he was fast asleep. Beside him lay a little child. Her round white arms were around his brown, strong neck. Her golden-haired head rested on the breast of his velveteen tunic. Her rosy lips were parted. They showed the regular line of snow-white teeth within. A playful smile played over her childish features. Her plump little white legs ended in white socks and neat shoes with shining buckles. They offered a strange contrast to the long, thin limbs of her companion. On the shelf of rock above this strange couple there stood three serious buzzards. At the sight of the new arrivals they gave harsh screams of disappointment. They flew away with angry wing beats.

The cries of the foul birds woke the two sleepers. They stared about them in confusion. The man struggled to his feet and looked down upon the plain. It had been so empty when sleep had come over him. Now this enormous body of men and beasts was moving across it. His face took on an expression of disbelief as he gazed. He passed his bony hand over his eyes. "This is what they call delirium, I guess," he muttered. The child stood beside him, holding on to the skirt of his coat. She said nothing but looked all round her. She had the wondering, questioning gaze of childhood.

The rescuing party were quickly able to convince the two castaways that their appearance was no delusion. One of them seized the little girl and lifted her onto his shoulder. Two others supported her gaunt companion and helped him towards the waggons.

"My name is John Ferrier," the wanderer explained. "Me and that little one are all that is left of twenty-one people. The rest are all dead of thirst and hunger away down in the south."

"Is she your child?" asked someone.

"I guess she is now," the other cried, defiantly. "She is mine because I saved her. No man will take her from me. She is Lucy Ferrier from this day on. Who are you, though?" he continued. He glanced with curiosity at his strong, sunburned rescuers. "There seems to be a powerful lot of you."

"Nearly ten thousand," said one of the young men. "We are the persecuted children of God—the chosen of the Angel Merona."

"I never heard tell of him," said the wanderer. "He appears to have chosen a fair crowd of you."

"Do not joke about that which is sacred," said the other sternly. "We are of those who believe in those sacred writings. They were drawn in Egyptian letters on plates of beaten gold. They were handed to the holy Joseph Smith at Palmyra. We have come from Nauvoo, in the State of Illinois. There we had founded our temple. We have come to seek a refuge from the violent man and from the godless. This is true even though it be the heart of the desert."

The name of Nauvoo evidently brought back memories to John Ferrier. "I see," he said. "You are the Mormons."

"We are the Mormons," answered his companions with one voice.

"And where are you going?"

"We do not know. The hand of God is leading us under the person of our Prophet. You must come before him. He shall say what is to be done with you."

They had reached the base of the hill by this time. They were surrounded by crowds of the pilgrims. There were pale-faced, meek-looking women. There were strong laughing children. There were anxious, serious-eyed men. Many were the cries of surprise and of pity that arose from them. They saw how young one of the strangers was and how poor the other was. Their escort did not stop, however. They pushed on, followed by a great crowd of Mormons. At last they reached a waggon. It was easy to notice because of its great size and its bright, showy appearance. Six horses were tied to it, while the others had two, or at most four each. Beside the driver there sat a man who could not have been more than thirty years of age. But his massive head and resolute expression marked him as a leader. He was reading a brown-backed volume. As the crowd approached, he laid it aside. He listened carefully to an account of what had happened. Then he turned to the two castaways.

"If we take you with us," he said, in serious words, "it can only be as believers in our own creed. We shall have no wolves in our fold. Better far that your bones should bleach in this wilderness. That is better than that you should prove to be that little speck of decay which in time corrupts the whole fruit. Will you come with us on these terms?"

"Guess I'll come with you on any terms," said Ferrier. He said it with such emphasis that the grave Elders could not stop a smile. The leader alone kept his stern, impressive expression.

"Take him, Brother Stangerson," he said. "Give him food and drink, and the child likewise. Let it be your task also to teach him our holy creed. We have delayed long enough. Forward! On, on to Zion!"

"On, on to Zion!" cried the crowd of Mormons. The words passed down the long line of travelers, from mouth to mouth, until they faded away into a low sound in the far distance. With the cracking of whips and the creaking of wheels, the great wagons began to move. Soon the whole group was moving along once more. The Elder who had been given charge of the two homeless children led them to his wagon. A meal was already waiting for them there.

"You shall stay here," he said. "In a few days you will have recovered from your tiredness. In the meantime, remember that now and forever you belong to our religion. Brigham Young has said it. He has spoken with the voice of Joseph Smith, which is the voice of God."

CHAPTER 2. THE SCIENCE OF DEDUCTION.

THE SCIENCE OF DEDUCTION.

We met the next day as he had arranged. We looked at the rooms at No. 221B, Baker Street, which he had told me about at our meeting. They were two comfortable bedrooms and one large sitting-room with plenty of light. The room had two wide windows and pleasant furniture. The rooms were so desirable in every way, and the price seemed so fair when we split it between us, that we made the deal right there. We moved in at once. That very evening I moved my things from the hotel. The next morning Sherlock Holmes came after me with several boxes and bags. For a day or two we were busy opening our things and putting them in the best places. After that, we slowly began to settle down and get used to our new home.

Holmes was certainly not a hard man to live with. He was quiet in his ways, and his habits were regular. He was rarely up after ten at night. He had always eaten breakfast and gone out before I got up in the morning. Sometimes he spent his day at the chemical laboratory. Sometimes he was in the rooms where bodies are cut open for study. Now and then he took long walks, which seemed to take him into the poorest parts of the City. Nothing could beat his energy when the working mood was on him. But now and then a reaction would take hold of him. For days on end he would lie on the sofa in the sitting-room. He hardly said a word or moved a muscle from morning to night. On these occasions I noticed such a far-away, empty look in his eyes that I might have suspected him of using some drug. But his whole life was so moderate and clean that such an idea was not possible.

As the weeks went by, my interest in him grew. My curiosity about his aims in life slowly deepened and increased. His very person and appearance were such as to catch the attention of even the most casual observer. He was a little over six feet tall. He was so very thin that he seemed much taller. His eyes were sharp and piercing, except during those periods of dullness I have mentioned. His thin, hawk-like nose gave his whole face a look of alertness and decision. His chin, too, had the prominence and squareness that mark a man of determination. His hands were always marked with ink and stained with chemicals. Yet he had an extraordinary delicacy of touch. I often had occasion to observe this when I watched him handling his fragile scientific instruments.

The reader may think me a hopeless busybody when I confess how much this man stirred my curiosity. I often tried to break through the silence he showed on all that concerned himself. Before you judge me, however, remember how aimless my life was, and how little there was to hold my attention. My health forbade me from going out unless the weather was exceptionally mild. I had no friends who would call on me and break the sameness of my daily life. Under these circumstances, I eagerly welcomed the little mystery that hung around my companion. I spent much of my time trying to unravel it.

He was not studying medicine. He had himself confirmed Stamford's opinion on that point in reply to a question. Neither did he appear to have followed any course of reading that might fit him for a degree in science. Nor had he followed any other recognized path that would give him an entrance into the learned world. Yet his zeal for certain studies was remarkable. Within eccentric limits his knowledge was so extraordinarily ample and minute that his observations have fairly astounded me. Surely no man would work so hard or gain such precise information unless he had some definite end in view. Readers who jump from one subject to another are seldom remarkable for the exactness of their learning. No man loads his mind with small matters unless he has some very good reason for doing so.

His ignorance was as remarkable as his knowledge. Of contemporary literature, philosophy and politics he appeared to know next to nothing. When I quoted Thomas Carlyle, he asked in the naivest way who he might be and what he had done. My surprise reached a peak, however, when I found by chance that he was ignorant of the Copernican Theory and of the composition of the Solar System. That any civilized human being in this nineteenth century should not be aware that the earth travelled round the sun appeared to me such an extraordinary fact that I could hardly realize it.

"You appear to be astonished," he said, smiling at my expression of surprise. "Now that I do know it I shall do my best to forget it."

"To forget it!"

"You see," he explained, "I consider that a man's brain at first is like a little empty attic. You have to stock it with such furniture as you choose. A fool takes in all the lumber of every sort that he comes across. So the knowledge that might be useful to him gets crowded out. Or at best it is jumbled up with a lot of other things, so that he has a difficulty in laying his hands upon it. Now the skilful workman is very careful indeed as to what he takes into his brain-attic. He will have nothing but the tools which may help him in doing his work. But of these he has a large mixed set, and all in the most perfect order. It is a mistake to think that that little room has elastic walls and can stretch to any extent. Depend upon it, there comes a time when for every addition of knowledge you forget something that you knew before. It is of the highest importance, therefore, not to have useless facts pushing out the useful ones."

"But the Solar System!" I protested.

"What the deuce is it to me?" he interrupted impatiently. "You say that we go round the sun. If we went round the moon it would not make a pennyworth of difference to me or to my work."

I was on the point of asking him what that work might be. But something in his manner showed me that the question would be an unwelcome one. I thought over our short conversation, however, and tried to draw my deductions from it. He said that he would acquire no knowledge which did not bear upon his object. Therefore all the knowledge which he possessed was such as would be useful to him. I listed in my own mind all the various points on which he had shown me that he was exceptionally well-informed. I even took a pencil and jotted them down. I could not help smiling at the document when I had completed it. It ran in this way—

SHERLOCK HOLMES—his limits.

1. Knowledge of Literature.—Nothing. 2. Philosophy.—Nothing. 3. Astronomy.—Nothing. 4. Politics.—Weak. 5. Botany.—Not fixed. Good at belladonna, opium, and poisons in general. Knows nothing of practical gardening. 6. Geology.—Practical, but limited. Tells different soils from each other at a glance. After walks he has shown me marks on his trousers, and told me by their colour and thickness in what part of London he had received them. 7. Chemistry.—Deep. 8. Anatomy.—Accurate, but not orderly. 9. Crime Literature.—Huge. He appears to know every detail of every horror carried out in the century. 10. Plays the violin well. 11. Is an expert singlestick player, boxer, and swordsman. 12. Has a good practical knowledge of British law.

When I had got so far in my list I threw it into the fire in despair. “If I can only find what the fellow is driving at by bringing all these skills together, and discovering a job which needs them all,” I said to myself, “I may as well give up the attempt at once.”

I see that I have mentioned above his powers upon the violin. These were very remarkable, but as strange as all his other skills. That he could play pieces, and difficult pieces, I knew well, because at my request he has played me some of Mendelssohn’s Lieder, and other favourites. When left to himself, however, he would seldom produce any music or attempt any known air. Leaning back in his arm-chair of an evening, he would close his eyes and scrape carelessly at the fiddle which was thrown across his knee. Sometimes the chords were deep and sad. Occasionally they were strange and happy. Clearly they reflected the thoughts which possessed him, but whether the music aided those thoughts, or whether the playing was simply the result of a whim or fancy was more than I could determine. I might have rebelled against these irritating solos had it not been that he usually ended them by playing in quick succession a whole series of my favourite airs as a small compensation for the trial upon my patience.

During the first week or so we had no callers, and I had begun to think that my companion was as friendless a man as I was myself. Presently, however, I found that he had many acquaintances, and those in the most different classes of society. There was one little sallow rat-faced, dark-eyed fellow who was introduced to me as Mr. Lestrade, and who came three or four times in a single week. One morning a young girl called, fashionably dressed, and stayed for half an hour or more. The same afternoon brought a grey-headed, seedy visitor, looking like a Jew pedlar, who appeared to me to be much excited, and who was closely followed by a slip-shod elderly woman. On another occasion an old white-haired gentleman had an interview with my companion; and on another a railway porter in his velveteen uniform. When any of these strange individuals put in an appearance, Sherlock Holmes used to beg for the use of the sitting-room, and I would retire to my bed-room. He always apologized to me for putting me to this inconvenience. “I have to use this room as a place of business,” he said, “and these people are my clients.” Again I had an opportunity of asking him a point blank question, and again my delicacy prevented me from forcing another man to confide in me. I imagined at the time that he had some strong reason for not mentioning it, but he soon dispelled the idea by coming round to the subject of his own accord.

It was upon the 4th of March, as I have good reason to remember, that I rose somewhat earlier than usual, and found that Sherlock Holmes had not yet finished his breakfast. The landlady had become so accustomed to my late habits that my place had not been laid nor my coffee prepared. With the unreasonable petulance of mankind I rang the bell and gave a curt intimation that I was ready. Then I picked up a magazine from the table and attempted to while away the time with it, while my companion munched silently at his toast. One of the articles had a pencil mark at the heading, and I naturally began to run my eye through it.

Its somewhat ambitious title was “The Book of Life,” and it attempted to show how much an observant man might learn by an accurate and systematic examination of all that came in his way. It struck me as being a remarkable mixture of shrewdness and of absurdity. The reasoning was close and intense, but the deductions appeared to me to be far-fetched and exaggerated. The writer claimed by a momentary expression, a twitch of a muscle or a glance of an eye, to fathom a man’s inmost thoughts. Deceit, according to him, was an impossibility in the case of one trained to observation and analysis. His conclusions were as infallible as so many propositions of Euclid. So startling would his results appear to the uninitiated that until they learned the processes by which he had arrived at them they might well consider him as a necromancer.

“From a drop of water,” said the writer, “a logician could infer the possibility of an Atlantic or a Niagara without having seen or heard of one or the other. So all life is a great chain, the nature of which is known whenever we are shown a single link of it. Like all other arts, the Science of Deduction and Analysis is one which can only be acquired by long and patient study nor, is life long enough to allow any mortal to attain the highest possible perfection in it. Before turning to those moral and mental aspects of the matter which present the greatest difficulties, let the enquirer begin by mastering more elementary problems. Let him, on meeting a fellow-mortal, learn at a glance to distinguish the history of the man, and the trade or profession to which he belongs. Puerile as such an exercise may seem, it sharpens the faculties of observation, and teaches one where to look and what to look for. By a man’s finger nails, by his coat-sleeve, by his boot, by his trouser knees, by the callosities of his forefinger and thumb, by his expression, by his shirt cuffs—by each of these things a man’s calling is plainly revealed. That all united should fail to enlighten the competent enquirer in any case is almost inconceivable.”

“What ineffable twaddle!” I cried, slapping the magazine down on the table, “I never read such rubbish in my life.”

“What is it?” asked Sherlock Holmes.

"Why, this article," I said. I pointed at it with my egg spoon as I sat down to my breakfast. "I see that you have read it, because you have marked it. I do not deny that it is written with skill. It makes me angry, though. It is clearly the theory of some idle person in a comfortable chair. He makes up all these neat little strange ideas alone in his study. It is not practical. I would like to see him put down in a third class carriage on the Underground. Then someone should ask him to give the jobs of all the other travellers. I would bet a thousand to one against him."

"You would lose your money," Sherlock Holmes said quietly. "As for the article, I wrote it myself."

"You!"

"Yes, I have a skill for both watching and for reasoning. The theories I have expressed there seem to you to be so strange. But they are really extremely practical. So practical that I depend on them for my bread and cheese."

"And how?" I asked without thinking.

"Well, I have a job of my own. I suppose I am the only one in the world. I am a consulting detective, if you can understand what that is. Here in London we have lots of Government detectives and lots of private ones. When these men are stuck, they come to me, and I manage to put them on the right track. They lay all the evidence before me. I am usually able, with the help of my knowledge of the history of crime, to set them straight. There is a strong family resemblance about crimes. If you have all the details of a thousand at your finger ends, it is strange if you cannot solve the thousand and first. Lestrade is a well-known detective. He got himself into a fog recently over a forgery case, and that was what brought him here."

"And these other people?"

"They are mostly sent on by private inquiry agencies. They are all people who are in trouble about something, and want a little enlightening. I listen to their story, they listen to my comments, and then I pocket my fee."

"But do you mean to say," I said, "that without leaving your room you can solve some knot which other men can make nothing of? Although they have seen every detail for themselves?"

"Quite so. I have a kind of intuition that way. Now and again a case turns up which is a little more complex. Then I have to move about and see things with my own eyes. You see I have a lot of special knowledge which I apply to the problem. That knowledge makes matters wonderfully easy. Those rules of deduction laid down in that article which aroused your scorn are invaluable to me in practical work. Observation with me is second nature. You appeared to be surprised when I told you, on our first meeting, that you had come from Afghanistan."

"You were told, no doubt."

"Nothing of the sort. I _knew_ you came from Afghanistan. From long habit the train of thoughts ran so swiftly through my mind that I arrived at the conclusion without being conscious of intermediate steps. There were such steps, however. The train of reasoning ran, 'Here is a gentleman of a medical type, but with the air of a military man. Clearly an army doctor, then. He has just come from the tropics, for his face is dark. That is not the natural tint of his skin, for his wrists are fair. He has undergone hardship and sickness, as his haggard face says clearly. His left arm has been injured. He holds it in a stiff and unnatural manner. Where in the tropics could an English army doctor have seen much hardship and got his arm wounded? Clearly in Afghanistan.' The whole train of thought did not occupy a second. I then remarked that you came from Afghanistan, and you were astonished."

"It is simple enough as you explain it," I said, smiling. "You remind me of Edgar Allen Poe's Dupin. I had no idea that such individuals did exist outside of stories."

Sherlock Holmes rose and lit his pipe. "No doubt you think that you are complimenting me in comparing me to Dupin," he observed. "Now, in my opinion, Dupin was a very inferior fellow. That trick of his of breaking in on his friends' thoughts with an apropos remark after a quarter of an hour's silence is really very showy and superficial. He had some analytical genius, no doubt. But he was by no means such a phenomenon as Poe appeared to imagine."

"Have you read Gaboriau's works?" I asked. "Does Lecoq come up to your idea of a detective?"

Sherlock Holmes sniffed sardonically. "Lecoq was a miserable bungler," he said, in an angry voice. "He had only one thing to recommend him, and that was his energy. That book made me positively ill. The question was how to identify an unknown prisoner. I could have done it in twenty-four hours. Lecoq took six months or so. It might be made a text-book for detectives to teach them what to avoid."

I felt rather indignant at having two characters whom I had admired treated in this cavalier style. I walked over to the window, and stood looking out into the busy street. "This fellow may be very clever," I said to myself, "but he is certainly very conceited."

"There are no crimes and no criminals in these days," he said, querulously. "What is the use of having brains in our profession. I know well that I have it in me to make my name famous. No man lives or has ever lived who has brought the same amount of study and of natural talent to the detection of crime which I have done. And what is the result? There is no crime to detect, or, at most, some bungling villainy with a motive so transparent that even a Scotland Yard official can see through it."

I was still annoyed at his bumptious style of conversation. I thought it best to change the topic.

"I wonder what that fellow is looking for?" I asked. I pointed to a stalwart, plainly-dressed individual who was walking slowly down the other side of the street. He was looking anxiously at the numbers. He had a large blue envelope in his hand, and was clearly the bearer of a message.

"You mean the retired sergeant of Marines," said Sherlock Holmes.

"Brag and bounce!" thought I to myself. "He knows that I cannot verify his guess."

The thought had hardly passed through my mind when the man whom we were watching caught sight of the number on our door. He ran rapidly across the roadway. We heard a loud knock, a deep voice below, and heavy steps ascending the stair.

"For Mr. Sherlock Holmes," he said. He stepped into the room and handed my friend the letter.

Here was an opportunity of taking the conceit out of him. He little thought of this when he made that random shot. "May I ask, my lad," I said, in the blandest voice, "what your trade may be?"

"Commissionaire, sir," he said, in a rough voice. "Uniform away for repairs."

"And you were?" I asked. I gave my companion a slightly mean look.

"A sergeant, sir, Royal Marine Light Infantry, sir. No answer? Right, sir."

He clicked his heels together. He raised his hand to his head in a greeting, and was gone.

CHAPTER 3. THE LAURISTON GARDENS MYSTERY

THE LAURISTON GARDENS MYSTERY

I must say I was quite surprised by this new proof of how practical my friend's theories were. My respect for his power of analysis grew a lot. But some hidden doubt still stayed in my mind. I wondered if the whole thing was a scene arranged in advance, meant to impress me. Yet I could not understand what possible reason he could have for fooling me. When I looked at him, he had finished reading the note. His eyes had taken on the empty, dull look that showed he was deep in thought.

"How in the world did you work that out?" I asked.

"Work out what?" he said, with some annoyance.

"Why, that he was a retired sergeant of Marines."

"I have no time for small things," he answered, shortly. Then with a smile, "Excuse my bad manners. You broke my chain of thought. But perhaps it is just as well. So you really were not able to see that that man was a sergeant of Marines?"

"No, indeed."

"It was easier to know it than to explain why I knew it. If you were asked to prove that two and two made four, you might find some difficulty. And yet you are quite sure of the fact. Even across the street I could see a great blue anchor drawn on the back of the man's hand. That suggested the sea. He had a military way of carrying himself, however, and regulation side hair on his face. There we have the marine. He was a man with some amount of self-importance and a certain air of command. You must have noticed the way in which he held his head and swung his cane. A steady, worthy, middle-aged man, too, from his appearance—all facts which led me to believe that he had been a sergeant."

"Wonderful!" I cried out.

"Ordinary," said Holmes, though I thought from his expression that he was pleased at my clear surprise and respect. "I said just now that there were no criminals. It appears that I am wrong—look at this!" He threw me the note which the door attendant had brought.

"Why," I cried, as I looked it over, "this is terrible!"

"It does seem to be a little out of the common," he remarked, quietly. "Would you mind reading it to me aloud?"

This is the letter which I read to him—

"MY DEAR MR. SHERLOCK HOLMES,—

"There has been a bad business during the night at 3, Lauriston Gardens, off the Brixton Road. Our man on the beat saw a light there about two in the morning. As the house was an empty one, he suspected that something was wrong. He found the door open. In the front room, which is bare of furniture, he discovered the body of a gentleman, well dressed, with cards in his pocket bearing the name of 'Enoch J. Drebber, Cleveland, Ohio, U.S.A.' There had been no theft, nor is there any evidence as to how the man met his death. There are marks of blood in the room, but there is no wound upon his person. We are at a loss as to how he came into the empty house. Indeed, the whole affair is a puzzle. If you can come round to the house any time before twelve, you will find me there. I have left everything as it was until I hear from you. If you are unable to come I shall give you fuller details. I would value it as a great favour if you would give me your opinion.

Yours truly, "TOBIAS GREGSON."

"Gregson is the smartest of the Scotland Yard men," my friend remarked. "He and Lestrade are the best of a bad group. They are both quick and full of energy, but ordinary—shockingly so. They have their knives into one another, too. They are as envious as a pair of professional beauties. There will be some fun over this case if they are both put on the trail."

I was surprised at the calm way in which he went on. "Surely there is not a moment to be lost," I cried. "Shall I go and order you a cab?"

"I'm not sure about whether I shall go. I am the most hopelessly idle devil that ever stood in shoe leather—that is, when the mood is on me, for I can be quick enough at times."

"Why, it is just such a chance as you have been longing for."

"My dear fellow, what does it matter to me. Supposing I solve the whole matter, you may be sure that Gregson, Lestrade, and Co. will take all the credit. That comes of being an unofficial person."

"But he begs you to help him."

"Yes. He knows that I am his superior, and admits it to me. But he would cut his tongue out before he would admit it to any third person. However, we may as well go and have a look. I shall work it out on my own. I may have a laugh at them if I have nothing else. Come on!"

He pulled on his heavy coat, and moved about in a way that showed a burst of energy had replaced the dull mood.

"Get your hat," he said.

"You wish me to come?"

"Yes, if you have nothing better to do." A minute later we were both in a horse-drawn cab, driving fast for the Brixton Road.

It was a misty, grey morning. A dull-coloured cover hung over the house-tops, looking like the reflection of the mud-coloured streets beneath. My friend was in the best of spirits, and talked on about Cremona violins, and the difference between a Stradivarius and an Amati. As for myself, I was silent, for the dull weather and the sad business we were engaged in lowered my spirits.

"You do not seem to give much thought to the matter in hand," I said at last, interrupting Holmes' talk about music.

"No facts yet," he answered. "It is a great mistake to form a theory before you have all the evidence. It affects your judgment."

"You will have your facts soon," I remarked, pointing with my finger. "This is the Brixton Road, and that is the house, if I am not very much mistaken."

"So it is. Stop, driver, stop!" We were still a hundred yards or so from it, but he insisted upon our getting out, and we finished our journey on foot.

Number 3, Lauriston Gardens had a bad and threatening look. It was one of four houses that stood back a little from the street. Two were occupied and two were empty. The empty ones looked out with three rows of empty, sad windows. They were blank and sad, except that here and there a "To Let" card had appeared like a cloud over the dirty glass. A small garden with a few weak plants separated each of these houses from the street. A narrow path went through it, yellow in color, and made apparently of a mix of clay and gravel. The whole place was very wet from the rain that had fallen through the night. The garden was surrounded by a low brick wall, three feet high, with a line of wood rails on top. Against this wall leaned a strong police officer, surrounded by a small group of men with nothing to do. They stretched their necks and strained their eyes in the useless hope of catching some glimpse of what was happening inside.

I had imagined that Sherlock Holmes would at once have hurried into the house and plunged into a study of the mystery. Nothing seemed further from his intention. With an air of calm lack of interest which, under the circumstances, seemed to me to border upon a false show, he walked slowly up and down the sidewalk. He stared without expression at the ground, the sky, the opposite houses and the line of rails. Having finished his look, he went slowly down the path, or rather down the edge of grass which ran beside the path, keeping his eyes fixed upon the ground. Twice he stopped, and once I saw him smile, and heard him give a cry of satisfaction. There were many marks of footsteps upon the wet clay soil. But since the police had been coming and going over it, I was unable to see how my companion could hope to learn anything from it. Still I had had such extraordinary proof of the quickness of his sharp mind, that I had no doubt that he could see a great deal which was hidden from me.

At the door of the house we were met by a tall, white-faced man with light yellow hair, with a notebook in his hand. He rushed forward and shook my companion's hand with warmth. "It is indeed kind of you to come," he said, "I have had everything left as it was."

"Except that!" my friend answered, pointing at the path. "If a herd of buffalo had passed along there could not be a greater mess. No doubt, however, you had drawn your own conclusions, Gregson, before you permitted this."

"I have had so much to do inside the house," the detective said, avoiding the question. "My colleague, Mr. Lestrade, is here. I had relied upon him to look after this."

Holmes glanced at me and raised his eyebrows in a mocking way. "With two such men as yourself and Lestrade upon the ground, there will not be much for a third party to find out," he said.

Gregson rubbed his hands in a self-satisfied way. "I think we have done all that can be done," he answered; "it is a strange case though, and I knew your taste for such things."

"You did not come here in a cab?" asked Sherlock Holmes.

"No, sir."

"Nor Lestrade?"

"No, sir."

"Then let us go and look at the room." With this odd remark he walked on into the house, followed by Gregson, whose face showed his surprise.

A short hallway, bare boards and dusty, led to the kitchen and offices. Two doors opened out of it to the left and to the right. One of these had obviously been closed for many weeks. The other belonged to the dining-room, which was the room in which the mysterious affair had occurred. Holmes walked in, and I followed him with that low feeling in my heart which the presence of death brings.

It was a large square room, looking all the larger from the absence of all furniture. A common bright wallpaper decorated the walls, but it was stained in places with mold, and here and there great strips had come loose and hung down, showing the yellow plaster beneath. Opposite the door was a showy fireplace, topped by a mantelpiece of fake white marble. On one corner of this was stuck the stump of a red wax candle. The single window was so dirty that the light was hazy and uncertain, giving a dull grey tinge to everything. This was made stronger by the thick layer of dust which covered the whole room.

All these details I observed afterwards. At present my attention was fixed upon the single grim motionless figure which lay stretched upon the boards, with empty sightless eyes staring up at the discolored ceiling. It was that of a man about forty-three or forty-four years of age, middle-sized, broad shouldered, with crisp curling black hair, and a short stubbly beard. He was dressed in a heavy broadcloth frock coat and waistcoat, with light-colored trousers, and spotless collar and cuffs. A top hat, well brushed and neat, was placed upon the floor beside him. His hands were clenched and his arms thrown wide, while his lower limbs were locked together as though his death struggle had been a terrible one. On his rigid face there stood an expression of horror, and as it seemed to me, of hatred, such as I have never seen upon human features. This evil and terrible twist, combined with the low forehead, blunt nose, and sticking-out jaw gave the dead man a strangely monkey-like appearance. This was increased by his twisting, unnatural posture. I have seen death in many forms, but never has it appeared to me in a more fearsome way than in that dark dirty room, which looked out upon one of the main roads of suburban London.

Lestrade, lean and ferret-like as ever, was standing by the doorway, and greeted my companion and myself.

"This case will make a stir, sir," he remarked. "It beats anything I have seen, and I am no chicken."

"There is no clue?" said Gregson.

"None at all," joined in Lestrade.

Sherlock Holmes approached the body, and, kneeling down, examined it closely. "You are sure that there is no wound?" he asked, pointing to numerous spots and splashes of blood which lay all round.

"Positive!" cried both detectives.

"Then, of course, this blood belongs to a second person—probably the murderer, if murder has been committed. It reminds me of the circumstances around the death of Van Jansen, in Utrecht, in the year '34. Do you remember the case, Gregson?"

"No, sir."

"Read it up—you really should. There is nothing new under the sun. It has all been done before."

As he spoke, his quick fingers were flying here, there, and everywhere, feeling, pressing, unbuttoning, examining. His eyes wore the same far-away expression which I have already remarked upon. So swiftly was the examination made, that one would hardly have guessed how careful it was. Finally, he sniffed the dead man's lips, and then glanced at the soles of his shiny leather boots.

“He has not been moved at all?” he asked.

“No more than was necessary for the purposes of our examination.”

“You can take him to the mortuary now,” he said. “There is nothing more to be learned.”

Gregson had a stretcher and four men at hand. At his call they entered the room. The stranger was lifted and carried out. As they raised him, a ring tinkled down and rolled across the floor. Lestrade grabbed it up. He stared at it with mystified eyes.

“There’s been a woman here,” he cried. “It’s a woman’s wedding-ring.”

He held it out on the palm of his hand as he spoke. We all gathered round him and gazed at it. There could be no doubt that this circle of plain gold had once been on the finger of a bride.

“This complicates matters,” said Gregson. “Heaven knows, they were complicated enough before.”

“You’re sure it doesn’t simplify them?” observed Holmes. “There’s nothing to be learned by staring at it. What did you find in his pockets?”

“We have it all here,” said Gregson. He pointed to a pile of objects on one of the bottom steps of the stairs. “A gold watch, No. 97163, by Barraud, of London. Gold Albert chain, very heavy and solid. Gold ring, with masonic device. Gold pin—bull-dog’s head, with rubies as eyes. Russian leather card-case, with cards of Enoch J. Drebber of Cleveland. The letters E. J. D. are on the linen. No purse, but loose money to the amount of seven pounds thirteen. Pocket edition of Boccaccio’s _Decameron_, with the name of Joseph Stangerson on the fly-leaf. Two letters—one addressed to E. J. Drebber and one to Joseph Stangerson.”

“At what address?”

“American Exchange, Strand—to be left till called for. They are both from the Guion Steamship Company. They refer to the sailing of their boats from Liverpool. It is clear that this unfortunate man was about to return to New York.”

“Have you made any inquiries about this man, Stangerson?”

“I did it at once, sir,” said Gregson. “I have had advertisements sent to all the newspapers. One of my men has gone to the American Exchange, but he has not returned yet.”

“Have you sent to Cleveland?”

“We telegraphed this morning.”

“How did you word your inquiries?”

“We simply detailed the circumstances. We said that we should be glad of any information which could help us.”

“You did not ask for particulars on any point which appeared to you to be crucial?”

“I asked about Stangerson.”

“Nothing else? Is there no circumstance on which this whole case appears to hinge? Will you not telegraph again?”

“I have said all I have to say,” said Gregson, in an offended voice.

Sherlock Holmes chuckled to himself. He appeared to be about to make some remark. Then Lestrade reappeared upon the scene. He had been in the front room while we were holding this conversation in the hall. He was rubbing his hands in a pompous and self-satisfied manner.

“Mr. Gregson,” he said, “I have just made a discovery of the highest importance. It would have been overlooked had I not made a careful examination of the walls.”

The little man’s eyes sparkled as he spoke. He was evidently in a state of suppressed exultation at having scored a point against his colleague.

“Come here,” he said. He bustled back into the room. The atmosphere of the room felt clearer since the removal of its ghastly inmate. “Now, stand there!”

He struck a match on his boot and held it up against the wall.

“Look at that!” he said, triumphantly.

I have remarked that the paper had fallen away in parts. In this particular corner of the room a large piece had peeled off. This left a yellow square of coarse plastering. Across this bare space there was scrawled in blood-red letters a single word—

RACHE.

“What do you think of that?” cried the detective. He had the air of a showman exhibiting his show. “This was overlooked because it was in the darkest corner of the room. No one thought of looking there. The murderer has written it with his or her own blood. See this smear where it has trickled down the wall! That disposes of the idea of suicide anyhow. Why was that corner chosen to write it on? I will tell you. See that candle on the mantelpiece. It was lit at the time. If it was lit, this corner would be the brightest instead of the darkest portion of the wall.”

“And what does it mean now that you _have_ found it?” asked Gregson in a depreciatory voice.

“Mean? Why, it means that the writer was going to put the female name Rachel. But he or she was disturbed before there was time to finish. You mark my words. When this case comes to be cleared up, you will find that a woman named Rachel has something to do with it. It’s all very well for you to laugh, Mr. Sherlock Holmes. You may be very smart and clever. But the old hound is the best, when all is said and done.”

“I really beg your pardon!” said my companion. He had ruffled the little man’s temper by bursting into an explosion of laughter. “You certainly have the credit of being the first of us to find this out. As you say, it bears every mark of having been written by the other participant in last night’s mystery. I have not had time to examine this room yet. But with your permission I shall do so now.”

As he spoke, he whipped a tape measure and a large round magnifying glass from his pocket. With these two tools he trotted noiselessly about the room. Sometimes he stopped. Occasionally he knelt. Once he lay flat upon his face. He was so engrossed with his occupation that he appeared to have forgotten our presence. He chattered away to himself under his breath the whole time. He kept up a running fire of exclamations, groans, whistles, and little cries suggestive of encouragement and of hope. As I watched him I was irresistibly reminded of a pure-blooded well-trained foxhound. It dashes backwards and forwards through the covert, whining in its eagerness, until it comes across the lost scent. For twenty minutes or more he continued his researches. He measured with the most exact care the distance between marks which were entirely invisible to me. Occasionally he applied his tape to the walls in an equally incomprehensible manner. In one place he gathered up very carefully a little pile of grey dust from the floor. He packed it away in an envelope. Finally, he examined with his glass the word upon the wall. He went over every letter of it with the most minute exactness. This done, he appeared to be satisfied. He replaced his tape and his glass in his pocket.

“They say that genius is an infinite capacity for taking pains,” he remarked with a smile. “It’s a very bad definition, but it does apply to detective work.”

Gregson and Lestrade had watched the movements of their amateur companion with great curiosity and some dislike. They clearly failed to see the fact that I had begun to understand. Every small action of Sherlock Holmes was aimed at some clear and practical goal.

"What do you think of it, sir?" they both asked.

"It would be taking the credit of the case away from you if I dared to help you," said my friend. "You are doing so well now that it would be a shame for anyone to interfere." There was deep mockery in his voice as he spoke. "If you will let me know how your investigations go," he continued, "I shall be happy to give you any help I can. For now I would like to speak to the police officer who found the body. Can you give me his name and address?"

Lestrade looked at his notebook. "John Rance," he said. "He is off duty now. You will find him at 46, Audley Court, Kennington Park Gate."

Holmes wrote down the address.

"Come along, Doctor," he said. "We shall go and find him. I will tell you one thing that may help you in the case," he continued, turning to the two detectives. "A murder has been done, and the murderer was a man. He was more than six feet tall, was in the best years of his life, and had small feet for his height. He wore rough boots with square toes and smoked a Trichinopoly cigar. He came here with his victim in a four-wheeled cab. The cab was pulled by a horse with three old shoes and one new one on his far front leg. Most likely the murderer had a red face, and the finger-nails of his right hand were very long. These are only a few signs, but they may help you."

Lestrade and Gregson looked at each other with a smile of disbelief.

"If this man was murdered, how was it done?" asked the first.

"Poison," said Sherlock Holmes shortly, and walked off. "One other thing, Lestrade," he added, turning round at the door. "'Rache' is the German word for 'revenge'. So do not waste your time looking for Miss Rachel."

With that parting shot he walked away, leaving the two rivals with their mouths open behind him.

CHAPTER 4. A FLIGHT FOR LIFE.

A FLIGHT FOR LIFE.

On the morning after his talk with the Mormon Prophet, John Ferrier went into Salt Lake City. He found his friend, who was going to the Nevada Mountains. He gave him his message for Jefferson Hope. In it he told the young man about the great danger that threatened them. He also told him how necessary it was that he should return. After this he felt easier in his mind. He went home with a lighter heart.

As he came near his farm, he was surprised to see a horse tied to each post of the gate. He was even more surprised when he entered. He found two young men in his sitting-room. One had a long pale face. He was leaning back in the rocking-chair, with his feet up on the stove. The other was a young man with a thick neck and a rough, swollen face. He stood in front of the window with his hands in his pockets, whistling a popular hymn. Both of them nodded to Ferrier as he entered. The one in the rocking-chair began the conversation.

“Maybe you don’t know us,” he said. “This is the son of Elder Drebber. I’m Joseph Stangerson. I travelled with you in the desert when the Lord reached out His hand and gathered you into the true fold.”

“As He will all the nations in His own good time,” said the other in a voice through his nose. “He works slowly but very finely.”

John Ferrier bowed coldly. He had guessed who his visitors were.

“We have come,” continued Stangerson, “on the advice of our fathers. We want to ask for the hand of your daughter. She may choose whichever of us seems good to you and to her. I have only four wives, and Brother Drebber here has seven. So it seems to me that my claim is the stronger one.”

“No, no, Brother Stangerson,” cried the other. “The question is not how many wives we have, but how many we can keep. My father has now given his mills to me, and I am the richer man.”

“But my future is better,” said the other warmly. “When the Lord takes my father, I shall have his tanning yard and his leather factory. Then I am your elder, and I am higher in the Church.”

“It will be for the girl to decide,” answered young Drebber. He smiled at his own reflection in the glass. “We will leave it all to her decision.”

During this talk, John Ferrier had stood in the doorway, burning with anger. He could hardly keep his riding-whip from the backs of his two visitors.

“Look here,” he said at last, walking up to them, “when my daughter calls you, you can come. But until then I don’t want to see your faces again.”

The two young Mormons stared at him in great surprise. In their eyes, this competition between them for the girl’s hand was the highest honour for her and for her father.

“There are two ways out of the room,” cried Ferrier. “There is the door, and there is the window. Which do you want to use?”

His brown face looked so fierce, and his thin hands so threatening, that his visitors jumped to their feet and left in a hurry. The old farmer followed them to the door.

“Let me know when you have decided which one it is to be,” he said with a bitter smile.

“You shall pay for this!” Stangerson cried, white with anger. “You have defied the Prophet and the Council of Four. You shall regret it to the end of your days.”

“The hand of the Lord shall be heavy upon you,” cried young Drebber. “He will rise and strike you!”

“Then I’ll start the striking,” shouted Ferrier furiously. He would have rushed upstairs for his gun, but Lucy seized him by the arm and held him back. Before he could get free from her, the sound of horses’ feet told him that they were beyond his reach.

“The young hypocrites!” he exclaimed, wiping the sweat from his forehead. “I would sooner see you in your grave, my girl, than the wife of either of them.”

“And so would I, father,” she answered with spirit. “But Jefferson will soon be here.”

“Yes. It will not be long before he comes. The sooner the better, for we do not know what their next move may be.”

It was indeed high time that someone able to give advice and help should come to the aid of the strong old farmer and his adopted daughter. In the whole history of the settlement there had never been such a case of open disobedience to the authority of the Elders. If small errors were punished so sternly, what would be the fate of this great rebel? Ferrier knew that his wealth and position would be of no use to him. Others as well known and as rich as himself had been taken away before now, and their goods given to the Church. He was a brave man, but he trembled at the vague, shadowy terrors that hung over him. Any known danger he could face with a firm lip, but this waiting was unnerving. He hid his fears from his daughter, however. He pretended to make light of the whole matter, though she, with the keen eye of love, saw plainly that he was uneasy.

He expected that he would receive some message or warning from Young about his conduct. He was not mistaken, though it came in an unexpected way. When he rose next morning, he found, to his surprise, a small square of paper pinned to the coverlet of his bed just over his chest. On it were printed, in bold uneven letters:—

“Twenty-nine days are given you to change your ways, and then——”

The dash was more frightening than any threat could have been. How this warning came into his room puzzled John Ferrier greatly. His servants slept in an outhouse, and the doors and windows had all been locked. He crushed the paper up and said nothing to his daughter, but the incident struck a chill into his heart. The twenty-nine days were clearly the rest of the month that Young had promised. What strength or courage could help against an enemy armed with such mysterious powers? The hand that fastened that pin might have struck him to the heart, and he could never have known who had killed him.

He was even more shaken the next morning. They had sat down to breakfast when Lucy pointed upwards with a cry of surprise. In the centre of the ceiling was scrawled the number 28, apparently with a burned stick. To his daughter it was a mystery, and he did not explain it to her. That night he sat up with his gun and kept watch. He saw and heard nothing, and yet in the morning a great 27 had been painted on the outside of his door.

Day after day passed. Each morning he found that his hidden enemies had kept their record. They had marked in some easy-to-see place how many days were left to him out of the month of mercy. Sometimes the deadly numbers appeared on the walls. Sometimes they were on the floors. Now and then they were on small signs stuck on the garden gate or the fences. With all his watchfulness John Ferrier could not find where these daily warnings came from. A fear that was almost like a belief in magic came over him when he saw them. He became thin and pale and unable to rest. His eyes had the troubled look of a hunted animal. He had only one hope in life now. That was for the arrival of the young hunter from Nevada.

Twenty had changed to fifteen and fifteen to ten. But there was no news of the missing man. One by one the numbers went down. Still there came no sign of him. Whenever a rider came down the road with a loud noise, or a driver shouted at his team, the old farmer hurried to the gate. He thought that help had arrived at last. At last, when he saw five become four and that again become three, he lost heart. He gave up all hope of escape. Alone, and with his limited knowledge of the mountains around the settlement, he knew that he had no power. The roads with more people were closely watched and guarded. No one could pass along them without an order from the Council. Whichever way he turned, there seemed to be no way to avoid the blow that hung over him. Yet the old man never once doubted his decision to give up his own life before he agreed to what he saw as his daughter's shame.

He was sitting alone one evening. He was thinking deeply about his troubles and searching without success for some way out of them. That morning the number 2 had appeared on the wall of his house. The next day would be the last of the given time. What would happen then? All kinds of vague and terrible pictures filled his mind. And his daughter—what would become of her after he was gone? Was there no escape from the invisible net that was drawn all around them? He put his head down on the table and cried at the thought of his own helplessness.

What was that? In the silence he heard a gentle scratching sound. It was low, but very clear in the quiet of the night. It came from the door of the house. Ferrier crept into the hall and listened with great attention. There was a pause for a few moments. Then the low, quiet sound came again. Someone was clearly tapping very gently on one of the panels of the door. Was it some midnight killer who had come to carry out the murderous orders of the secret court? Or was it some agent who was marking that the last day of mercy had arrived? John Ferrier felt that instant death would be better than the waiting that shook his nerves and chilled his heart. He jumped forward, drew the bolt and threw the door open.

Outside all was calm and quiet. The night was fine, and the stars were shining brightly overhead. The little front garden lay before the farmer's eyes, enclosed by the fence and gate. But neither there nor on the road was any human being to be seen. With a sigh of relief, Ferrier looked to the right and to the left. Then he happened to look straight down at his own feet. To his great surprise he saw a man lying flat on his face on the ground, with arms and legs spread out in all directions.

He was so shaken by the sight that he leaned against the wall with his hand to his throat to hold back his wish to call out. His first thought was that the man on the ground was someone wounded or dying. But as he watched, he saw him twist along the ground and into the hall with the speed and silence of a snake. Once inside the house the man jumped to his feet, closed the door, and showed the surprised farmer the fierce face and firm expression of Jefferson Hope.

"Good God!" gasped John Ferrier. "How you scared me! Whatever made you come in like that?"

"Give me food," the other said, in a rough voice. "I have had no time for a bite or a drink for forty-eight hours." He threw himself on the cold meat and bread that were still lying on the table from his host's supper, and ate it hungrily. "Does Lucy bear up well?" he asked, when he had satisfied his hunger.

"Yes. She does not know the danger," her father answered.

"That is well. The house is watched on every side. That is why I crawled my way up to it. They may be very sharp, but they're not quite sharp enough to catch a Washoe hunter."

John Ferrier felt like a different man now that he realized he had a loyal ally. He seized the young man's leathery hand and shook it warmly. "You're a man to be proud of," he said. "There are not many who would come to share our danger and our troubles."

"You've hit it there, pard," the young hunter answered. "I have respect for you. But if you were alone in this business I'd think twice before I put my head into such a hornet's nest. It's Lucy that brings me here. And before harm comes to her I guess there will be one less of the Hope family in Utah."

"What are we to do?"

"Tomorrow is your last day. Unless you act tonight you are lost. I have a mule and two horses waiting in the Eagle Ravine. How much money have you?"

"Two thousand dollars in gold, and five in notes."

"That will do. I have as much more to add to it. We must push for Carson City through the mountains. You had best wake Lucy. It is as well that the servants do not sleep in the house."

While Ferrier was away, preparing his daughter for the coming journey, Jefferson Hope packed all the food he could find into a small parcel. He filled a stoneware jar with water, for he knew by experience that the mountain wells were few and far apart. He had hardly finished his arrangements before the farmer returned with his daughter, all dressed and ready to start. The greeting between the lovers was warm, but brief, for minutes were precious, and there was much to be done.

"We must make our start at once," said Jefferson Hope. He spoke in a low but firm voice, like one who realizes the greatness of the danger, but has made his heart strong to meet it. "The front and back entrances are watched. But with care we may get away through the side window and across the fields. Once on the road we are only two miles from the Ravine where the horses are waiting. By daybreak we should be halfway through the mountains."

“What if we are stopped?” asked Ferrier.

Hope hit the gun handle that stuck out from the front of his coat. “If there are too many of them for us, we shall take two or three of them with us,” he said with an evil smile.

The lights inside the house had all been put out. From the dark window Ferrier looked over the fields that had been his own. He was now about to leave them for ever. He had long prepared himself for this sacrifice. The thought of his daughter’s honor and happiness was stronger than any regret at his ruined fortunes. All looked so peaceful and happy—the moving trees and the broad, silent stretch of grain-land. It was hard to realize that the spirit of murder hid through it all. Yet the white face and firm expression of the young hunter showed that on his way to the house he had seen enough to satisfy him on that point.

Ferrier carried the bag of gold and notes. Jefferson Hope had the small amount of food and water. Lucy had a small bundle containing a few of her more valued possessions. Opening the window very slowly and carefully, they waited until a dark cloud had partly hidden the night. Then one by one they passed through into the little garden. With held breath and bent bodies they stumbled across it. They reached the shelter of the hedge and followed it until they came to the gap that opened into the cornfields. They had just reached this point when the young man seized his two companions and pulled them down into the shadow. There they lay silent and trembling.

It was as well that his prairie training had given Jefferson Hope very sharp ears. He and his friends had hardly bent down before the sad call of a mountain owl was heard within a few yards of them. Another call answered it at a small distance. At the same moment a vague, shadowy figure came out from the gap they had been heading for. It gave the sad signal cry again, and then a second man appeared out of the darkness.

“Tomorrow at midnight,” said the first, who seemed to be in authority. “When the Whip-poor-Will calls three times.”

“It is well,” returned the other. “Shall I tell Brother Drebber?”

“Pass it on to him, and from him to the others. Nine to seven!”

“Seven to five!” repeated the other, and the two figures moved quickly away in different directions. Their last words had clearly been some form of sign and password. The instant their steps had died away in the distance, Jefferson Hope sprang to his feet. He helped his companions through the gap and led the way across the fields as fast as he could. He supported and half-carried the girl when her strength seemed to fail her.

“Hurry on! hurry on!” he gasped from time to time. “We are through the line of guards. Everything depends on speed. Hurry on!”

Once on the high road they made rapid progress. Only once did they meet anyone, and then they managed to slip into a field and so avoid being recognized. Before reaching the town the hunter turned away into a rough and narrow path which led to the mountains. Two dark, jagged peaks rose above them through the darkness. The narrow pass that led between them was the Eagle Cañon, where the horses were waiting for them. With sure instinct Jefferson Hope picked his way among the great rocks and along the bed of a dried-up stream. At last he came to the hidden corner, screened with rocks, where the faithful animals had been tied. The girl was placed upon the mule, and old Ferrier upon one of the horses, with his money-bag. Jefferson Hope led the other along the steep and dangerous path.

It was a confusing route for anyone who was not used to facing Nature in her wildest moods. On one side a great rock rose up a thousand feet or more. It was black, stern, and threatening, with long stone columns upon its rough surface like the ribs of some stone monster. On the other hand a wild chaos of rocks and debris made all advance impossible. Between the two ran the uneven track. In places it was so narrow that they had to travel in single file. It was so rough that only skilled riders could have traveled it at all. Yet in spite of all dangers and difficulties, the hearts of the fugitives were light. Every step increased the distance between them and the terrible tyranny from which they were running away.

They soon had a proof, however, that they were still within the power of the Saints. They had reached the very wildest and most empty portion of the pass when the girl gave a startled cry and pointed upwards. On a rock which overlooked the track, dark and plain against the sky, there stood a single guard. He saw them as soon as they saw him. His military challenge of “Who goes there?” rang through the silent valley.

“Travelers for Nevada,” said Jefferson Hope, with his hand upon the rifle which hung by his saddle.

They could see the lonely guard touching his gun and looking down at them as if not satisfied with their answer.

“By whose permission?” he asked.

“The Holy Four,” answered Ferrier. His Mormon experiences had taught him that this was the highest authority he could name.

“Nine from seven,” cried the guard.

“Seven from five,” returned Jefferson Hope promptly, remembering the password he had heard in the garden.

“Pass, and the Lord go with you,” said the voice from above. Beyond his post the path widened, and the horses were able to break into a trot. Looking back, they could see the lonely guard leaning upon his gun. They knew that they had passed the outer post of the chosen people, and that freedom lay before them.

CHAPTER 5. THE AVENGING ANGELS.

THE AVENGING ANGELS.

All night they traveled through winding narrow valleys and over rough paths covered with rocks. More than once they lost their way. But Hope knew the mountains very well, and this helped them find the track again. When morning came, a scene of wonderful but wild beauty lay before them. In every direction great snow-topped peaks closed them in. These peaks seemed to look over each other's shoulders to the far horizon. The rocky banks on either side of them were so steep that the larch and the pine trees seemed to hang over their heads. It seemed that only one gust of wind was needed to send them crashing down on them. Nor was this fear entirely imagined. The empty valley was thickly covered with trees and large rocks which had fallen in a similar way. Even as they passed, a great rock came crashing down with a rough, loud rattle. This woke the echoes in the silent deep valleys and startled the tired horses into a gallop.

As the sun rose slowly above the eastern horizon, the tops of the great mountains lit up one after the other, like lamps at a festival. Soon they were all red and glowing. The magnificent sight cheered the hearts of the three fleeing people and gave them fresh energy. At a wild rushing stream which swept out of a narrow valley they stopped and watered their horses. They also ate a quick breakfast. Lucy and her father would gladly have rested longer, but Jefferson Hope would not allow it. "They will be on our track by this time," he said. "Everything depends on our speed. Once we are safe in Carson, we may rest for the rest of our lives."

During the whole of that day they struggled on through the narrow valleys. By evening they calculated that they were more than thirty miles from their enemies. At night they chose the base of a hanging rock, where the rocks offered some protection from the cold wind. There they crowded together for warmth and enjoyed a few hours' sleep. Before daybreak, however, they were up and on their way once more. They had seen no signs of any pursuers. Jefferson Hope began to think that they were safely out of the reach of the terrible organization whose hatred they had caused. He little knew how far that iron grasp could reach, or how soon it was to close upon them and crush them.

About the middle of the second day of their flight their small store of food began to run out. This gave the hunter little worry, however. There was game to be had among the mountains, and he had often before had to depend on his rifle for the needs of life. He chose a sheltered corner and piled together a few dried branches. He made a bright fire so that his companions might warm themselves. They were now nearly five thousand feet above sea level, and the air was bitter and sharp. He tied the horses and said goodbye to Lucy. Then he threw his gun over his shoulder and set out in search of whatever chance might bring his way. Looking back, he saw the old man and the young girl bending over the bright fire, while the three animals stood motionless in the background. Then the rocks between them hid them from his view.

He walked for a couple of miles through one narrow valley after another without success. From the marks on the bark of the trees and other signs, he judged that there were many bears nearby. At last, after two or three hours of fruitless search, he was thinking of turning back in despair. Then he looked up and saw a sight which sent a thrill of pleasure through his heart. On the edge of a jutting peak, three or four hundred feet above him, stood a creature somewhat like a sheep in appearance. But it had a pair of gigantic horns. The big-horn—for so it is called—was probably acting as a guardian over a flock which the hunter could not see. Fortunately it was heading in the opposite direction and had not seen him. Lying on his face, he rested his rifle on a rock and took a long, steady aim before pulling the trigger. The animal sprang into the air, staggered for a moment on the edge of the cliff, and then came crashing down into the valley below.

The creature was too heavy to lift, so the hunter contented himself with cutting away one leg and part of the side. With this prize over his shoulder, he hurried to retrace his steps, for evening was already coming on. He had hardly started, however, before he realized the difficulty which faced him. In his eagerness he had wandered far past the valleys which were known to him. It was no easy matter to pick out the path which he had taken. The valley in which he found himself divided and sub-divided into many deep narrow valleys. These were so like each other that it was impossible to tell one from the other. He followed one for a mile or more until he came to a mountain stream which he was sure he had never seen before. Convinced that he had taken the wrong turn, he tried another, but with the same result. Night was coming on rapidly, and it was almost dark before he at last found himself in a narrow valley which was familiar to him. Even then it was no easy matter to keep to the right track. The moon had not yet risen, and the high cliffs on either side made the darkness deeper. Weighed down with his burden and tired from his efforts, he stumbled along. He kept up his heart by thinking that every step brought him nearer to Lucy, and that he carried with him enough to ensure them food for the rest of their journey.

He had now come to the mouth of the very narrow valley in which he had left them. Even in the darkness he could recognize the outline of the cliffs which bounded it. They must, he thought, be waiting for him anxiously, for he had been absent nearly five hours. In the gladness of his heart he put his hands to his mouth and made the valley echo to a loud halloo as a signal that he was coming. He paused and listened for an answer. None came except his own cry. It clattered up the dreary silent valleys and was carried back to his ears in countless repetitions. Again he shouted, even louder than before. Again no whisper came back from the friends whom he had left such a short time ago. A vague, nameless dread came over him. He hurried onwards frantically, dropping the precious food in his agitation.

When he turned the corner, he came full in sight of the spot where the fire had been lit. There was still a glowing pile of wood ashes there. But clearly no one had tended it since he left. The same dead silence still ruled all around. His fears all changed to certainties, and he hurried on. There was no living creature near the remains of the fire. Animals, man, young woman—all were gone. It was only too clear that some sudden and terrible disaster had happened during his absence. It was a disaster that had taken them all, and yet had left no traces behind it.

Bewildered and stunned by this blow, Jefferson Hope felt his head spin round. He had to lean upon his rifle to save himself from falling. He was essentially a man of action, however. He quickly recovered from his temporary weakness. He seized a half-burned piece of wood from the smoking fire and blew it into a flame. Then he used it to examine the little camp. The ground was all stamped down by the feet of horses. This showed that a large party of mounted men had caught up with the runaways. The direction of their tracks proved that they had afterwards turned back to Salt Lake City. Had they carried back both of his companions with them? Jefferson Hope had almost persuaded himself that they must have done so. Then his eye fell upon an object that made every nerve of his body shake within him. A little way on one side of the camp was a low-lying pile of red soil. It had certainly not been there before. There was no mistaking it for anything but a newly-dug grave. As the young hunter approached it, he saw that a stick had been planted on it. A sheet of paper was stuck in the split fork of it. The writing upon the paper was brief, but to the point:

JOHN FERRIER, FORMERLY OF SALT LAKE CITY, Died August 4th, 1860.

The strong old man, whom he had left so short a time before, was gone, then. And this was all his grave writing. Jefferson Hope looked wildly round to see if there was a second grave. But there was no sign of one. Lucy had been carried back by their terrible followers to fulfil her original destiny. She was to become one of the group of wives of the Elder’s son. As the young fellow realized the certainty of her fate, and his own weakness to prevent it, he wished that he, too, was lying with the old farmer in his last silent resting-place.

Again, however, his active spirit shook off the tiredness that springs from despair. If there was nothing else left to him, he could at least devote his life to revenge. With firm patience and perseverance, Jefferson Hope also had a power of sustained desire for revenge. He may have learned it from the Indians among whom he had lived. As he stood by the empty fire, he felt that the only one thing that could ease his grief would be thorough and complete punishment. It must be brought by his own hand upon his enemies. His strong will and tireless energy should, he determined, be devoted to that one end. With a grim, white face, he went back to where he had dropped the food. He stirred up the smoking fire and cooked enough to last him for a few days. This he made up into a pack. Tired as he was, he set himself to walk back through the mountains upon the track of the avenging angels.

For five days he worked with sore feet and tired through the narrow passes that he had already crossed on horseback. At night he threw himself down among the rocks and took a few hours of sleep. But before sunrise he was always well on his way. On the sixth day, he reached the Eagle Cañon, from which they had begun their unlucky flight. From there he could look down upon the home of the saints. Worn and exhausted, he leaned upon his rifle. He shook his thin hand fiercely at the silent, wide city beneath him. As he looked at it, he observed that there were flags in some of the main streets, and other signs of celebration. He was still wondering what this might mean when he heard the clatter of horse’s hoofs. He saw a mounted man riding towards him. As he approached, he recognized him as a Mormon named Cowper, to whom he had given help at different times. He therefore spoke to him when he got up to him, with the object of finding out what Lucy Ferrier’s fate had been.

“I am Jefferson Hope,” he said. “You remember me.”

The Mormon looked at him with open astonishment. Indeed, it was difficult to recognize in this ragged, untidy wanderer, with deathly white face and fierce, wild eyes, the neat young hunter of former days. Having, however, at last, satisfied himself as to who he was, the man’s surprise changed to shock.

“You are mad to come here,” he cried. “It is as much as my own life is worth to be seen talking with you. There is a warrant against you from the Holy Four for helping the Ferriers away.”

“I don’t fear them, or their warrant,” Hope said, earnestly. “You must know something of this matter, Cowper. I beg you by everything you hold dear to answer a few questions. We have always been friends. For God’s sake, don’t refuse to answer me.”

“What is it?” the Mormon asked uneasily. “Be quick. The very rocks have ears and the trees eyes.”

“What has become of Lucy Ferrier?”

“She was married yesterday to young Drebber. Hold up, man, hold up, you have no life left in you.”

“Don’t mind me,” said Hope faintly. He was white to the very lips, and had sunk down on the stone against which he had been leaning. “Married, you say?”

“Married yesterday—that’s what those flags are for on the Endowment House. There was some words between young Drebber and young Stangerson as to which was to have her. They’d both been in the party that followed them, and Stangerson had shot her father, which seemed to give him the best claim. But when they argued it out in council, Drebber’s party was the stronger, so the Prophet gave her over to him. No one won’t have her very long though, for I saw death in her face yesterday. She is more like a ghost than a woman. Are you off, then?”

“Yes, I am off,” said Jefferson Hope, who had risen from his seat. His face might have been cut out of marble, so hard and set was its expression, while its eyes glowed with a deadly light.

“Where are you going?”

“Never mind,” he answered; and, throwing his weapon over his shoulder, strode off down the gorge and so away into the heart of the mountains to the homes of the wild beasts. Among them all there was none so fierce and so dangerous as himself.

The Mormon's prediction came true only too well. It may have been the terrible death of her father. Or it may have been the effects of the hateful marriage she had been forced into. Either way, poor Lucy never held up her head again. She pined away and died within a month. Her sottish husband had married her mainly for the sake of John Ferrier's property. He did not show any great grief at his loss. But his other wives mourned over her. They sat up with her the night before the burial, as is the Mormon custom. They were grouped round the bier in the early hours of the morning. Then, to their inexpressible fear and astonishment, the door was flung open. A savage-looking, weather-beaten man in tattered garments strode into the room. Without a glance or a word to the cowering women, he walked up to the white silent figure. That figure had once held the pure soul of Lucy Ferrier. Stooping over her, he pressed his lips reverently to her cold forehead. Then he snatched up her hand and took the wedding-ring from her finger. "She shall not be buried in that," he cried with a fierce snarl. Before an alarm could be raised, he sprang down the stairs and was gone. The episode was so strange and so brief that the watchers might have found it hard to believe it themselves. They might have found it hard to persuade other people of it, too. But there was the undeniable fact that the circlet of gold which marked her as having been a bride had disappeared.

For some months Jefferson Hope lingered among the mountains. He led a strange wild life. He nursed in his heart the fierce desire for vengeance which possessed him. Tales were told in the City of the weird figure which was seen prowling about the suburbs. This figure also haunted the lonely mountain gorges. Once a bullet whistled through Stangerson's window. It flattened itself upon the wall within a foot of him. On another occasion, Drebber passed under a cliff. A great boulder crashed down on him. He only escaped a terrible death by throwing himself upon his face. The two young Mormons were not long in discovering the reason for these attempts upon their lives. They led repeated expeditions into the mountains in the hope of capturing or killing their enemy. But they always failed. Then they adopted the precaution of never going out alone or after nightfall. They also had their houses guarded. After a time they were able to relax these measures. Nothing was either heard or seen of their opponent. They hoped that time had cooled his vindictiveness.

Far from doing so, time had, if anything, augmented it. The hunter's mind was of a hard, unyielding nature. The predominant idea of revenge had taken such complete possession of it that there was no room for any other emotion. He was, however, above all things practical. He soon realized that even his iron constitution could not stand the incessant strain which he was putting upon it. Exposure and want of wholesome food were wearing him out. If he died like a dog among the mountains, what was to become of his revenge then? And yet such a death was sure to overtake him if he persisted. He felt that that was to play his enemy's game. So he reluctantly returned to the old Nevada mines. There he would recruit his health. He would also amass money enough to allow him to pursue his object without privation.

His intention had been to be absent a year at the most. But a combination of unforeseen circumstances prevented his leaving the mines for nearly five. At the end of that time, however, his memory of his wrongs and his craving for revenge were quite as keen as on that memorable night. On that night he had stood by John Ferrier's grave. Disguised, and under an assumed name, he returned to Salt Lake City. He was careless what became of his own life, as long as he obtained what he knew to be justice. There he found evil tidings awaiting him. There had been a schism among the Chosen People a few months before. Some of the younger members of the Church had rebelled against the authority of the Elders. The result had been the secession of a certain number of the malcontents. They had left Utah and become Gentiles. Among these had been Drebber and Stangerson. And no one knew whither they had gone. Rumour reported that Drebber had managed to convert a large part of his property into money. He had departed a wealthy man. His companion, Stangerson, was comparatively poor. There was no clue at all, however, as to their whereabouts.

Many a man, however vindictive, would have abandoned all thought of revenge in the face of such a difficulty. But Jefferson Hope never faltered for a moment. He had a small competence. He eked it out by such employment as he could pick up. He travelled from town to town through the United States in quest of his enemies. Year passed into year. His black hair turned grizzled. But still he wandered on, a human bloodhound. His mind was wholly set upon the one object upon which he had devoted his life. At last his perseverance was rewarded. It was but a glance of a face in a window. But that one glance told him that Cleveland in Ohio possessed the men whom he was in pursuit of. He returned to his miserable lodgings with his plan of vengeance all arranged. It chanced, however, that Drebber, looking from his window, had recognized the vagrant in the street. He had read murder in his eyes. He hurried before a justice of the peace, accompanied by Stangerson, who had become his private secretary. He represented to him that they were in danger of their lives from the jealousy and hatred of an old rival. That evening Jefferson Hope was taken into custody. Not being able to find sureties, he was detained for some weeks. When at last he was liberated, it was only to find that Drebber's house was deserted. He and his secretary had departed for Europe.

Again the avenger had been foiled. Again his concentrated hatred urged him to continue the pursuit. Funds were wanting, however. For some time he had to return to work, saving every dollar for his approaching journey. At last, having collected enough to keep life in him, he departed for Europe. He tracked his enemies from city to city. He worked his way in any menial capacity. But he never overtook the fugitives. When he reached St. Petersburg they had departed for Paris. When he followed them there he learned that they had just set off for Copenhagen. At the Danish capital he was again a few days late. They had journeyed on to London. There he at last succeeded in running them to earth. As to what occurred there, we cannot do better than quote the old hunter's own account. It is duly recorded in Dr. Watson's Journal, to which we are already under such obligations.

CHAPTER 6. A CONTINUATION OF THE REMINISCENCES OF JOHN WATSON, M.D.

A CONTINUATION OF THE REMINISCENCES OF JOHN WATSON, M.D.

Our prisoner’s angry fight did not seem to show any cruelty in his feelings towards us. When he found he could not move, he smiled in a friendly way. He said he hoped he had not hurt any of us in the fight. “I guess you are going to take me to the police station,” he said to Sherlock Holmes. “My cab is at the door. If you free my legs, I will walk down to it. I am not as light to lift as I used to be.”

Gregson and Lestrade looked at each other. They seemed to think this idea was rather bold. But Holmes at once believed the prisoner and untied the towel we had tied around his ankles. He stood up and stretched his legs, as if to make sure they were free again. I remember I thought to myself, as I looked at him, that I had seldom seen a man with a stronger body. His dark, sunburned face showed determination and energy. It was as strong as his body.

“If there is an empty place for a chief of police, I think you are the man for it,” he said. He looked at my fellow lodger with open admiration. “The way you kept following me was amazing.”

“You had better come with me,” said Holmes to the two detectives.

“I can drive you,” said Lestrade.

“Good! And Gregson can come inside with me. You too, Doctor. You have taken an interest in the case and may as well stay with us.”

I agreed gladly, and we all went down together. Our prisoner did not try to escape. He stepped calmly into the cab that had been his, and we followed him. Lestrade climbed onto the driver’s seat, whipped the horse, and brought us to our destination in a very short time. We were led into a small room. There a police Inspector wrote down our prisoner’s name and the names of the men he was charged with murdering. The official was a white-faced man with no emotion. He did his duties in a dull, mechanical way. “The prisoner will be brought before the magistrates during the week,” he said. “In the meantime, Mr. Jefferson Hope, do you have anything you wish to say? I must warn you that your words will be written down and may be used against you.”

“I have a good deal to say,” our prisoner said slowly. “I want to tell you gentlemen all about it.”

“Had you not better save that for your trial?” asked the Inspector.

“I may never be tried,” he answered. “You need not look shocked. It is not suicide I am thinking of. Are you a Doctor?” He turned his fierce dark eyes on me as he asked this last question.

“Yes, I am,” I answered.

“Then put your hand here,” he said with a smile. He moved his wrists, which had chains on them, towards his chest.

I did so. At once I felt an extraordinary beating and movement going on inside. The walls of his chest seemed to shake and tremble, like a weak building inside when some powerful engine is at work. In the silence of the room I could hear a dull humming and buzzing noise coming from the same place.

“Why,” I cried, “you have an aortic aneurism!”

“That is what they call it,” he said calmly. “I went to a doctor last week about it. He told me it is bound to burst before many days pass. It has been getting worse for years. I got it from too much exposure and too little food among the Salt Lake Mountains. I have done my work now, and I do not care how soon I go. But I should like to leave some account of the business behind me. I do not want to be remembered as a common cut-throat.”

The Inspector and the two detectives had a hurried discussion about whether it was advisable to let him tell his story.

“Do you consider, Doctor, that there is immediate danger?” the former asked.

“Most certainly there is,” I answered.

“In that case it is clearly our duty, in the interests of justice, to take his statement,” said the Inspector. “You are free, sir, to give your account. I again warn you that it will be written down.”

“I will sit down, with your permission,” the prisoner said, and did so. “This aneurism of mine makes me easily tired. The struggle we had half an hour ago has not helped matters. I am on the brink of the grave, and I am not likely to lie to you. Every word I say is the absolute truth. How you use it is a matter of no consequence to me.”

With these words, Jefferson Hope leaned back in his chair and began the following remarkable statement. He spoke in a calm and orderly manner, as though the events he described were quite ordinary. I can vouch for the accuracy of the account below, for I have had access to Lestrade’s notebook. In it the prisoner’s words were written down exactly as they were spoken.

“It does not much matter to you why I hated these men,” he said. “It is enough that they were guilty of the death of two human beings—a father and a daughter—and that they had, therefore, lost their own lives. After all the time that has passed since their crime, it was impossible for me to secure a conviction against them in any court. I knew of their guilt, though, and I determined that I should be judge, jury, and executioner all rolled into one. You would have done the same, if you have any manhood in you, if you had been in my place.

“That girl I spoke of was to have married me twenty years ago. She was forced into marrying that same Drebber, and it broke her heart. I took the marriage ring from her dead finger. I vowed that his dying eyes should rest on that very ring, and that his last thoughts should be of the crime for which he was punished. I have carried it about with me. I have followed him and his accomplice over two continents until I caught them. They thought to tire me out, but they could not do it. If I die tomorrow, as is likely enough, I die knowing that my work in this world is done, and well done. They have perished, and by my hand. There is nothing left for me to hope for, or to desire.

“They were rich and I was poor. So it was not easy for me to follow them. When I got to London, my pocket was almost empty. I found that I had to do something for my living. Driving and riding are as natural to me as walking. So I went to a cab owner’s office and soon got work. I had to bring a certain sum each week to the owner. Anything above that I could keep for myself. There was seldom much over, but I managed to get by somehow. The hardest job was to learn my way around. I think that of all the confusing places ever made, this city is the most confusing. I had a map beside me, though. Once I had found the main hotels and stations, I did pretty well.

It was some time before I found out where my two gentlemen were living. But I asked and asked until at last I came across them. They were at a boarding-house at Camberwell, on the other side of the river. Once I found them, I knew I had them in my power. I had grown my beard, and there was no chance of them recognizing me. I would follow them and follow them until I saw my chance. I was determined that they should not escape me again.

They almost did it anyway. Wherever they went around London, I was always close behind them. Sometimes I followed them in my cab, and sometimes on foot. The cab was better, because then they could not get away from me. I could only earn money early in the morning or late at night. So I began to fall behind with my employer. I did not mind that, as long as I could get my hands on the men I wanted.

They were very clever, though. They must have thought there was some chance of being followed. They never went out alone, and never after dark. For two weeks I drove behind them every day, and never once saw them separate. Drebber himself was drunk half the time, but Stangerson was not easy to catch off guard. I watched them late and early, but never saw even a small chance. But I was not discouraged, because something told me that the hour had almost come. My only fear was that this thing in my chest might burst a little too soon and leave my work undone.

At last, one evening I was driving up and down Torquay Terrace, as the street was called in which they boarded. Then I saw a cab drive up to their door. Soon some bags and cases were brought out. After a time Drebber and Stangerson followed them and drove off. I whipped up my horse and kept them in sight. I felt very uneasy, because I feared they were going to move to another place. At Euston Station they got out. I left a boy to hold my horse and followed them onto the platform. I heard them ask for the Liverpool train. The guard answered that one had just gone and there would not be another for some hours. Stangerson seemed upset at that, but Drebber was rather pleased. I got so close to them in the crowd that I could hear every word between them. Drebber said he had a little business of his own to do. He said that if the other would wait for him, he would soon come back to him. His companion argued with him and reminded him that they had agreed to stay together. Drebber answered that the matter was a delicate one and that he must go alone. I could not catch what Stangerson said to that. But the other burst out swearing. He reminded him that he was nothing more than his paid servant and must not dare to tell him what to do. On that the Secretary gave it up as a bad job. He simply made him promise that if he missed the last train he should meet him at Halliday’s Private Hotel. Drebber answered that he would be back on the platform before eleven, and made his way out of the station.

The moment I had waited so long for had at last come. I had my enemies in my power. Together they could protect each other, but alone they were in my hands. I did not act too quickly, though. My plans were already formed. There is no satisfaction in revenge unless the offender has time to realize who it is that strikes him, and why punishment has come upon him. I had my plans arranged so that I should have the chance to make the man who had wronged me understand that his old sin had found him out. It happened that some days before, a gentleman who had been looking over some houses in the Brixton Road had dropped the key of one of them in my cab. It was claimed that same evening and returned. But in the meantime I had taken a copy of it and had another key made. By means of this I had access to at least one spot in this great city where I could be sure of being free from anyone coming in. How to get Drebber to that house was the difficult problem I now had to solve.

He walked down the road and went into one or two liquor shops. He stayed for nearly half an hour in the last of them. When he came out he walked unsteadily, and was clearly pretty drunk. There was a hansom just in front of me, and he called it. I followed it so close that the nose of my horse was within a yard of his driver the whole way. We rattled across Waterloo Bridge and through miles of streets. Then, to my great surprise, we found ourselves back in the Terrace in which he had boarded. I could not imagine what he meant to do by returning there. But I went on and pulled up my cab a hundred yards or so from the house. He entered it, and his hansom drove away. Give me a glass of water, if you please. My mouth gets dry with the talking.”

I handed him the glass, and he drank it down.

“That’s better,” he said. “Well, I waited for a quarter of an hour, or more. Then suddenly there came a noise like people fighting inside the house. The next moment the door was thrown open and two men appeared. One was Drebber, and the other was a young man I had never seen before. This fellow had Drebber by the collar. When they came to the top of the steps, he gave him a push and a kick. This sent him half across the road. ‘You dog,’ he cried, shaking his stick at him. ‘I’ll teach you to insult a honest girl!’ He was so angry that I think he would have beaten Drebber with his stick. But the dog moved away down the road as fast as his legs would carry him. He ran as far as the corner. Then he saw my cab, called me and jumped in. ‘Drive me to Halliday’s Private Hotel,’ said he.

“When I had him safely inside my cab, my heart jumped so with joy. I feared that at this last moment my heart problem might go wrong. I drove along slowly and thought about what was best to do. I might take him right out into the country. There in some empty lane I could have my last talk with him. I had almost decided on this when he solved the problem for me. The strong wish for drink had seized him again. He ordered me to stop outside a bar. He went in and told me to wait for him. He stayed there until closing time. When he came out he was so drunk that I knew the game was in my own hands.

“Don’t imagine that I meant to kill him in cold blood. It would only have been strict justice if I had done so. But I could not bring myself to do it. I had long decided that he should have a chance for his life if he chose to take it. I have done many jobs in America during my wandering life. Once I was janitor and cleaner at the laboratory at York College. One day the professor was teaching about poisons. He showed his students some alkaloid, as he called it. He had taken it from some South American arrow poison. It was so powerful that the smallest grain meant instant death. I saw the bottle where this preparation was kept. When they were all gone, I took a little of it. I was a fairly good dispenser. So I made this alkaloid into small pills that dissolve. Each pill I put in a box with a similar pill made without the poison. I decided then that when I had my chance, my gentlemen should each draw from one of these boxes. I would eat the pill that remained. It would be just as deadly, and much less noisy than firing across a handkerchief. From that day I always had my pill boxes with me. The time had now come when I was to use them.

“It was nearer one than twelve. It was a wild, cold night, blowing hard and raining in great amounts. It was sad outside, but I was glad within. I was so glad that I could have shouted from pure joy. Some of you gentlemen may have wished for a thing for twenty long years. Then suddenly you found it within your reach. You would understand my feelings. I lit a cigar and smoked it to steady my nerves. But my hands were shaking, and my temples were beating with excitement. As I drove, I could see old John Ferrier and sweet Lucy looking at me out of the darkness and smiling at me. They were as plain as I see you all in this room. All the way they were ahead of me, one on each side of the horse, until I stopped at the house in the Brixton Road.

“There was not a person to be seen, nor a sound to be heard, except the falling rain. When I looked in at the window, I found Drebber all gathered together in a drunken sleep. I shook him by the arm. ‘It’s time to get out,’ I said.

“‘All right, driver,’ said he.

“I suppose he thought we had come to the hotel that he had mentioned. He got out without another word and followed me down the garden. I had to walk beside him to keep him steady, for he was still a little unsteady. When we came to the door, I opened it and led him into the front room. I give you my word that all the way, the father and the daughter were walking in front of us.

“‘It’s very dark,’ said he, stepping about.

“‘We’ll soon have a light,’ I said. I struck a match and put it to a wax candle which I had brought with me. ‘Now, Enoch Drebber,’ I continued, turning to him and holding the light to my own face, ‘who am I?’

“He looked at me with red, drunken eyes for a moment. Then I saw horror spring up in them and shake his whole face. This showed me that he knew me. He moved back with a pale face. I saw sweat break out on his brow, and his teeth chattered in his head. At the sight, I leaned my back against the door and laughed loud and long. I had always known that revenge would be sweet. But I had never hoped for the deep satisfaction which now filled me.

“‘You dog!’ I said. ‘I have hunted you from Salt Lake City to St. Petersburg, and you have always escaped me. Now at last your wanderings have come to an end. Either you or I shall never see tomorrow’s sun rise.’ He moved still further away as I spoke. I could see on his face that he thought I was mad. So I was for the time. The pulses in my temples beat like heavy hammers. I believe I would have had a fit of some sort if blood had not come from my nose and relieved me.

“‘What do you think of Lucy Ferrier now?’ I cried. I locked the door and shook the key in his face. ‘Punishment has been slow in coming, but it has caught you at last.’ I saw his coward lips tremble as I spoke. He would have begged for his life, but he knew well that it was useless.

“‘Would you murder me?’ he said with difficulty.

“‘There is no murder,’ I answered. ‘Who talks of murdering a mad dog? What mercy had you upon my poor darling? You dragged her from her killed father and took her away to your cursed and shameful harem.’

“‘It was not I who killed her father,’ he cried.

"'But it was you who broke her innocent heart,' I cried, pushing the box in front of him. 'Let the high God judge between us. Choose and eat. There is death in one and life in the other. I shall take what you leave. Let us see if there is justice upon the earth, or if we are ruled by chance.'

"He moved back with wild cries and prayers for mercy. But I pulled out my knife and held it to his throat until he had obeyed me. Then I swallowed the other, and we stood facing one another in silence for a minute or more. We waited to see which was to live and which was to die. Shall I ever forget the look which came over his face when the first warning pains told him that the poison was in his body? I laughed as I saw it, and held Lucy's marriage ring in front of his eyes. It was only for a moment, because the poison acts fast. A sudden fit of pain twisted his face. He threw his hands out in front of him, lost his balance, and then, with a rough, harsh cry, fell heavily upon the floor. I turned him over with my foot, and placed my hand upon his heart. There was no movement. He was dead!

"The blood had been running from my nose, but I had taken no notice of it. I do not know what put it into my head to write upon the wall with it. Perhaps it was some playful idea of setting the police upon a wrong track, for I felt light and happy. I remembered a German being found in New York with RACHE written up above him. At the time, the newspapers argued that the secret societies must have done it. I guessed that what puzzled the New Yorkers would puzzle the Londoners. So I dipped my finger in my own blood and printed it on a convenient place on the wall. Then I walked down to my cab and found that there was nobody about, and that the night was still very wild. I had driven some distance when I put my hand into the pocket in which I usually kept Lucy's ring, and found that it was not there. I was struck with shock at this, for it was the only keepsake that I had of her. I thought that I might have dropped it when I bent over Drebber's body. So I drove back, and leaving my cab in a side street, I went bravely up to the house—for I was ready to risk anything rather than lose the ring. When I arrived there, I walked right into the arms of a police-officer who was coming out. I only managed to remove his suspicions by pretending to be completely drunk.

"That was how Enoch Drebber came to his end. All I had to do then was to do as much for Stangerson, and so pay off John Ferrier's debt. I knew that he was staying at Halliday's Private Hotel, and I waited about all day, but he never came out. I think that he suspected something when Drebber failed to appear. He was clever, was Stangerson, and always on his guard. If he thought he could keep me off by staying inside he was very much mistaken. I soon found out which was the window of his bedroom. Early next morning I used some ladders which were lying in the lane behind the hotel, and so made my way into his room in the grey light of the dawn. I woke him up and told him that the hour had come when he was to answer for the life he had taken so long before. I described Drebber's death to him, and I gave him the same choice of the poisoned pills. Instead of taking the chance of safety which that offered him, he jumped from his bed and flew at my throat. In self-defence I stabbed him to the heart. It would have been the same in any case, for Providence would never have allowed his guilty hand to pick out anything but the poison.

"I have little more to say, and it is as well, for I am almost finished. I went on driving my cab for a day or so, intending to keep at it until I could save enough to take me back to America. I was standing in the yard when a poorly dressed young fellow asked if there was a cab driver there called Jefferson Hope. He said that his cab was wanted by a gentleman at 221B, Baker Street. I went round, suspecting no harm. The next thing I knew, this young man here had the handcuffs on my wrists, and as neatly fastened as ever I saw in my life. That is the whole of my story, gentlemen. You may consider me to be a murderer; but I hold that I am just as much an officer of justice as you are."

So exciting had the man's story been, and his manner was so powerful that we had sat silent and full of attention. Even the professional detectives, used to every detail of crime as they were, appeared to be deeply interested in the man's story. When he finished we sat for some minutes in a silence which was only broken by the scratching of Lestrade's pencil as he gave the finishing touches to his shorthand account.

"There is only one point on which I should like a little more information," Sherlock Holmes said at last. "Who was your helper who came for the ring which I advertised?"

The prisoner winked at my friend playfully. "I can tell my own secrets," he said, "but I do not get other people into trouble. I saw your advertisement, and I thought it might be a trick, or it might be the ring which I wanted. My friend offered to go and see. I think you will agree he did it cleverly."

"Not a doubt of that," said Holmes warmly.

"Now, gentlemen," the Inspector remarked seriously, "the forms of the law must be followed. On Thursday the prisoner will be brought before the magistrates, and your attendance will be required. Until then I will be responsible for him." He rang the bell as he spoke, and Jefferson Hope was led off by a couple of guards, while my friend and I made our way out of the Station and took a cab back to Baker Street.

CHAPTER 7. LIGHT IN THE DARKNESS.

LIGHT IN THE DARKNESS.

The news that Lestrade brought us was so important and so surprising that all three of us were completely shocked. Gregson jumped out of his chair and knocked over the rest of his whiskey and water. I stared in silence at Sherlock Holmes. His lips were pressed together and his brows were pulled down over his eyes.

“Stangerson too!” he said in a low voice. “The plot is growing more complex.”

“It was already complex enough before,” Lestrade complained as he took a chair. “I seem to have walked into a kind of war council.”

“Are you—are you sure about this news?” Gregson asked, stumbling over his words.

“I have just come from his room,” said Lestrade. “I was the first to discover what had happened.”

“We have been hearing Gregson’s view of the matter,” Holmes said. “Would you mind telling us what you have seen and done?”

“I have no objection,” Lestrade answered, sitting down. “I freely admit that I believed Stangerson was involved in the death of Drebber. This new development has shown me that I was completely wrong. Full of that one idea, I set out to find out what had happened to the Secretary. They had been seen together at Euston Station at about half past eight on the evening of the third. At two in the morning Drebber was found in the Brixton Road. The question I faced was to find out how Stangerson had spent his time between 8.30 and the time of the crime, and what had happened to him afterwards. I sent a telegram to Liverpool, describing the man and warning them to watch the American boats. Then I began calling at all the hotels and lodging houses near Euston. You see, I reasoned that if Drebber and his companion had separated, the natural thing for the companion would be to stay somewhere nearby for the night, and then wait around the station again the next morning.”

“They would likely have agreed on some meeting place beforehand,” Holmes remarked.

“So it turned out. I spent the whole of yesterday evening making enquiries with no success at all. This morning I began very early, and at eight o’clock I reached Halliday’s Private Hotel, in Little George Street. When I asked whether a Mr. Stangerson was staying there, they at once said yes.

“‘No doubt you are the gentleman he was expecting,’ they said. ‘He has been waiting for a gentleman for two days.’

“‘Where is he now?’ I asked.

“‘He is upstairs in bed. He wished to be called at nine.’

“‘I will go up and see him at once,’ I said.

“It seemed to me that my sudden appearance might shake his nerves and lead him to say something careless. The Boots offered to show me the room. It was on the second floor, and there was a small corridor leading up to it. The Boots pointed out the door to me and was about to go downstairs again when I saw something that made me feel sick, even after twenty years of experience. From under the door curled a little red ribbon of blood. It had run across the passage and formed a small pool along the baseboard on the other side. I gave a cry, which brought the Boots back. He nearly fainted when he saw it. The door was locked from the inside, but we put our shoulders to it and knocked it in. The window of the room was open, and beside the window, all curled up, lay the body of a man in his nightclothes. He was quite dead, and had been for some time, for his limbs were stiff and cold. When we turned him over, the Boots recognized him at once as the same gentleman who had taken the room under the name of Joseph Stangerson. The cause of death was a deep stab in the left side, which must have gone through the heart. And now comes the strangest part of the affair. What do you suppose was above the murdered man?”

I felt my skin crawl, and a sense of coming horror, even before Sherlock Holmes answered.

“The word RACHE, written in letters of blood,” he said.

“That was it,” said Lestrade, in a voice full of shock; and we were all silent for a while.

There was something so orderly and so impossible to understand about the deeds of this unknown killer that it gave his crimes a fresh horror. My nerves, which were steady enough on the battlefield, felt a chill as I thought of it.

“The man was seen,” Lestrade continued. “A milk boy, on his way to the dairy, happened to walk down the lane which leads from the mews at the back of the hotel. He noticed that a ladder, which usually lay there, was raised against one of the windows of the second floor, which was wide open. After passing, he looked back and saw a man come down the ladder. He came down so quietly and openly that the boy thought he was some carpenter or joiner working at the hotel. He took no particular notice of him, except thinking to himself that it was early for him to be at work. He has an impression that the man was tall, had a reddish face, and was dressed in a long, brownish coat. He must have stayed in the room some little time after the murder, for we found blood-stained water in the basin, where he had washed his hands, and marks on the sheets where he had deliberately wiped his knife.”

I glanced at Holmes when I heard the description of the murderer, which matched his own so exactly. There was, however, no trace of triumph or satisfaction on his face.

“Did you find nothing in the room that could give a clue to the murderer?” he asked.

“Nothing. Stangerson had Drebber’s purse in his pocket, but it seems that this was usual, as he did all the paying. There was eighty odd pounds in it, but nothing had been taken. Whatever the motives of these extraordinary crimes, robbery is certainly not one of them. There were no papers or notes in the murdered man’s pocket, except a single telegram, dated from Cleveland about a month ago, and containing the words, ‘J. H. is in Europe.’ There was no name signed to this message.”

“And there was nothing else?” Holmes asked.

“Nothing of any importance. The man’s novel, with which he had read himself to sleep, was lying on the bed, and his pipe was on a chair beside him. There was a glass of water on the table, and on the window-sill a small chip ointment box containing a couple of pills.”

Sherlock Holmes sprang from his chair with a cry of delight.

“The last link,” he cried, triumphant. “My case is complete.”

The two detectives stared at him in amazement.

“I now have in my hands,” my companion said, with confidence, “all the threads that have formed such a tangle. Of course, there are details to be filled in. But I am as certain of all the main facts, from the time that Drebber left Stangerson at the station, up to the discovery of the body of the latter, as if I had seen them with my own eyes. I will give you a proof of my knowledge. Could you put your hand on those pills?”

“I have them,” said Lestrade, producing a small white box. “I took them and the purse and the telegram. I intended to have them put in a safe place at the Police Station. It was the merest chance that I took these pills. I must say that I do not attach any importance to them.”

“Give them here,” said Holmes. “Now, Doctor,” he said, turning to me, “are those ordinary pills?”

They certainly were not. They were a pearly gray color, small, round, and almost transparent against the light. “From their lightness and transparency, I should imagine that they are soluble in water,” I remarked.

“Exactly so,” answered Holmes. “Now would you mind going down and bringing up that poor little devil of a terrier? It has been bad so long, and the landlady wanted you to put it out of its pain yesterday.”

I went downstairs and carried the dog upstairs in my arms. Its labored breathing and glazing eye showed that it was not far from its end. Indeed, its snow-white muzzle showed that it had already exceeded the usual term of canine existence. I placed it upon a cushion on the rug.

“I will now cut one of these pills in two,” said Holmes. He drew his penknife and suited the action to the word. “One half we return to the box for future purposes. The other half I will place in this wine glass, in which is a teaspoonful of water. You see that our friend, the Doctor, is right, and that it readily dissolves.”

“This may be very interesting,” said Lestrade, in the injured tone of one who suspects that he is being laughed at. “I cannot see, however, what it has to do with the death of Mr. Joseph Stangerson.”

“Patience, my friend, patience! You will find in time that it has everything to do with it. I shall now add a little milk to make the mixture palatable. When we present it to the dog, we find that he laps it up readily enough.”

As he spoke, he turned the contents of the wine glass into a saucer and placed it in front of the terrier. The dog speedily licked it dry. Sherlock Holmes’ earnest demeanor had so far convinced us that we all sat in silence, watching the animal intently, and expecting some startling effect. None such appeared, however. The dog continued to lie stretched upon the cushion, breathing in a labored way. But it was apparently neither the better nor the worse for its drink.

Holmes had taken out his watch. As minute followed minute without result, an expression of the utmost chagrin and disappointment appeared on his features. He gnawed his lip, drummed his fingers upon the table, and showed every other symptom of acute impatience. His emotion was so great that I felt sincerely sorry for him. The two detectives smiled derisively. They were by no means displeased at this check which he had met.

“It can’t be a coincidence,” he cried at last, springing from his chair and pacing wildly up and down the room. “It is impossible that it should be a mere coincidence. The very pills which I suspected in the case of Drebber are actually found after the death of Stangerson. And yet they are inert. What can it mean? Surely my whole chain of reasoning cannot have been false. It is impossible! And yet this wretched dog is none the worse. Ah, I have it! I have it!” With a perfect shriek of delight he rushed to the box, cut the other pill in two, dissolved it, added milk, and presented it to the terrier. The unfortunate creature’s tongue seemed hardly to have been moistened in it before it gave a convulsive shiver in every limb. It lay as rigid and lifeless as if it had been struck by lightning.

Sherlock Holmes drew a long breath and wiped the perspiration from his forehead. “I should have more faith,” he said. “I ought to know by this time that when a fact appears to be opposed to a long train of deductions, it invariably proves to be capable of bearing some other interpretation. Of the two pills in that box, one was of the most deadly poison, and the other was entirely harmless. I ought to have known that before I ever saw the box at all.”

This last statement appeared to me to be so startling that I could hardly believe that he was in his sober senses. There was the dead dog, however, to prove that his conjecture had been correct. It seemed to me that the mists in my own mind were gradually clearing away. I began to have a dim, vague perception of the truth.

“All this seems strange to you,” continued Holmes, “because you failed at the beginning of the inquiry to grasp the importance of the single real clue which was presented to you. I had the good fortune to seize upon that. Everything which has occurred since then has served to confirm my original supposition. Indeed, it was the logical sequence of it. Hence things which have perplexed you and made the case more obscure have served to enlighten me and to strengthen my conclusions. It is a mistake to confound strangeness with mystery. The most commonplace crime is often the most mysterious, because it presents no new or special features from which deductions may be drawn. This murder would have been infinitely more difficult to unravel had the body of the victim been simply found lying in the roadway without any of those outré and sensational accompaniments which have rendered it remarkable. These strange details, far from making the case more difficult, have really had the effect of making it less so.”

Mr. Gregson had listened to this address with considerable impatience. He could contain himself no longer. “Look here, Mr. Sherlock Holmes,” he said. “We are all ready to acknowledge that you are a smart man, and that you have your own methods of working. We want something more than mere theory and preaching now, though. It is a case of taking the man. I have made my case out, and it seems I was wrong. Young Charpentier could not have been engaged in this second affair. Lestrade went after his man, Stangerson, and it appears that he was wrong too. You have thrown out hints here and hints there, and seem to know more than we do. But the time has come when we feel that we have a right to ask you straight how much you do know of the business. Can you name the man who did it?”

“I cannot help feeling that Gregson is right, sir,” said Lestrade. “We have both tried, and we have both failed. You have said more than once since I came into the room that you had all the evidence you need. Surely you will not keep it back any longer.”

“Any delay in arresting the assassin,” I said, “might give him time to commit some fresh atrocity.”

Pushed by all of us like this, Holmes showed signs of uncertainty. He kept walking up and down the room. His head was sunk on his chest and his brows were pulled down. He always did this when lost in thought.

“There will be no more murders,” he said at last. He stopped suddenly and faced us. “You can put that thought out of your mind. You have asked me if I know the name of the assassin. I do. But just knowing his name is a small thing compared with the power to lay our hands on him. I expect to do that very soon. I have good hopes of managing it through my own plans. But it is a thing that needs careful handling. We have a clever and desperate man to deal with. He is supported, as I have had reason to prove, by another man who is as clever as himself. As long as this man has no idea that anyone can have a clue, there is some chance of catching him. But if he had the slightest suspicion, he would change his name. He would vanish in an instant among the four million people of this great city. I do not mean to hurt either of your feelings. But I must say that I consider these men more than a match for the official police. That is why I have not asked for your help. If I fail, I shall of course take all the blame for this omission. But I am prepared for that. For now I am ready to promise that the instant I can speak to you without endangering my own plans, I shall do so.”

Gregson and Lestrade seemed far from satisfied by this promise. They were also unhappy with the low opinion of the detective police. The former had gone red to the roots of his flaxen hair. The other’s beady eyes shone with curiosity and anger. Neither of them had time to speak, however, before there was a tap at the door. The spokesman of the street boys, young Wiggins, brought in his small and dirty person.

“Please, sir,” he said, touching his forelock, “I have the cab downstairs.”

“Good boy,” said Holmes, smoothly. “Why don’t you introduce this pattern at Scotland Yard?” he went on. He took a pair of steel handcuffs from a drawer. “See how beautifully the spring works. They close in an instant.”

“The old pattern is good enough,” said Lestrade, “if we can only find the man to put them on.”

“Very good, very good,” said Holmes, smiling. “The cabman may as well help me with my boxes. Just ask him to step up, Wiggins.”

I was surprised to find my companion speaking as though he were about to set out on a journey. He had not said anything to me about it. There was a small portmanteau in the room. He pulled it out and began to strap it. He was busy at it when the cabman entered the room.

“Just give me a help with this buckle, cabman,” he said. He was kneeling over his task and never turned his head.

The fellow came forward with a somewhat sullen, defiant air. He put down his hands to help. At that instant there was a sharp click, the sound of metal, and Sherlock Holmes sprang to his feet again.

“Gentlemen,” he cried, with flashing eyes, “let me introduce you to Mr. Jefferson Hope, the murderer of Enoch Drebber and of Joseph Stangerson.”

The whole thing happened in a moment. It was so quick that I had no time to realize it. I have a vivid memory of that instant. I remember Holmes’ triumphant expression and the ring of his voice. I remember the cabman’s dazed, savage face as he glared at the glittering handcuffs. They had appeared as if by magic upon his wrists. For a second or two we might have been a group of statues. Then, with a wordless roar of fury, the prisoner wrenched himself free from Holmes’s grasp. He threw himself through the window. Wood and glass gave way before him. But before he got quite through, Gregson, Lestrade, and Holmes sprang upon him like so many staghounds. He was dragged back into the room, and then began a terrible fight. He was so powerful and so fierce that the four of us were shaken off again and again. He seemed to have the convulsive strength of a man in an epileptic fit. His face and hands were terribly cut by his passage through the glass. But loss of blood had no effect in reducing his resistance. It was not until Lestrade succeeded in getting his hand inside his neckcloth and half-strangling him that we made him realize his struggles were of no use. Even then we felt no security until we had tied his feet as well as his hands. That done, we rose to our feet breathless and panting.

“We have his cab,” said Sherlock Holmes. “It will serve to take him to Scotland Yard. And now, gentlemen,” he went on, with a pleasant smile, “we have reached the end of our little mystery. You are very welcome to put any questions you like to me now. There is no danger that I will refuse to answer them.”
