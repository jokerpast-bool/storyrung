STORY OF THE DOOR

Mr. Utterson the lawyer was a man with a rough face. A smile never lit it. He was cold, sparing with words, and uncomfortable in talk. He held back his feelings. He was thin, tall, dusty, and dull, yet somehow easy to like. At friendly meetings, when the wine was to his taste, something very human shone from his eye. That something never came into his talk. But it spoke in these silent signs of the after-dinner face. More often and more loudly, it spoke in the acts of his life. He was hard on himself. He drank strong drink when he was alone, to kill a taste for fine wine. And though he enjoyed the stage, he had not crossed the doors of one for twenty years. But he had an accepted tolerance for others. Sometimes he wondered, almost with jealousy, at the high force of spirit in their bad acts. In any extreme case he was likely to help rather than to scold. “I lean to Cain’s wrong belief,” he used to say in an odd way: “I let my brother go to the devil in his own way.” Because of this character, it was often his fortune to be the last good-name friend and the last good influence in the lives of men going down. And to such men, as long as they came to his rooms, he never showed a shade of change in his manner.

No doubt the act was easy for Mr. Utterson. He was undemonstrative at the best. Even his friendship seemed to be founded in a similar wide good-nature. It is the mark of a modest man to accept his friendly circle ready-made from the hands of chance. And that was the lawyer’s way. His friends were those of his own blood or those he had known the longest. His affections, like climbing plants, were the growth of time. They implied no fitness in the object. Hence, no doubt, the bond that united him to Mr. Richard Enfield, his distant relative, the well-known man about town. It was a hard nut for many to crack: what these two could see in each other, or what subject they could find in common. It was reported by those who met them on their Sunday walks that they said nothing, looked strangely dull, and would call out with obvious relief the appearance of a friend. For all that, the two men put the greatest store by these outings. They counted them the chief treasure of each week. They not only set aside occasions of pleasure, but even resisted the calls of business, so they might enjoy them without a break.

It chanced on one of these walks that their way led them down a side street in a busy quarter of London. The street was small and what is called quiet, but it drove a thriving trade on the week-days. The inhabitants were all doing well, it seemed. They all hoped with eagerness to do better still. They laid out the extra of their gains in show. So the shop fronts stood along that street with an air of invitation, like rows of smiling female sellers. Even on Sunday, when it covered its more showy charms and lay comparatively empty of passage, the street shone out in contrast to its dark neighbourhood, like a fire in a forest. With its freshly painted window covers, well-polished brasses, and general cleanliness and cheerfulness of note, it instantly caught and pleased the eye of the passer-by.

Two doors from one corner, on the left hand going east, the line was broken by the entry of a court. Just at that point, a certain evil-looking block of building thrust forward its pointed roof on the street. It was two stories high. It showed no window, nothing but a door on the lower story and a blind forehead of stained wall on the upper. It bore in every feature the marks of long and dirty negligence. The door, which had neither bell nor knocker, was blistered and stained. Tramps slouched into the recess and struck matches on the panels. Children kept shop upon the steps. The schoolboy had tried his knife on the mouldings. And for close on a generation, no one had appeared to drive away these random visitors or to repair their damage.

Mr. Enfield and the lawyer were on the other side of the side street. But when they came abreast of the entry, the former lifted up his cane and pointed.

“Did you ever notice that door?” he asked. And when his companion had replied yes, “It is connected in my mind,” added he, “with a very odd story.”

“Indeed?” said Mr. Utterson, with a slight change of voice, “and what was that?”

“Well, it was like this,” said Mr. Enfield. “I was coming home from some place far away. It was about three in the morning on a dark winter night. My way went through a part of town where there was nothing to see but street lamps. Street after street, and all the people asleep. Street after street, all lit up as if for a big public event, and all as empty as a church. At last I got into that state of mind when a man listens and listens and begins to wish for the sight of a policeman. All at once, I saw two figures. One was a little man walking quickly toward the east. The other was a girl of maybe eight or ten, running as hard as she could down a side street. Well, sir, the two ran into one another at the corner. Then came the horrible part. The man walked calmly over the child’s body and left her screaming on the ground. It sounds like nothing to hear, but it was terrible to see. It was not like a man. It was like some cruel machine. I shouted, ran after him, caught my gentleman, and brought him back. A crowd had already gathered around the screaming child. He was perfectly calm and made no resistance. But he gave me one look, so ugly that it brought out sweat on me like running. The people who had come out were the girl’s own family. Soon the doctor came, for whom she had been sent. Well, the child was not much worse, only more frightened, according to the doctor. And you might have supposed that would be the end of it. But there was one curious thing. I had felt a strong dislike for my gentleman at first sight. So had the child’s family, which was only natural. But the doctor’s case was what struck me. He was the usual plain doctor, of no particular age or color, with a strong Edinburgh accent, and about as emotional as a bagpipe. Well, sir, he was like the rest of us. Every time he looked at my prisoner, I saw that doctor turn sick and white with the desire to kill him. I knew what was in his mind, just as he knew what was in mine. And killing being out of the question, we did the next best thing. We told the man we could and would make such a scandal out of this as should make his name stink from one end of London to the other. If he had any friends or any good name, we promised that he should lose them. And all the time, as we were saying this in great anger, we were keeping the women off him as best we could, for they were as wild as angry birds. I never saw a circle of such hateful faces. And there was the man in the middle, with a kind of black, mocking calmness—frightened too, I could see that—but carrying it off, sir, really like Satan. ‘If you choose to make money out of this accident,’ said he, ‘I am naturally helpless. No gentleman but wishes to avoid a scene,’ says he. ‘Name your figure.’ Well, we pushed him up to a hundred pounds for the child’s family. He would clearly have liked to refuse. But there was something about all of us that meant trouble, and at last he paid. The next thing was to get the money. And where do you think he carried us but to that place with the door? He pulled out a key, went in, and soon came back with about ten pounds in gold and a check for the rest on Coutts’s, made payable to bearer and signed with a name that I cannot mention. Though it is one of the points of my story, it was a name at least very well known and often printed. The amount was high. But the signature was good for more than that, if it was only genuine. I took the liberty of pointing out to my gentleman that the whole business looked doubtful. And that a man does not, in real life, walk into a cellar door at four in the morning and come out of it with another man’s check for close upon a hundred pounds. But he was quite easy and mocking. ‘Set your mind at rest,’ says he. ‘I will stay with you till the banks open and cash the check myself.’ So we all set off, the doctor, and the child’s father, and our friend and myself. We passed the rest of the night in my rooms. And next day, when we had eaten breakfast, we went in a body to the bank. I gave in the check myself, and said I had every reason to believe it was a forgery. Not a bit of it. The check was genuine.”

“Tut-tut,” said Mr. Utterson.

“I see you feel as I do,” said Mr. Enfield. “Yes, it is a bad story. For my man was a fellow that nobody could have to do with, a really terrible man. And the person that drew the check is the very model of good behavior, celebrated too, and (what makes it worse) one of your fellows who do what they call good. Blackmail, I suppose. An honest man paying through the nose for some of the wild actions of his youth. Black-Mail House is what I call that place with the door, in consequence. Though even that, you know, is far from explaining all,” he added, and with the words fell into a vein of thinking.

From this he was recalled by Mr. Utterson asking rather suddenly: “And you do not know if the drawer of the check lives there?”

“A likely place, is it not?” returned Mr. Enfield. “But I happen to have noticed his address. He lives in some square or other.”

“And you never asked about the—place with the door?” said Mr. Utterson.

“No, sir: I had a delicacy,” was the reply. “I feel very strongly about putting questions. It partakes too much of the style of the day of judgment. You start a question, and it is like starting a stone. You sit quietly on the top of a hill. And away the stone goes, starting others. And soon some mild old bird (the last you would have thought of) is knocked on the head in his own back garden and the family have to change their name. No, sir, I make it a rule of mine: the more it looks like Queer Street, the less I ask.”

“A very good rule, too,” said the lawyer.

"But I have studied the place myself," Mr. Enfield went on. "It hardly looks like a house. There is no other door. Nobody goes in or out of that one except, once in a long while, the gentleman of my adventure. There are three windows looking on the court on the first floor. There are none below. The windows are always shut, but they are clean. And there is a chimney that usually has smoke. So somebody must live there. And yet it is not so sure. The buildings are so packed together around that court that it is hard to say where one ends and another begins."

The two men walked on again for a while in silence. Then, "Enfield," said Mr. Utterson, "that is a good rule of yours."

"Yes, I think it is," Enfield answered.

"But even so," the lawyer went on, "there is one point I want to ask. I want to ask the name of that man who walked over the child."

"Well," said Mr. Enfield, "I do not see what harm it would do. It was a man named Hyde."

"H'm," said Mr. Utterson. "What sort of man is he to look at?"

"He is not easy to describe. There is something wrong with his appearance. Something unpleasant, something truly awful. I never saw a man I did not like so much, and yet I hardly know why. He must be twisted somewhere. He gives a strong feeling of something twisted, although I could not say where. He is a very strange-looking man, and yet I really cannot name anything unusual. No, sir, I cannot make sense of it. I cannot describe him. And it is not because I have forgotten. I say I can see him this moment."

Mr. Utterson again walked some way in silence and clearly under a weight of thought.

"You are sure he used a key?" he asked at last.

"My dear sir…" began Enfield, surprised out of himself.

"Yes, I know," said Utterson. "I know it must seem strange. The fact is, if I do not ask you the name of the other party, it is because I know it already. You see, Richard, your tale has hit home. If you have been not exact in any point, you had better correct it."

"I think you might have warned me," the other returned, with a touch of bad mood. "But I have been exact in a small, picky way, as you call it. The fellow had a key. And more than that, he still has it. I saw him use it, not a week ago."

Mr. Utterson sighed deeply but said never a word. And the young man soon began again. "Here is another lesson to say nothing," he said. "I am ashamed of my long tongue. Let us make a deal never to speak of this again."

"With all my heart," said the lawyer. "I shake hands on that, Richard."

SEARCH FOR MR. HYDE

That evening Mr. Utterson came home to his house as a single man, in low spirits, and sat down to dinner without pleasure. It was his habit on a Sunday, when this meal was over, to sit close by the fire with a book of some dry religion on his reading-desk, until the clock of the nearby church rang out the hour of twelve. Then he would go quietly and with thanks to bed. On this night, however, as soon as the cloth was taken away, he took up a candle and went into his business-room. There he opened his safe. From the most private part of it he took a document marked on the envelope as Dr. Jekyll's Will. He sat down with a clouded brow to study its contents. The will was written in the doctor's own hand. Mr. Utterson had charge of it now that it was made, but he had refused to lend the least help in the making of it. It provided not only that, in case of the death of Henry Jekyll, M.D., D.C.L., L.L.D., F.R.S., etc., all his possessions were to pass into the hands of his "friend and helper Edward Hyde." It also provided that in case of Dr. Jekyll's "disappearance or unexplained absence for any period exceeding three calendar months," the said Edward Hyde should step into the said Henry Jekyll's shoes without further delay. He should be free from any burden or duty, beyond the payment of a few small sums to the members of the doctor's household. This document had long been a pain to the lawyer's eyes. It offended him both as a lawyer and as a lover of the sane and usual sides of life. To him, the fanciful was the immodest. And until now it was his ignorance of Mr. Hyde that had swelled his anger. Now, by a sudden turn, it was his knowledge. It was already bad enough when the name was only a name of which he could learn no more. It was worse when it began to be given awful qualities. And out of the shifting, thin mists that had so long baffled his eye, there leaped up the sudden, clear picture of a devil.

"I thought it was madness," he said, as he replaced the offensive paper in the safe, "and now I begin to fear it is shame."

With that he blew out his candle, put on a great-coat, and set out in the direction of Cavendish Square, that stronghold of medicine. There his friend, the great Dr. Lanyon, had his house and received his crowding patients. "If any one knows, it will be Lanyon," he had thought.

The serious butler knew and welcomed him. He was subjected to no stage of delay. He was shown direct from the door to the dining-room, where Dr. Lanyon sat alone over his wine. This was a warm, healthy, neat, red-faced gentleman, with a shock of hair early white, and a noisy and decided manner. At sight of Mr. Utterson, he sprang up from his chair and welcomed him with both hands. The friendliness, as was the way of the man, was somewhat showy to the eye. But it rested on genuine feeling. For these two were old friends, old mates both at school and college. Both were thorough respecters of themselves and of each other. And, what does not always follow, they were men who thoroughly enjoyed each other's company.

After a little rambling talk, the lawyer led up to the subject which so unpleasantly filled his mind.

"I suppose, Lanyon," said he, "you and I must be the two oldest friends that Henry Jekyll has?"

"I wish the friends were younger," chuckled Dr. Lanyon. "But I suppose we are. And what of that? I see little of him now."

"Indeed?" said Utterson. "I thought you had a bond of common interest."

"We had," was the reply. "But it is more than ten years since Henry Jekyll became too fanciful for me. He began to go wrong, wrong in mind. And though of course I continue to take an interest in him for old sake's sake, as they say, I see and I have seen very little of the man. Such unscientific nonsense," added the doctor, flushing suddenly purple, "would have estranged Damon and Pythias."

This little spirit of anger was somewhat of a relief to Mr. Utterson. “They have only differed on some point of science,” he thought. He was a man of no scientific passions (except in the matter of property law). He even added: “It is nothing worse than that!” He gave his friend a few seconds to recover his calm. Then he approached the question he had come to put. “Did you ever come across a person he supported—one Hyde?” he asked.

“Hyde?” repeated Lanyon. “No. Never heard of him. Not since my time.”

That was the amount of information that the lawyer carried back with him to the great, dark bed. He tossed back and forth on it until the small hours of the morning began to grow large. It was a night of little ease to his working mind. It worked in mere darkness and was surrounded by questions.

Six struck on the bells of the church that was so near and easy to reach from Mr. Utterson’s home. Still he was digging at the problem. Until now it had touched him on the thinking side alone. But now his imagination was also engaged, or rather captured. He lay and tossed in the thick darkness of the night and the curtained room. Mr. Enfield’s tale went by before his mind in a roll of lighted pictures. He would be aware of the great field of lamps of a city at night. Then of the figure of a man walking fast. Then of a child running from the doctor. Then these met, and that human Juggernaut stepped on the child and passed on regardless of her screams. Or else he would see a room in a rich house, where his friend lay asleep, dreaming and smiling at his dreams. Then the door of that room would be opened, the curtains of the bed pulled apart, the sleeping man called back, and look! there would stand by his side a figure to whom power was given. Even at that dead hour, he must rise and do its bidding. The figure in these two phases haunted the lawyer all night. If at any time he slept lightly, it was only to see it move more quietly through sleeping houses. Or move faster and still faster, even to a feeling of spinning, through wider mazes of the lamp-lit city. And at every street corner crush a child and leave her screaming. And still the figure had no face by which he might know it. Even in his dreams, it had no face, or one that confused him and melted before his eyes. And so it was that there sprang up and grew fast in the lawyer’s mind a strangely strong, almost too great, curiosity to see the features of the real Mr. Hyde. If he could but once set eyes on him, he thought the mystery would grow lighter and perhaps roll altogether away. That was the habit of mysterious things when well examined. He might see a reason for his friend’s strange preference or captivity (call it which you please) and even for the startling part of the will. At least it would be a face worth seeing: the face of a man who was without mercy. A face which had but to show itself to raise up, in the mind of the unimpressionable Enfield, a spirit of lasting hatred.

CHAPTER 2. Chapter 1（2）

From that time on, Mr. Utterson began to watch the door in the side street of shops. In the morning before office hours, at noon when business was busy and time was short, at night under the fogged city moon, in all lights and at all hours of being alone or among crowds, the lawyer could be found at his chosen spot.

“If he is Mr. Hyde,” he had thought, “I shall be Mr. Seek.”

And at last his patience was rewarded. It was a fine dry night; there was cold white ice in the air; the streets were as clean as a dance floor; the lamps, not moved by any wind, made a regular pattern of light and shadow. By ten, when the shops were closed, the side street was very lonely and, in spite of the low sound of London from all around, very silent. Small sounds carried far; sounds from inside the houses were clearly heard on both sides of the street; and the sound of any person coming near could be heard long before he arrived. Mr. Utterson had been at his post for some minutes when he noticed an odd, light step coming near. During his walks at night, he had long grown used to the strange effect with which the steps of a single person, while he is still a great way off, suddenly stand out clearly from the great low noise and sound of the city. Yet his attention had never before been so sharply and firmly caught; and it was with a strong, fearful feeling that he would succeed that he moved back into the entrance of the court.

The steps came quickly nearer, and suddenly grew louder as they turned the end of the street. The lawyer, looking out from the entrance, could soon see what kind of man he had to deal with. He was small and very simply dressed, and the look of him, even at that distance, went somehow strongly against the wish of the one watching. But he came straight for the door, crossing the street to save time; and as he came, he took a key from his pocket like a man coming home.

Mr. Utterson stepped out and touched him on the shoulder as he passed. “Mr. Hyde, I think?”

Mr. Hyde pulled back with a sharp breath. But his fear was only for a moment; and though he did not look the lawyer in the face, he answered quietly enough: “That is my name. What do you want?”

“I see you are going in,” returned the lawyer. “I am an old friend of Dr. Jekyll’s—Mr. Utterson of Gaunt Street—you must have heard my name; and meeting you so easily, I thought you might let me in.”

“You will not find Dr. Jekyll; he is not at home,” replied Mr. Hyde, blowing on the key. And then suddenly, but still without looking up, “How did you know me?” he asked.

“On your side,” said Mr. Utterson, “will you do me a kindness?”

“With pleasure,” replied the other. “What shall it be?”

“Will you let me see your face?” asked the lawyer.

Mr. Hyde seemed to wait and think, and then, as if upon some sudden thought, turned around with an air of challenge; and the two stared at each other quite fixedly for a few seconds. “Now I shall know you again,” said Mr. Utterson. “It may be useful.”

“Yes,” returned Mr. Hyde, “it is as well we have met; and by the way, you should have my address.” And he gave a number of a street in Soho.

“Good God!” thought Mr. Utterson, “can he, too, have been thinking of the will?” But he kept his feelings to himself and only made a small sound to show he had heard the address.

“And now,” said the other, “how did you know me?”

“By description,” was the reply.

“Whose description?”

“We have common friends,” said Mr. Utterson.

“Common friends?” echoed Mr. Hyde, a little in a rough voice. “Who are they?”

“Jekyll, for instance,” said the lawyer.

“He never told you,” cried Mr. Hyde, with a flush of anger. “I did not think you would have lied.”

“Come,” said Mr. Utterson, “that is not fitting language.”

The other gave a loud angry laugh; and the next moment, with extraordinary speed, he had opened the door and disappeared into the house.

The lawyer stood for a while when Mr. Hyde had left him, the picture of unease. Then he began slowly to walk up the street, pausing every step or two and putting his hand to his brow like a man in mental confusion. The problem he was thus debating as he walked, was one of a class that is rarely solved. Mr. Hyde was pale and small like a dwarf, he gave an impression of ugliness without any nameable bad shape, he had a displeasing smile, he had borne himself to the lawyer with a sort of murderous mixture of shyness and boldness, and he spoke with a rough, whispering and somewhat broken voice; all these were points against him, but not all of these together could explain the until now unknown disgust, hate, and fear with which Mr. Utterson regarded him. “There must be something else,” said the confused gentleman. “There is something more, if I could find a name for it. God bless me, the man seems hardly human! Something like a cave man, shall we say? or can it be the old story of Dr. Fell? or is it the mere light of a foul soul that thus shines through, and changes, its clay body? The last, I think; for, O my poor old Harry Jekyll, if ever I read Satan’s signature upon a face, it is on that of your new friend.”

Round the corner from the side street, there was a square of ancient, handsome houses, now for the most part decayed from their high estate and let in flats and rooms to all sorts and conditions of men: map-engravers, architects, shady lawyers, and the agents of obscure enterprises. One house, however, second from the corner, was still occupied entire; and at the door of this, which wore a great air of wealth and comfort, though it was now plunged in darkness except for the fan-light, Mr. Utterson stopped and knocked. A well-dressed, elderly servant opened the door.

“Is Dr. Jekyll at home, Poole?” asked the lawyer.

“I will see, Mr. Utterson,” said Poole, admitting the visitor, as he spoke, into a large, low-roofed, comfortable hall, paved with flags, warmed (after the fashion of a country house) by a bright, open fire, and furnished with costly cabinets of oak. “Will you wait here by the fire, sir? or shall I give you a light in the dining room?”

“Here, thank you,” said the lawyer. He came closer and leaned on the tall fire guard. This hall, where he was now left alone, was a favorite spot of his friend the doctor. Utterson himself used to call it the pleasantest room in London. But tonight he felt a chill in his blood. The face of Hyde lay heavy on his memory. He felt a sick dislike of life, which was rare for him. In his low spirits, he seemed to see a threat in the firelight dancing on the polished cabinets. He also saw the shadow on the roof moving in a restless way. He was ashamed of his relief when Poole soon returned to say that Dr. Jekyll was gone out.

“I saw Mr. Hyde go in by the old cutting-room door, Poole,” he said. “Is that right, when Dr. Jekyll is from home?”

“Quite right, Mr. Utterson, sir,” replied the servant. “Mr. Hyde has a key.”

“Your master seems to place a great deal of trust in that young man, Poole,” the other went on, thinking aloud.

“Yes, sir, he does indeed,” said Poole. “We have all orders to obey him.”

“I do not think I ever met Mr. Hyde?” asked Utterson.

“O, dear no, sir. He never _dines_ here,” replied the butler. “Indeed we see very little of him on this side of the house. He mostly comes and goes by the laboratory.”

“Well, good-night, Poole.”

“Good-night, Mr. Utterson.”

And the lawyer set out for home with a very heavy heart. “Poor Harry Jekyll,” he thought, “my mind tells me he is in deep waters! He was wild when he was young. That was a long while ago, to be sure. But in the law of God, there is no time limit. Yes, it must be that. The ghost of some old sin, the cancer of some hidden shame: punishment coming, _pede claudo_, years after memory has forgotten and self-love has forgiven the fault.” The lawyer was scared by the thought. He thought for a while about his own past. He searched every corner of his memory, in case some Jack-in-the-Box of an old sin should suddenly jump out. His past was fairly clean. Few men could read the record of their life with less fear. Yet he was brought low by the many bad things he had done. And he was lifted up again into a calm and fearful thanks by the many bad things he had almost done, yet avoided. Then he returned to his earlier subject, and a spark of hope came to him. “This Master Hyde, if he were studied,” he thought, “must have secrets of his own. Black secrets, by the look of him. Secrets compared to which poor Jekyll’s worst would be like sunshine. Things cannot continue as they are. It turns me cold to think of this creature stealing like a thief to Harry’s bedside. Poor Harry, what a wakening! And the danger of it. For if this Hyde suspects the existence of the will, he may grow impatient to inherit. Yes, I must put my shoulder to the wheel if Jekyll will but let me,” he added, “if Jekyll will only let me.” For once more he saw before his mind’s eye, as clear as a transparency, the strange clauses of the will.

DR. JEKYLL WAS QUITE AT EASE

A fortnight later, by excellent good fortune, the doctor gave one of his pleasant dinners to some five or six old friends. They were all intelligent, respected men and all judges of good wine. Mr. Utterson so arranged it that he stayed behind after the others had left. This was no new arrangement. It was a thing that had happened many scores of times. Where Utterson was liked, he was liked well. Hosts loved to keep the dry lawyer back when the light-hearted and the loose-tongued had already their foot on the threshold. They liked to sit a while in his quiet company, practising for solitude. They liked to calm their minds in the man’s rich silence after the expense and strain of gaiety. To this rule, Dr. Jekyll was no exception. He now sat on the opposite side of the fire—a large, well-made, smooth-faced man of fifty, with something of a slyish cast perhaps, but every mark of capacity and kindness. You could see by his looks that he held for Mr. Utterson a sincere and warm affection.

“I have been wanting to speak to you, Jekyll,” began the latter. “You know that will of yours?”

A close observer might have gathered that the topic was unpleasant. But the doctor carried it off gaily. “My poor Utterson,” said he, “you are unfortunate in such a client. I never saw a man so distressed as you were by my will. Unless it were that hide-bound pedant, Lanyon, at what he called my scientific heresies. Oh, I know he’s a good fellow—you needn’t frown—an excellent fellow, and I always mean to see more of him. But a hide-bound pedant for all that. An ignorant, blatant pedant. I was never more disappointed in any man than Lanyon.”

“You know I never approved of it,” pursued Utterson, ruthlessly disregarding the fresh topic.

“My will? Yes, certainly, I know that,” said the doctor, a trifle sharply. “You have told me so.”

“Well, I tell you so again,” continued the lawyer. “I have been learning something of young Hyde.”

The large handsome face of Dr. Jekyll grew pale to the very lips. A blackness came about his eyes. “I do not care to hear more,” said he. “This is a matter I thought we had agreed to drop.”

“What I heard was abominable,” said Utterson.

“It can make no change. You do not understand my position,” returned the doctor, with a certain incoherency of manner. “I am painfully situated, Utterson. My position is a very strange—a very strange one. It is one of those affairs that cannot be mended by talking.”

“Jekyll,” said Utterson, “you know me. I am a man to be trusted. Make a clean breast of this in confidence. And I make no doubt I can get you out of it.”

“My good Utterson,” said the doctor, “this is very good of you, this is downright good of you, and I cannot find words to thank you in. I believe you fully. I would trust you before any man alive, yes, before myself, if I could make the choice. But indeed it isn’t what you fancy. It is not so bad as that. And just to put your good heart at rest, I will tell you one thing: the moment I choose, I can be rid of Mr. Hyde. I give you my hand upon that. And I thank you again and again. And I will just add one little word, Utterson, that I’m sure you’ll take in good part: this is a private matter, and I beg of you to let it sleep.”

Utterson reflected a little, looking in the fire.

“I have no doubt you are perfectly right,” he said at last, getting to his feet.

"Well, but since we have talked about this business, and for the last time I hope," continued the doctor, "there is one point I want you to understand. I have a very great interest in poor Hyde. I know you have seen him. He told me so. And I fear he was rude. But I do truly take a great, a very great interest in that young man. And if I am taken away, Utterson, I want you to promise me that you will be patient with him and get his rights for him. I think you would, if you knew all. And it would be a weight off my mind if you would promise."

"I cannot pretend that I shall ever like him," said the lawyer.

"I do not ask that," pleaded Jekyll, laying his hand upon the other man's arm. "I only ask for justice. I only ask you to help him for my sake, when I am no longer here."

Utterson gave a sigh he could not hold back. "Well," he said, "I promise."

THE CAREW MURDER CASE

Nearly a year later, in the month of October, 18——, London was shocked by a crime of strange cruelty. It was made even more notable by the high position of the victim. The details were few and shocking. A female servant living alone in a house not far from the river had gone upstairs to bed about eleven. A fog rolled over the city in the small hours, but the early part of the night was clear. The lane, which the servant's window overlooked, was lit brightly by the full moon. It seems she was romantically given, for she sat down upon her box, which stood right under the window, and fell into a dream of thinking. Never (she used to say, with streaming tears, when she told that experience), never had she felt more at peace with all men or thought more warmly of the world. And as she sat there she became aware of an aged and beautiful gentleman with white hair, drawing near along the lane. Advancing to meet him was another and very small gentleman, to whom at first she paid less attention. When they had come within speech (which was just under the servant's eyes) the older man bowed and greeted the other with a very pretty manner of politeness. It did not seem as if the subject of his address were of great importance. Indeed, from his pointing, it sometimes appeared as if he were only asking his way. But the moon shone on his face as he spoke, and the girl was pleased to watch it. It seemed to breathe such an innocent and old-world warmth of character, yet with something high too, as of a well-founded self-content. Presently her eye wandered to the other, and she was surprised to know in him a certain Mr. Hyde, who had once visited her master and for whom she had felt a dislike. He had in his hand a heavy cane, with which he was playing. But he answered never a word, and seemed to listen with an impatience he could not hide. And then all of a sudden he broke out in a great flame of anger, stamping with his foot, waving the cane, and carrying on (as the servant described it) like a madman. The old gentleman took a step back, with the air of one very much surprised and a little hurt. And at that Mr. Hyde broke out of all limits and clubbed him to the earth. And next moment, with ape-like anger, he was trampling his victim under foot and calling down a storm of blows. Under these the bones were heard to break and the body jumped upon the road. At the horror of these sights and sounds, the servant fainted.

It was two o'clock when she came to herself and called for the police. The murderer was gone long ago. But there lay his victim in the middle of the lane, incredibly mangled. The stick with which the deed had been done, although it was of some rare and very tough and heavy wood, had broken in the middle under the stress of this senseless cruelty. And one splintered half had rolled in the neighbouring gutter—the other, without doubt, had been carried away by the murderer. A purse and a gold watch were found upon the victim. But there were no cards or papers, except a sealed and stamped envelope, which he had probably been carrying to the post, and which bore the name and address of Mr. Utterson.

This was brought to the lawyer the next morning, before he was out of bed. And he had no sooner seen it, and been told the circumstances, than he shot out a solemn lip. "I shall say nothing till I have seen the body," said he. "This may be very serious. Please wait while I dress." And with the same grave face he hurried through his breakfast and drove to the police station, where the body had been carried. As soon as he came into the cell, he nodded.

"Yes," said he, "I know him. I am sorry to say that this is Sir Danvers Carew."

"Good God, sir," exclaimed the officer, "is it possible?" And the next moment his eye lighted up with professional ambition. "This will make a deal of noise," he said. "And perhaps you can help us to the man." And he briefly told what the servant had seen, and showed the broken stick.

Mr. Utterson had already felt fear at the name of Hyde. But when the stick was laid before him, he could doubt no longer. Broken and battered as it was, he knew it for one that he had himself presented many years before to Henry Jekyll.

"Is this Mr. Hyde a person of small height?" he asked.

"Particularly small and particularly wicked-looking, is what the servant calls him," said the officer.

Mr. Utterson thought for a moment. And then, raising his head, "If you will come with me in my cab," he said, "I think I can take you to his house."

By this time it was about nine in the morning. It was the first fog of the season. A great chocolate-colored cloud hung over the sky. But the wind kept pushing and breaking up these fighting clouds. The cab moved slowly from street to street. Mr. Utterson saw a wonderful number of shades and colors of half-light. Here it would be dark like the end of the evening. There would be a glow of a rich, strange brown, like the light of some strange big fire. Here, for a moment, the fog would break apart. A thin, tired beam of daylight would look in between the moving rings of fog. The sad part of Soho was seen under these changing glimpses. It had muddy streets and dirty-looking passengers. Its lamps had never been put out, or had been lit again to fight this sad return of darkness. In the lawyer's eyes, it seemed like a district of some city in a bad dream. His thoughts were also of the darkest kind. When he looked at the person riding with him, he felt some touch of that fear of the law and the law's officers. This fear may sometimes attack the most honest man.

As the cab stopped in front of the address given, the fog lifted a little. It showed him a dirty street, a bar, a cheap French eating-house, and a shop selling cheap magazines and cheap salads. Many children in torn clothes were crowded in the doorways. Many women of different countries were going out with keys in their hands to have a morning drink. Then the fog came down again on that part, as brown as dark earth, and cut him off from his bad surroundings. This was the home of Henry Jekyll's favorite; of a man who would inherit a quarter of a million pounds.

An old woman with a face like ivory and silver hair opened the door. She had an evil face, made smooth by false kindness, but her manners were excellent. Yes, she said, this was Mr. Hyde's, but he was not at home. He had come in very late that night, but had gone away again in less than an hour. There was nothing strange in that. His habits were very uneven, and he was often away. For example, it was nearly two months since she had seen him until yesterday.

"Very well, then, we wish to see his rooms," said the lawyer. When the woman began to say it was impossible, "I had better tell you who this person is," he added. "This is Inspector Newcomen of Scotland Yard."

A flash of hateful joy appeared on the woman's face. "Ah!" she said, "he is in trouble! What has he done?"

Mr. Utterson and the inspector looked at each other. "He does not seem a very popular person," said the inspector. "And now, my good woman, just let me and this gentleman have a look around."

In the whole house, which was empty except for the old woman, Mr. Hyde had used only a couple of rooms. But these were decorated with luxury and good taste. A small room was filled with wine. The plates were silver, the table cloths elegant. A good picture hung on the walls, a gift (as Utterson supposed) from Henry Jekyll, who knew much about art. The carpets were thick and pleasant in color. At this moment, however, the rooms showed every sign of having been recently and quickly searched. Clothes lay on the floor with their pockets turned inside out. Locked drawers stood open. On the fireplace there lay a pile of gray ashes, as though many papers had been burned. From these ashes the inspector dug out the burnt end of a green checkbook, which had survived the fire. The other half of the stick was found behind the door. As this confirmed his suspicions, the officer said he was delighted. A visit to the bank, where several thousand pounds were found in the murderer's account, completed his satisfaction.

"You may depend on it, sir," he told Mr. Utterson. "I have him in my hand. He must have lost his head, or he never would have left the stick or, above all, burned the checkbook. Why, money is life to the man. We have nothing to do but wait for him at the bank, and send out the handbills."

This last, however, was not so easy to do. Mr. Hyde had few friends. Even the master of the servant girl had only seen him twice. His family could not be traced anywhere. He had never been photographed. The few who could describe him differed widely, as ordinary observers will. They agreed on only one point: the troubling sense of hidden deformity that the fugitive left in the minds of those who saw him.

INCIDENT OF THE LETTER

It was late in the afternoon when Mr. Utterson found his way to Dr. Jekyll's door. Poole let him in at once and led him down through the kitchen offices and across a yard that had once been a garden. They came to the building known as the laboratory or the dissecting-rooms. The doctor had bought the house from the heirs of a famous surgeon. His own tastes were more chemical than anatomical, so he had changed the purpose of the block at the bottom of the garden. It was the first time the lawyer had been received in that part of his friend's quarters. He looked at the dirty, windowless structure with curiosity. He looked around with a distasteful sense of strangeness as he crossed the theatre, once crowded with eager students and now lying empty and silent. The tables were loaded with chemical equipment. The floor was covered with boxes and littered with packing straw. The light fell dimly through the foggy dome. At the far end, a flight of stairs led up to a door covered with red cloth. Through this, Mr. Utterson was at last received into the doctor's cabinet. It was a large room, fitted with glass presses. Among other things, it had a tall mirror and a business table. It looked out on the court through three dusty windows with iron bars. A fire burned in the grate. A lamp was lit on the chimney shelf, for even in the houses the fog began to lie thickly. There, close to the warmth, sat Dr. Jekyll, looking deathly sick. He did not rise to meet his visitor, but held out a cold hand and welcomed him in a changed voice.

"And now," said Mr. Utterson, as soon as Poole had left them, "you have heard the news?"

The doctor shuddered. "They were crying it in the square," he said. "I heard them in my dining-room."

"One word," said the lawyer. "Carew was my client, but so are you, and I want to know what I am doing. You have not been mad enough to hide this fellow?"

"Utterson, I swear to God," cried the doctor, "I swear to God I will never set eyes on him again. I bind my honor to you that I am done with him in this world. It is all at an end. And indeed he does not want my help. You do not know him as I do. He is safe, he is quite safe. Mark my words, he will never more be heard of."

The lawyer listened gloomily. He did not like his friend's feverish manner. "You seem pretty sure of him," said he. "And for your sake, I hope you may be right. If it came to a trial, your name might appear."

"I am quite sure of him," replied Jekyll. "I have grounds for certainty that I cannot share with anyone. But there is one thing on which you may advise me. I have—I have received a letter, and I am at a loss whether I should show it to the police. I should like to leave it in your hands, Utterson. You would judge wisely, I am sure. I have so great a trust in you."

"You fear, I suppose, that it might lead to his detection?" asked the lawyer.

“No,” said the other. “I cannot say I care what happens to Hyde. I am quite done with him. I was thinking of my own character. This hateful business has exposed it.”

Utterson thought for a while. He was surprised at his friend’s selfishness. Yet he felt relieved by it. “Well,” he said at last, “let me see the letter.”

The letter was written in an odd, upright hand and signed “Edward Hyde.” It said, briefly enough, that the writer’s benefactor, Dr. Jekyll, had long been repaid unworthily for a thousand generosities. But Dr. Jekyll need not fear for his safety. He had means of escape on which he placed a sure dependence. The lawyer liked this letter well enough. It put a better colour on the intimacy than he had looked for. And he blamed himself for some of his past suspicions.

“Have you the envelope?” he asked.

“I burned it,” replied Jekyll, “before I thought what I was about. But it bore no postmark. The note was handed in.”

“Shall I keep this and sleep upon it?” asked Utterson.

“I wish you to judge for me entirely,” was the reply. “I have lost confidence in myself.”

“Well, I shall consider,” returned the lawyer. “And now one word more. It was Hyde who dictated the terms in your will about that disappearance?”

The doctor seemed seized with a qualm of faintness. He shut his mouth tight and nodded.

“I knew it,” said Utterson. “He meant to murder you. You have had a fine escape.”

“I have had what is far more to the purpose,” returned the doctor solemnly. “I have had a lesson—O God, Utterson, what a lesson I have had!” And he covered his face for a moment with his hands.

On his way out, the lawyer stopped and had a word or two with Poole. “By the by,” said he, “there was a letter handed in to-day. What was the messenger like?” But Poole was positive nothing had come except by post. “And only circulars by that,” he added.

This news sent off the visitor with his fears renewed. Plainly the letter had come by the laboratory door. Possibly, indeed, it had been written in the cabinet. And if that were so, it must be differently judged, and handled with the more caution. The newsboys, as he went, were crying themselves hoarse along the footways: “Special edition. Shocking murder of an M. P.” That was the funeral oration of one friend and client. And he could not help a certain apprehension lest the good name of another should be sucked down in the eddy of the scandal. It was, at least, a ticklish decision that he had to make. And self-reliant as he was by habit, he began to cherish a longing for advice. It was not to be had directly. But perhaps, he thought, it might be fished for.

Presently after, he sat on one side of his own hearth, with Mr. Guest, his head clerk, upon the other. Midway between, at a nicely calculated distance from the fire, stood a bottle of a particular old wine that had long dwelt unsunned in the foundations of his house. The fog still slept on the wing above the drowned city, where the lamps glimmered like carbuncles. And through the muffle and smother of these fallen clouds, the procession of the town’s life was still rolling in through the great arteries with a sound as of a mighty wind. But the room was gay with firelight. In the bottle the acids were long ago resolved. The imperial dye had softened with time, as the colour grows richer in stained windows. And the glow of hot autumn afternoons on hillside vineyards was ready to be set free and to disperse the fogs of London. Insensibly the lawyer melted. There was no man from whom he kept fewer secrets than Mr. Guest. And he was not always sure that he kept as many as he meant. Guest had often been on business to the doctor’s. He knew Poole. He could scarce have failed to hear of Mr. Hyde’s familiarity about the house. He might draw conclusions. Was it not as well, then, that he should see a letter which put that mystery to rights? And above all since Guest, being a great student and critic of handwriting, would consider the step natural and obliging? The clerk, besides, was a man of counsel. He would scarce read so strange a document without dropping a remark. And by that remark Mr. Utterson might shape his future course.

“This is a sad business about Sir Danvers,” he said.

“Yes, sir, indeed. It has elicited a great deal of public feeling,” returned Guest. “The man, of course, was mad.”

“I should like to hear your views on that,” replied Utterson. “I have a document here in his handwriting. It is between ourselves, for I scarce know what to do about it. It is an ugly business at the best. But there it is—quite in your way, a murderer’s autograph.”

Guest’s eyes brightened, and he sat down at once and studied it with passion. “No, sir,” he said. “Not mad. But it is an odd hand.”

“And by all accounts a very odd writer,” added the lawyer.

Just then the servant entered with a note.

“Is that from Dr. Jekyll, sir?” inquired the clerk. “I thought I knew the writing. Anything private, Mr. Utterson?”

“Only an invitation to dinner. Why? Do you want to see it?”

“One moment. I thank you, sir.” And the clerk laid the two sheets of paper alongside and sedulously compared their contents. “Thank you, sir,” he said at last, returning both. “It’s a very interesting autograph.”

There was a pause, during which Mr. Utterson struggled with himself. “Why did you compare them, Guest?” he inquired suddenly.

“Well, sir,” returned the clerk, “there’s a rather singular resemblance. The two hands are in many points identical. Only differently sloped.”

“Rather quaint,” said Utterson.

“It is, as you say, rather quaint,” returned Guest.

“I wouldn’t speak of this note, you know,” said the master.

“No, sir,” said the clerk. “I understand.”

But no sooner was Mr. Utterson alone that night than he locked the note into his safe, where it reposed from that time forward. “What!” he thought. “Henry Jekyll forge for a murderer!” And his blood ran cold in his veins.

REMARKABLE INCIDENT OF DR. LANYON

Time passed. Thousands of pounds were offered as a reward. The death of Sir Danvers was seen as a public injury. But Mr. Hyde had disappeared from the police as if he had never existed. Much of his past was dug up, and all of it was bad. Stories came out about the man's cruelty, which was both heartless and violent. There were stories of his evil life, his strange friends, and the hatred that seemed to surround his career. But about where he was now, there was not a whisper. From the time he left the house in Soho on the morning of the murder, he was simply wiped out. Slowly, as time went on, Mr. Utterson began to recover from the heat of his alarm. He grew calmer. In his view, the death of Sir Danvers was more than paid for by the disappearance of Mr. Hyde. Now that the evil influence was gone, a new life began for Dr. Jekyll. He came out of his hiding. He renewed relations with his friends. He became their familiar guest and host once more. He had always been known for his charities, and now he was also known for his religion. He was busy. He was often outside. He did good. His face seemed to open and become brighter, as if he felt a deep sense of service inside. For more than two months, the doctor was at peace.

On the 8th of January, Utterson had dinner at the doctor's house with a small group. Lanyon had been there. The host's face looked from one to the other, as in the old days when the three were close friends. On the 12th, and again on the 14th, the door was shut to the lawyer. "The doctor is kept inside the house," Poole said, "and sees no one." On the 15th, he tried again, and was again refused. He had been used to seeing his friend almost daily for the last two months. This return of being alone weighed on his spirits. On the fifth night, he had Guest to dinner with him. On the sixth, he went to Dr. Lanyon's.

There at least he was not refused entry. But when he came in, he was shocked at the change in the doctor's appearance. His death was written clearly on his face. The rosy man had grown pale. His flesh had fallen away. He was clearly balder and older. Yet it was not so much these signs of quick physical decline that caught the lawyer's notice. It was a look in his eye and a quality in his manner that seemed to show some deep terror of the mind. It was unlikely that the doctor should fear death. And yet that was what Utterson was tempted to suspect. "Yes," he thought. "He is a doctor. He must know his own state and that his days are counted. And the knowledge is more than he can bear." And yet when Utterson remarked on his ill looks, it was with an air of greatness that Lanyon declared himself a doomed man.

"I have had a shock," he said, "and I shall never recover. It is a question of weeks. Well, life has been pleasant. I liked it. Yes, sir, I used to like it. I sometimes think if we knew all, we should be more glad to get away."

"Jekyll is ill, too," observed Utterson. "Have you seen him?"

But Lanyon's face changed, and he held up a trembling hand. "I wish to see or hear no more of Dr. Jekyll," he said in a loud, unsteady voice. "I am quite done with that person. And I beg that you will spare me any allusion to one whom I regard as dead."

"Tut-tut," said Mr. Utterson. Then after a long pause, "Can't I do anything?" he asked. "We are three very old friends, Lanyon. We shall not live to make others."

"Nothing can be done," returned Lanyon. "Ask himself."

"He will not see me," said the lawyer.

"I am not surprised at that," was the reply. "Some day, Utterson, after I am dead, you may perhaps come to learn the right and wrong of this. I cannot tell you. And in the meantime, if you can sit and talk with me of other things, for God's sake, stay and do so. But if you cannot keep clear of this accursed topic, then, in God's name, go, for I cannot bear it."

As soon as he got home, Utterson sat down and wrote to Jekyll. He complained about being kept out of the house. He asked the cause of this unhappy break with Lanyon. The next day brought him a long answer. It was often very sad in its wording, and sometimes darkly mysterious in its meaning. The quarrel with Lanyon was incurable. "I do not blame our old friend," Jekyll wrote, "but I share his view that we must never meet. I mean from now on to lead a life of extreme hiding. You must not be surprised, nor must you doubt my friendship, if my door is often shut even to you. You must let me go my own dark way. I have brought on myself a punishment and a danger that I cannot name. If I am the chief of sinners, I am the chief of sufferers also. I could not think that this earth contained a place for sufferings and terrors so unmanning. And you can do but one thing, Utterson, to lighten this fate, and that is to respect my silence." Utterson was amazed. The dark influence of Hyde was gone. The doctor had returned to his old tasks and friendships. A week ago, the future had smiled with every promise of a cheerful and honoured old age. And now in a moment, friendship, and peace of mind, and the whole course of his life were wrecked. So great and unprepared a change pointed to madness. But in view of Lanyon's manner and words, there must lie for it some deeper ground.

A week later, Dr. Lanyon went to bed sick. In less than two weeks, he was dead. The funeral was very sad, and it deeply affected Utterson. The night after the funeral, Utterson locked the door of his business room. He sat there by the light of a sad candle. He took out an envelope addressed by his dead friend's hand and sealed with his seal. He set it before him. "PRIVATE: for the hands of G. J. Utterson ALONE and in case of his death _to be destroyed unread_," it said in strong letters on the outside. The lawyer was afraid to see what was inside. "I have buried one friend today," he thought. "What if this should cost me another?" Then he called his fear a betrayal of his friend, and he broke the seal. Inside there was another envelope, also sealed. On the cover it said: "not to be opened till the death or vanishing of Dr. Henry Jekyll." Utterson could not believe his eyes. Yes, it said vanishing. Here again, as in the mad will he had long ago given back to its writer, here again were the idea of a vanishing and the name of Henry Jekyll joined together. But in the will, that idea had come from the evil hint of the man Hyde. It was put there with a purpose that was too clear and too terrible. Written by Lanyon's hand, what should it mean? A great curiosity came over the trustee. He wanted to ignore the ban and dive at once to the bottom of these mysteries. But his duty to his work and his faith to his dead friend were strict obligations. So the packet slept in the deepest corner of his private safe.

INCIDENT AT THE WINDOW

It happened on Sunday, when Mr. Utterson was on his usual walk with Mr. Enfield, that their way lay once again through the side street. When they came in front of the door, both stopped to look at it.

“Well,” said Enfield, “that story is at an end at least. We shall never see more of Mr. Hyde.”

“I hope not,” said Utterson. “Did I ever tell you that I once saw him, and shared your feeling of disgust?”

“It was impossible to do one without the other,” returned Enfield. “And by the way, what a fool you must have thought me, not to know that this was a back way to Dr. Jekyll’s! It was partly your own fault that I found it out, even when I did.”

“So you found it out, did you?” said Utterson. “But if that is so, we may step into the court and take a look at the windows. To tell you the truth, I am worried about poor Jekyll. Even outside, I feel as if the presence of a friend might do him good.”

The court was very cool and a little damp, and full of early dusk, although the sky, high up overhead, was still bright with sunset. The middle one of the three windows was half open. Sitting close beside it, taking the air with a great sadness in his face, like some hopeless prisoner, Utterson saw Dr. Jekyll.

“What! Jekyll!” he cried. “I trust you are better.”

“I am very low, Utterson,” replied the doctor, sadly, “very low. It will not last long, thank God.”

“You stay too much inside,” said the lawyer. “You should be out, getting your blood moving like Mr. Enfield and me. (This is my cousin—Mr. Enfield—Dr. Jekyll.) Come, now; get your hat and take a quick walk with us.”

“You are very good,” sighed the other. “I should like to very much; but no, no, no, it is quite impossible; I dare not. But indeed, Utterson, I am very glad to see you; this is really a great pleasure. I would ask you and Mr. Enfield up, but the place is really not fit.”

“Why then,” said the lawyer, in a friendly way, “the best thing we can do is to stay down here and speak with you from where we are.”

“That is just what I was about to suggest,” returned the doctor with a smile. But the words were hardly spoken before the smile was struck from his face. It was replaced by an expression of such deep terror and despair that it froze the blood of the two gentlemen below. They saw it but for a moment, for the window was instantly pulled down. But that moment had been enough, and they turned and left the court without a word. In silence, too, they went through the side street. It was not until they had come into a nearby main street, where even on a Sunday there were still some signs of life, that Mr. Utterson at last turned and looked at his companion. They were both pale, and there was an answering horror in their eyes.

“God forgive us, God forgive us,” said Mr. Utterson.

But Mr. Enfield only nodded his head very seriously and walked on once more in silence.

THE LAST NIGHT

Mr. Utterson was sitting by his fire one evening after dinner, when he was surprised to receive a visit from Poole.

“Bless me, Poole, what brings you here?” he cried; and then taking a second look at him, “What is wrong with you?” he added; “is the doctor ill?”

“Mr. Utterson,” said the man, “there is something wrong.”

“Take a seat, and here is a glass of wine for you,” said the lawyer. “Now, take your time, and tell me clearly what you want.”

“You know the doctor’s ways, sir,” replied Poole, “and how he shuts himself up. Well, he is shut up again in the room above the laboratory, and I do not like it, sir—I wish I may die if I like it. Mr. Utterson, sir, I am afraid.”

“Now, my good man,” said the lawyer, “be clear. What are you afraid of?”

“I have been afraid for about a week,” returned Poole, stubbornly ignoring the question, “and I can bear it no more.”

The man’s appearance fully supported his words. His manner was changed for the worse. Except for the moment when he had first announced his terror, he had not once looked the lawyer in the face. Even now, he sat with the glass of wine untasted on his knee, and his eyes directed to a corner of the floor. “I can bear it no more,” he repeated.

“Come,” said the lawyer, “I see you have some good reason, Poole; I see there is something seriously wrong. Try to tell me what it is.”

“I think there has been foul play,” said Poole, hoarsely.

“Foul play!” cried the lawyer, a good deal frightened and rather inclined to be irritated as a result. “What foul play? What does the man mean?”

“I dare not say, sir,” was the answer; “but will you come along with me and see for yourself?”

Mr. Utterson’s only answer was to rise and get his hat and great-coat. But he observed with wonder the greatness of the relief that appeared upon the butler’s face. And perhaps he observed with no less wonder that the wine was still untasted when he set it down to follow.

It was a wild, cold night in March. A pale moon lay on her back, as if the wind had turned her over. Thin, light clouds crossed the sky. The wind made talking difficult and brought blood to the face. It seemed to have swept the streets unusually empty of people. Mr. Utterson thought he had never seen that part of London so empty. He wished it were different. DR. LANYON’S NARRATIVE

On the ninth of January, now four days ago, I received by the evening delivery a registered envelope. It was addressed in the hand of my colleague and old school-friend, Henry Jekyll. I was very surprised by this. We were by no means in the habit of writing to each other. I had seen the man, dined with him, indeed, the night before. And I could imagine nothing in our dealings that should justify the formality of registration. The contents increased my wonder. For this is how the letter ran:

“10th December, 18——

“Dear Lanyon,—You are one of my oldest friends. And although we may have differed at times on scientific questions, I cannot remember, at least on my side, any break in our affection. There was never a day when, if you had said to me, ‘Jekyll, my life, my honour, my reason, depend upon you,’ I would not have sacrificed my left hand to help you. Lanyon, my life, my honour, my reason, are all at your mercy. If you fail me to-night I am lost. You might suppose, after this preface, that I am going to ask you for something dishonourable to grant. Judge for yourself.

“I want you to postpone all other engagements for to-night—ay, even if you were summoned to the bedside of an emperor. Take a cab, unless your carriage should be actually at the door. And with this letter in your hand for consultation, drive straight to my house. Poole, my butler, has his orders. You will find him waiting your arrival with a locksmith. The door of my cabinet is then to be forced. And you are to go in alone. Open the glazed press (letter E) on the left hand, breaking the lock if it be shut. And draw out, _with all its contents as they stand_, the fourth drawer from the top or (which is the same thing) the third from the bottom. In my extreme distress of mind, I have a morbid fear of misdirecting you. But even if I am in error, you may know the right drawer by its contents: some powders, a phial and a paper book. This drawer I beg of you to carry back with you to Cavendish Square exactly as it stands.

“That is the first part of the service. Now for the second. You should be back, if you set out at once on the receipt of this, long before midnight. But I will leave you that amount of margin, not only in the fear of one of those obstacles that can neither be prevented nor foreseen, but because an hour when your servants are in bed is to be preferred for what will then remain to do. At midnight, then, I have to ask you to be alone in your consulting-room. Admit with your own hand into the house a man who will present himself in my name. And place in his hands the drawer that you will have brought with you from my cabinet. Then you will have played your part and earned my gratitude completely. Five minutes afterwards, if you insist upon an explanation, you will have understood that these arrangements are of capital importance. And that by the neglect of one of them, fantastic as they must appear, you might have charged your conscience with my death or the shipwreck of my reason.

“Confident as I am that you will not trifle with this appeal, my heart sinks and my hand trembles at the bare thought of such a possibility. Think of me at this hour, in a strange place, labouring under a blackness of distress that no fancy can exaggerate. And yet well aware that, if you will but punctually serve me, my troubles will roll away like a story that is told. Serve me, my dear Lanyon, and save

“Your friend, “H. J.”

“P. S. I had already sealed this up when a fresh fear struck upon my soul. It is possible that the postoffice may fail me, and this letter not come into your hands until to-morrow morning. In that case, dear Lanyon, do my errand when it shall be most convenient for you in the course of the day; and once more expect my messenger at midnight. After I read this letter, I was sure my colleague was insane. But until that was proved beyond any doubt, I felt I had to do what he asked. The less I understood of this farrago, the less I could judge its importance. A request worded like that could not be ignored without serious responsibility. So I got up from the table, got into a hansom, and drove straight to Jekyll’s house. The butler was waiting for my arrival. He had received a registered letter of instruction by the same post as mine. He had sent at once for a locksmith and a carpenter. The tradesmen came while we were still talking. But this last man was a handy fellow, and after two hours’ work, the door stood open. The press marked E was unlocked. Here I went on to examine its contents. The powders were neatly made up, but not with the nicety of the dispensing chemist. So it was plain they were of Jekyll’s private manufacture. When I opened one of the wrappers, I found what seemed to me a simple crystalline salt of a white colour. I next turned my attention to the phial. It might have been about half-full of a blood-red liquor. The smell was highly pungent and seemed to me to contain phosphorus and some volatile ether. I could make no guess at the other ingredients. The book was an ordinary version-book and contained little but a series of dates. These covered a period of many years, but I observed that the entries ceased nearly a year ago and quite abruptly. Here and there a brief remark was appended to a date, usually no more than a single word. “Double” occurred perhaps six times in a total of several hundred entries. Once, very early in the list and followed by several marks of exclamation, was “total failure!!!” All this whetted my curiosity, but it told me little that was definite. He jumped to it, and then stopped, and put his hand on his heart. I could hear his teeth rub together as his jaws moved by themselves. His face was so pale and strange to see that I became afraid both for his life and his mind.

“Calm yourself,” I said.

He turned a terrible smile to me, and as if with the decision of despair, pulled away the sheet. At the sight of what was inside, he gave one loud cry of such immense relief that I sat frozen. And the next moment, in a voice that was already fairly well under control, “Do you have a measured glass?” he asked.

I rose from my place with some effort and gave him what he asked for.

He thanked me with a smiling nod, measured out a few small drops of the red liquid and added one of the powders. The mixture, which was at first of a red color, began, as the crystals melted, to become brighter in color, to make small bubbles with a sound, and to give off small amounts of gas. Suddenly and at the same moment, the boiling stopped and the compound changed to a dark purple, which faded again more slowly to a pale green. My visitor, who had watched these changes with a sharp eye, smiled, set down the glass upon the table, and then turned and looked at me with a searching look.

“And now,” he said, “to settle what remains. Will you be wise? Will you be guided? Will you let me take this glass in my hand and go out from your house without more talk? Or does the hunger for curiosity have too much power over you? Think before you answer, for it shall be done as you decide. As you decide, you shall be left as you were before, and neither richer nor wiser, unless the sense of service done to a man in deadly distress may be counted as a kind of riches of the soul. Or, if you prefer to choose so, a new area of knowledge and new roads to fame and power shall be opened to you, here, in this room, at once. And your sight shall be struck by a wonder that would shake the disbelief of Satan.”

“Sir,” I said, pretending a calm that I was far from truly having, “you speak in riddles, and you will perhaps not wonder that I hear you with no very strong feeling of belief. But I have gone too far in the way of strange services to stop before I see the end.”

“It is well,” replied my visitor. “Lanyon, you remember your vows: what follows is under the seal of our profession. And now, you who have so long been tied to the most narrow and material views, you who have denied the power of transcendental medicine, you who have laughed at your superiors—look!”

He put the glass to his lips and drank at one swallow. A cry followed. He reeled, swayed, caught at the table and held on, staring with bloodshot eyes, gasping with open mouth. And as I looked there came, I thought, a change—he seemed to swell—his face became suddenly black and the features seemed to melt and alter—and the next moment, I had jumped to my feet and leaped back against the wall, my arm raised to shield me from that wonder, my mind drowned in terror.

“O God!” I screamed, and “O God!” again and again; for there before my eyes—pale and shaken, and half-fainting, and feeling before him with his hands, like a man restored from death—there stood Henry Jekyll!

What he told me in the next hour, I cannot bring my mind to set on paper. I saw what I saw, I heard what I heard, and my soul sickened at it. And yet now when that sight has faded from my eyes, I ask myself if I believe it, and I cannot answer. My life is shaken to its roots. Sleep has left me. The deadliest terror sits by me at all hours of the day and night. I feel that my days are numbered, and that I must die. And yet I shall die in disbelief. As for the moral evil that man unveiled to me, even with tears of regret, I cannot, even in memory, dwell on it without a start of horror. I will say but one thing, Utterson, and that (if you can bring your mind to believe it) will be more than enough. The creature who crept into my house that night was, on Jekyll’s own confession, known by the name of Hyde and hunted for in every corner of the land as the murderer of Carew.

HASTIE LANYON

HENRY JEKYLL’S FULL STATEMENT OF THE CASE

I was born in the year 18—— to a large fortune. I was also given excellent abilities. By nature I was likely to work hard. I liked the respect of the wise and good among my fellow men. And so, as might have been supposed, I had every promise of a future full of honor and fame. And indeed the worst of my faults was a certain quick and happy character. This has made the happiness of many. But I found it hard to fit with my proud desire to carry my head high. I also wanted to wear a more than commonly serious face before the public. Hence it came about that I hid my pleasures. And when I reached years of thought, I began to look around me. I began to take stock of my progress and position in the world. I stood already committed to a deep double life. Many a man would have even shown off such faults as I was guilty of. But from the high views that I had set before me, I regarded and hid them with an almost sick sense of shame. It was thus rather the demanding nature of my hopes than any particular low point in my faults that made me what I was. And with even a deeper gap than in most men, it cut apart in me those areas of good and ill. These divide and mix the two-sided nature of man. In this case, I was driven to think deeply and always on that hard law of life. This law lies at the root of religion and is one of the most common springs of pain. Though so deep a double-dealer, I was in no sense a false person. Both sides of me were in dead earnest. I was no more myself when I laid aside restraint and plunged in shame, than when I worked, in the eye of day, at the support of knowledge or the relief of pain and suffering. And it chanced that the direction of my scientific studies led wholly toward the mystic and the transcendental. This acted back and shed a strong light on this awareness of the endless war among my parts. With every day, and from both sides of my intelligence, the moral and the intellectual, I thus drew steadily nearer to that truth. By its partial discovery I have been doomed to such a dreadful shipwreck: that man is not truly one, but truly two. I say two, because the state of my own knowledge does not pass beyond that point. Others will follow, others will outstrip me on the same lines. And I risk the guess that man will be ultimately known for a mere state of many, mismatched, and independent dwellers. I, for my part, from the nature of my life, moved without fail in one direction and in one direction only. It was on the moral side, and in my own person, that I learned to see the thorough and early two-sidedness of man. I saw that, of the two natures that fought in the field of my mind, even if I could rightly be said to be either, it was only because I was radically both. And from an early date, even before the course of my scientific discoveries had begun to suggest the most bare possibility of such a miracle, I had learned to dwell with pleasure on the thought of the separation of these elements. I dwelled on it as a beloved day-dream. If each, I told myself, could but be housed in separate identities, life would be relieved of all that was unbearable. The unjust might go his way, delivered from the hopes and remorse of his more upright twin. And the just could walk steadily and safely on his upward path. He could do the good things in which he found his pleasure. He would no longer be exposed to disgrace and regret by the hands of this outside evil. It was the curse of mankind that these mismatched sticks were thus tied together. In the pained womb of mind, these polar twins should be continuously struggling. How, then, were they separated?

I was so far in my thoughts when, as I have said, a side-light began to shine upon the subject from the laboratory table. I began to see more deeply than it has ever yet been stated the shaking immateriality of this seemingly so solid body in which we walk dressed. I also saw its mist-like briefness. Certain agents I found to have the power to shake and to pull back that fleshly garment. Even as a wind might toss the curtains of a tent. For two good reasons, I will not enter deeply into this scientific branch of my confession. First, because I have been made to learn that the doom and burden of our life is tied for ever on the shoulders of man. When the attempt is made to cast it off, it but returns upon us with more unfamiliar and more awful pressure. Second, because, as my story will make, alas! too clear, my discoveries were incomplete. Enough, then, that I not only saw my natural body for the mere aura and shine of certain of the powers that made up my spirit. I also managed to mix a drug by which these powers should be taken down from their rule. A second form and face would be put in place. They were none the less natural to me because they were the expression, and bore the stamp, of lower elements in my soul.

I waited long before I put this theory to the test of practice. I knew well that I risked death. Any drug that so strongly controlled and shook the very fortress of identity might utterly blot out that immaterial tent which I looked to it to change. This could happen by the least small bit of an overdose or at the least wrong moment in the moment of use. But the temptation of a discovery so singular and deep at last overcame the suggestions of alarm. I had long since prepared my tincture. I purchased at once, from a firm of wholesale chemists, a large quantity of a particular salt. I knew, from my experiments, that this was the last ingredient required. And late one cursed night, I mixed the elements. I watched them boil and smoke together in the glass. When the boiling had gone down, with a strong glow of courage, I drank off the potion.

The worst pains came next. My bones felt ground together. I had deadly nausea, and a horror of the spirit deeper than at birth or death. Then these pains began to fade quickly. I came back to myself as if after a great sickness. My feelings were strange. Something was new in a way I could not describe. And because it was so new, it was incredibly sweet. I felt younger, lighter, happier in body. Inside, I was aware of a wild recklessness. A stream of disordered pleasurable images ran through my mind like a mill-race. Every duty felt loosened. My soul had a freedom I did not know, and it was not innocent. At the first breath of this new life, I knew myself to be more evil, ten times more evil, a slave sold to my original evil. And that thought, in that moment, strengthened and delighted me like wine. I stretched out my hands, rejoicing in the freshness of these feelings. And as I did, I suddenly saw that I had lost height.

There was no mirror in my room at that date. The one that stands beside me as I write was brought there later, for the very purpose of these transformations. But the night had gone far into the morning. The morning, black as it was, was nearly ready to become day. Everyone in my house was locked in the deepest hours of sleep. I was flushed with hope and triumph. I decided to go as far as my bedroom in my new shape. I crossed the yard. The stars looked down at me, I could have thought, with wonder. I was the first creature of that kind their unsleeping watch had ever shown them. I stole through the corridors, a stranger in my own house. And coming to my room, I saw for the first time the appearance of Edward Hyde.

Here I must speak only from theory. I do not say what I know. I say what I suppose is most likely. The evil side of my nature was less strong and less developed than the good side I had just removed. I had now given the evil side the power to act. Also, in my life—which had been, after all, nine-tenths a life of effort, virtue, and control—the evil side had been used much less and worn out much less. And so, I think, it happened that Edward Hyde was so much smaller, slighter, and younger than Henry Jekyll. Good shone on the face of one. Evil was written large and clear on the face of the other. Evil also—which I must still believe is the deadly side of man—had left on that body a mark of ugliness and ruin. And yet, when I looked at that ugly idol in the glass, I felt no disgust. Instead, I felt a leap of welcome. This too was myself. It seemed natural and human. In my eyes it carried a livelier image of the spirit. It seemed more clear and single than the imperfect, divided face I had until then been used to calling mine. And in this I was surely right. I have observed that when I wore the appearance of Edward Hyde, no one could come near me at first without a visible bodily doubt. I take this to be because all human beings, as we meet them, are mixed out of good and evil. And Edward Hyde, alone among mankind, was pure evil.

I stayed at the mirror only a moment. The second and final experiment had still to be tried. It still remained to be seen if I had lost my identity beyond saving. It remained to be seen if I must flee before daylight from a house that was no longer mine. I hurried back to my cabinet. I once more prepared and drank the cup. I once more suffered the pains of breaking apart. And I came to myself once more with the character, the height, and the face of Henry Jekyll.

That night I had come to the fatal cross-roads. If I had approached my discovery in a more noble spirit, if I had risked the experiment while full of generous or holy hopes, all must have been different. From these pains of death and birth, I would have come out an angel instead of a demon. The drug had no power to choose. It was neither devilish nor divine. It only shook the doors of the prison of my nature. And like the prisoners of Philippi, whatever stood inside ran out. At that time my virtue slept. My evil, kept awake by ambition, was alert and quick to seize the moment. And the thing that came out was Edward Hyde. So now I had two characters as well as two appearances. One was completely evil. The other was still the old Henry Jekyll, that strange mix whose reform and improvement I had already learned to give up hoping for. The movement was this completely toward the worse.

Even at that time, I had not yet conquered my dislike of the dryness of a life of study. I would still be merry at times. And my pleasures were (to say the least) not dignified. I was not only well known and highly respected, but also growing toward old age. So this lack of order in my life was daily becoming less welcome. It was on this side that my new power tempted me until I fell into slavery. I had only to drink the cup to put off at once the body of the noted professor. I could then put on, like a thick cloak, the body of Edward Hyde. I smiled at the idea. It seemed to me at the time to be funny. And I made my preparations with the most careful study. I took and furnished that house in Soho, to which Hyde was tracked by the police. I hired as housekeeper a person whom I well knew to be silent and without scruples. On the other side, I told my servants that a Mr. Hyde (whom I described) was to have full freedom and power about my house in the square. And to prevent accidents, I even called and made myself a familiar object in my second character. Next I drew up that will to which you objected so much. So if anything happened to me in the person of Dr. Jekyll, I could enter that of Edward Hyde without money loss. And so, as I supposed, protected on every side, I began to profit by the strange freedoms of my position.

Men had paid killers before to do their crimes for them. Their own person and reputation stayed safe. I was the first that ever did so for his pleasures. I was the first that could walk in the public eye with a load of friendly good name. And in a moment, like a young boy, I could strip off these borrowed clothes and jump headlong into the sea of liberty. But for me, in my thick covering, the safety was complete. Think of it—I did not even exist! Let me just escape into my laboratory door. Give me just a second or two to mix and swallow the drink that I always kept ready. Then whatever he had done, Edward Hyde would pass away like the mark of breath upon a mirror. And there in his place, quietly at home, trimming the midnight lamp in his study, a man who could afford to laugh at suspicion would be Henry Jekyll.

The pleasures which I hurried to seek in my hiding were, as I have said, not worthy of respect. I would hardly use a harder word. But in the hands of Edward Hyde, they soon began to turn toward the shocking. When I would come back from these outings, I was often thrown into a kind of wonder at my second-hand badness. This familiar that I called out of my own soul, and sent out alone to do his good pleasure, was a being naturally evil and wicked. His every act and thought centered on self. He drank pleasure with animal hunger from any degree of pain to another. He was unrelenting like a man of stone. Henry Jekyll stood at times in horror before the acts of Edward Hyde. But the situation was apart from ordinary laws. It quietly loosened the hold of conscience. It was Hyde, after all, and Hyde alone, that was guilty. Jekyll was no worse. He woke again to his good qualities seemingly undamaged. He would even hurry, where it was possible, to undo the evil done by Hyde. And so his conscience slept.

I have no plan of entering into the details of the shame at which I thus helped (for even now I can hardly admit that I did it). I mean but to point out the warnings and the steps one after another with which my punishment came near. I met with one accident which, as it brought on no result, I shall no more than mention. An act of unkindness to a child raised against me the anger of a person walking by. I knew him the other day in the person of your relative. The doctor and the child's family joined him. There were moments when I feared for my life. And at last, in order to calm their too just anger, Edward Hyde had to bring them to the door, and pay them with a cheque drawn in the name of Henry Jekyll. But this danger was easily removed from the future, by opening an account at another bank in the name of Edward Hyde himself. And when, by sloping my own hand backward, I had supplied my double with a signature, I thought I sat beyond the reach of fate.

CHAPTER 7. Chapter 1（7）

About two months before the murder of Sir Danvers, I had gone out for one of my adventures. I returned late at night. The next day I woke in bed with strange feelings. I looked around me, but it was no use. I saw the good furniture and the tall size of my room in the square, but it was no use. I saw the pattern of the bed-curtains and the design of the wood frame, but it was no use. Something kept saying that I was not where I was. It said I had not woken where I seemed to be. It said I had woken in the little room in Soho where I usually slept in the body of Edward Hyde. I smiled to myself. In my usual way of thinking, I began to slowly ask about the parts of this false picture. Even as I did so, I sometimes fell back into a comfortable morning sleep. I was still doing this when, in one of my more awake moments, my eyes fell on my hand. The hand of Henry Jekyll (as you have often said) was a hand like a doctor's in shape and size. It was large, firm, white, and handsome. But the hand I now saw was clear enough in the yellow light of a London morning. It lay half closed on the bed-clothes. It was thin, with clear cords and large finger joints. It had a dark pale shade and was covered with thick dark hair. It was the hand of Edward Hyde.

I must have stared at it for almost half a minute. I was sunk in the simple lack of understanding of wonder. Then terror woke in my chest, as sudden and shocking as the crash of brass plates. I jumped from my bed and rushed to the mirror. At the sight that met my eyes, my blood turned into something very thin and cold. Yes, I had gone to bed as Henry Jekyll, and I had woken as Edward Hyde. How could this be explained? I asked myself. Then, with another jump of terror, how could it be fixed? It was well into the morning. The servants were up. All my drugs were in the cabinet. It was a long journey down two pairs of stairs, through the back passage, across the open court, and through the medical room. All that lay between me and the cabinet, as I stood there full of horror. It might be possible to cover my face. But what use was that, when I could not hide the change in my height? Then a strong sweet feeling of relief came back to my mind. The servants were already used to my second self coming and going. I soon dressed as well as I could in clothes of my own size. I soon passed through the house. Bradshaw stared and drew back when he saw Mr. Hyde at such an hour and in such strange clothes. Ten minutes later, Dr. Jekyll had returned to his own shape. He sat down with a dark look on his face and pretended to eat breakfast.

My appetite was indeed small. This strange event, this change back of my earlier experience, seemed like the Babylonian finger on the wall. It seemed to spell out the letters of my judgment. I began to think more seriously than ever before about the problems and possibilities of my double existence. That part of me which I had the power to project had lately been much used and fed. It had seemed to me lately that the body of Edward Hyde had grown in height. It had seemed that (when I wore that form) I felt a more generous flow of blood. I began to see a danger. If this went on much longer, the balance of my nature might be permanently destroyed. The power of voluntary change might be lost. The character of Edward Hyde might become forever mine. The power of the drug had not always been the same. Once, very early in my career, it had totally failed me. Since then I had been forced more than once to double the amount. Once, with great risk of death, I had to make it three times as much. These rare uncertainties had until now cast the only shadow on my satisfaction. Now, however, in the light of that morning's accident, I was led to notice something. At the beginning, the difficulty had been to throw off the body of Jekyll. Lately it had gradually but clearly moved to the other side. All things therefore seemed to point to this: I was slowly losing hold of my original and better self. I was slowly becoming joined with my second and worse self.

Between these two, I now felt I had to choose. My two natures had memory in common. But all other powers were most unfairly shared between them. Jekyll (who was a mix of both) now with the most sensitive feelings, now with a selfish taste, projected and shared in the pleasures and adventures of Hyde. But Hyde did not care about Jekyll. He only remembered him as the mountain thief remembers the cave where he hides from pursuit. Jekyll had more than a father's interest. Hyde had more than a son's lack of care. To cast my lot with Jekyll was to die to those appetites which I had long done in secret and had lately begun to treat too kindly. To cast it with Hyde was to die to a thousand interests and hopes. It was to become, at one blow and forever, hated and without friends. The bargain might appear unfair. But there was still another consideration in the scales. Jekyll would suffer sharply in the fires of self-denial. Hyde would not even be conscious of all that he had lost. Strange as my circumstances were, the terms of this debate are as old and common as man. Much the same rewards and fears cast the die for any tempted and trembling wrongdoer. And it fell out with me, as it falls with so vast a majority of my fellows, that I chose the better part and was found wanting in the strength to keep to it.

Yes, I preferred the elderly and unsatisfied doctor, surrounded by friends and holding dear honest hopes. I said a firm goodbye to the liberty, the more youthful years, the light step, leaping impulses and secret pleasures, that I had enjoyed in the false appearance of Hyde. I made this choice perhaps with some unconscious reservation. I neither gave up the house in Soho, nor destroyed the clothes of Edward Hyde, which still lay ready in my cabinet. For two months, however, I was true to my determination. For two months I led a life of such strictness as I had never before reached. I enjoyed the rewards of an approving conscience. But time began at last to rub out the newness of my alarm. The praises of conscience began to grow into a thing of course. I began to be tortured with sharp pains and strong desires, as of Hyde struggling after freedom. And at last, in an hour of moral weakness, I once again mixed and swallowed the transforming drink.

I do not think that when a drunkard reasons with himself about his bad habit, he is even one time in five hundred affected by the dangers he runs through his stupid, physical lack of feeling. Neither had I, though I had thought about my position for a long time, made enough allowance for the complete moral lack of feeling and the thoughtless readiness for evil that were the main qualities of Edward Hyde. Yet it was by these that I was punished. My devil had been locked up for a long time, and he came out roaring. I was aware, even when I took the drink, of a more uncontrolled, a more furious tendency to do wrong. It must have been this, I suppose, that stirred in my soul that storm of impatience with which I listened to the polite words of my unhappy victim. I declare, at least, before God, that no man in his right mind could have committed that crime on such a small cause for anger. And I struck in no more reasonable spirit than a sick child might break a toy. But I had willingly stripped myself of all those balancing instincts by which even the worst of us continues to walk with some degree of steadiness among temptations. And in my case, to be tempted, however slightly, was to fall.

At once the spirit of hell woke in me and raged. With a rush of joy, I beat the unresisting body, tasting delight from every blow. And it was not until tiredness had begun to follow, that I was suddenly, in the height of my wild excitement, struck through the heart by a cold thrill of terror. A mist cleared; I saw my life was lost; and I fled from the scene of these excesses. At once I was proud and trembling, my desire for evil satisfied and stirred up, my love of life screwed to the highest point. I ran to the house in Soho, and (to make sure twice over) destroyed my papers. From there I set out through the streets lit by lamps, in the same divided joy of mind, gloating on my crime, lightly planning others in the future, and yet still hurrying and still listening behind me for the steps of the avenger. Hyde had a song on his lips as he mixed the drink, and as he drank it, he pledged the dead man. The pains of transformation had not finished tearing him before Henry Jekyll, with streaming tears of gratitude and regret, had fallen on his knees and lifted his clasped hands to God. The veil of self-indulgence was torn from top to bottom. I saw my life as a whole. I followed it up from the days of childhood, when I had walked with my father's hand, and through the self-denying work of my professional life, to arrive again and again, with the same sense of unreality, at the damned horrors of the evening. I could have screamed aloud. I sought with tears and prayers to smother down the crowd of hideous images and sounds with which my memory swarmed against me. And still, between the prayers, the ugly face of my sin stared into my soul. As the sharpness of this regret began to die away, it was followed by a sense of joy. The problem of my conduct was solved. Hyde was from then on impossible. Whether I wanted it or not, I was now confined to the better part of my existence. And oh, how I rejoiced to think it! With what willing humility I embraced anew the limits of natural life! With what sincere giving up, I locked the door by which I had so often gone and come, and ground the key under my heel!

The next day came the news that the murder had not been overlooked, that the guilt of Hyde was clear to the world, and that the victim was a man held high in public opinion. It was not only a crime, it had been a tragic folly. I think I was glad to know it. I think I was glad to have my better impulses thus supported and guarded by the terrors of the scaffold. Jekyll was now my city of refuge. If Hyde only peeped out for an instant, the hands of all men would be raised to take and kill him.

I resolved in my future conduct to make up for the past. And I can say honestly that my resolve produced some good. You know yourself how earnestly in the last months of last year I worked to relieve suffering. You know that much was done for others, and that the days passed quietly, almost happily for myself. Nor can I truly say that I grew tired of this kind and innocent life. I think instead that I enjoyed it more completely every day. But I was still cursed with my double purpose. And as the first sharp edge of my regret wore off, the lower side of me, so long indulged, so recently chained down, began to growl for freedom. Not that I dreamed of bringing Hyde back; the bare idea of that would startle me to madness. No, it was in my own person that I was once more tempted to trifle with my conscience. And it was as an ordinary secret sinner that I at last fell before the attacks of temptation.

There comes an end to all things; the largest measure is filled at last; and this brief yielding to evil finally destroyed the balance of my soul. And yet I was not alarmed; the fall seemed natural, like a return to the old days before I had made my discovery. It was a fine, clear, January day, wet under foot where the frost had melted, but cloudless overhead. And the Regent's Park was full of winter bird sounds and sweet with spring smells. I sat in the sun on a bench. The animal within me licked its lips at the memory. The spiritual side was a little sleepy, promising regret later, but not yet moved to begin. After all, I reflected, I was like my neighbours. And then I smiled, comparing myself with other men, comparing my active goodwill with the lazy cruelty of their neglect. And at the very moment of that proud thought, a feeling of sickness came over me, a horrible nausea and the most deadly shaking. These passed away and left me faint. And then as the faintness in its turn went down, I began to be aware of a change in the temper of my thoughts: a greater boldness, a contempt for danger, a loosening of the bonds of duty. I looked down; my clothes hung loosely on my shrunken limbs; the hand that lay on my knee was corded and hairy. I was once more Edward Hyde. A moment before I had been safe from all men's disrespect, wealthy, loved—the cloth laid for me in the dining-room at home. And now I was the common hunted animal of mankind, hunted, houseless, a known murderer, slave to the gallows.

My reason shook, but it did not fail me completely. I have more than once noticed that, in my second character, my mind seemed sharpened to a point and my spirits more tightly stretched. So it happened that, where Jekyll perhaps might have given in, Hyde rose to meet the importance of the moment. My drugs were in one of the cupboards of my cabinet. How was I to reach them? That was the problem that I set myself to solve, pressing my hands to my head. I had closed the laboratory door. If I tried to enter by the house, my own servants would send me to the gallows. I saw I must use another hand, and I thought of Lanyon. How was he to be reached? How persuaded? Supposing that I escaped capture in the streets, how was I to make my way into his presence? And how should I, an unknown and unpleasant visitor, win over the famous physician to search the study of his colleague, Dr. Jekyll? Then I remembered that one part of my original character remained to me: I could write my own hand. Once I had that spark of an idea, the way I must follow became lit up from end to end.

So I arranged my clothes as best I could, called a passing hansom, and drove to a hotel in Portland Street, the name of which I happened to remember. At my appearance (which was indeed funny enough, however tragic a fate these clothes covered) the driver could not hide his laughter. I ground my teeth at him with a burst of devilish anger, and the smile faded from his face—happily for him—yet more happily for myself, for in another moment I had certainly dragged him from his seat. At the hotel, as I entered, I looked about me with so dark a face that the attendants trembled. Not a look did they exchange in my presence. But they obsequiously took my orders, led me to a private room, and brought me what I needed to write. Hyde in danger of his life was a creature new to me: shaken with too much anger, strung to the pitch of murder, longing to cause pain. Yet the creature was clever. He mastered his anger with a great effort of the will. He composed his two important letters, one to Lanyon and one to Poole. And so that he might receive actual evidence of their being posted, he sent them out with directions that they should be registered.

From then on, he sat all day over the fire in the private room, biting his nails. There he ate, sitting alone with his fears, the waiter visibly trembling before his eye. And from there, when the night was fully come, he set out in the corner of a closed cab, and was driven to and fro about the streets of the city. He, I say—I cannot say, I. That child of Hell had nothing human; nothing lived in him but fear and hatred. And when at last, thinking the driver had begun to grow suspicious, he sent the cab away and ventured on foot, dressed in his badly fitting clothes, an object marked out for observation, into the midst of the night passengers, these two base passions raged within him like a storm. He walked fast, hunted by his fears, chattering to himself, sneaking through the less-frequented streets, counting the minutes that still divided him from midnight. Once a woman spoke to him, offering, I think, a box of lights. He struck her in the face, and she fled.

When I came to myself at Lanyon’s, the horror of my old friend perhaps affected me somewhat: I do not know. It was at least but a drop in the sea to the hatred with which I looked back upon these hours. A change had come over me. It was no longer the fear of the gallows, it was the horror of being Hyde that racked me. I received Lanyon’s condemnation partly in a dream; it was partly in a dream that I came home to my own house and got into bed. I slept after the weakness of the day, with a tight and deep sleep which not even the nightmares that twisted me could break. I awoke in the morning shaken, weakened, but refreshed. I still hated and feared the thought of the brute that slept within me, and I had not of course forgotten the terrible dangers of the day before. But I was once more at home, in my own house and close to my drugs. And gratitude for my escape shone so strong in my soul that it almost rivalled the brightness of hope.

I was walking slowly across the court after breakfast. I was enjoying the cold air. Then those strange feelings came over me again—the ones that came before the change. I only had time to reach my cabinet before I was raging and freezing with the passions of Hyde. This time it took a double dose to bring me back to myself. And alas! Six hours later, as I sat looking sadly into the fire, the pains returned, and I had to take the drug again. In short, from that day on, it seemed only by a great effort—like hard exercise—and only right after taking the drug, that I could wear the face of Jekyll. At all hours of the day and night, I would feel the warning shake come over me. Above all, if I slept, or even rested for a moment in my chair, I always woke up as Hyde. Under the strain of this coming doom, and by the sleeplessness I now forced on myself—yes, even beyond what I had thought a man could bear—I became, in my own person, a creature eaten up and emptied by fever. I was weakly tired in both body and mind, and filled with only one thought: the horror of my other self. But when I slept, or when the power of the medicine wore off, I would jump almost without pause (for the pains of change grew less clear each day) into a mind full of terrifying images. I became a soul boiling with hatred that had no cause, and a body that seemed too weak to hold the raging energies of life. The powers of Hyde seemed to have grown as Jekyll grew weaker. And surely the hate that now divided them was equal on each side. With Jekyll, it was a matter of deep instinct. He had now seen the full ugliness of that creature that shared with him some of the phenomena of consciousness, and was co-heir with him to death. Beyond these links between them—which in themselves made the sharpest part of his distress—he thought of Hyde, for all his energy of life, as something not only hellish but inorganic. This was the shocking thing: that the slime of the pit seemed to cry out and speak; that the shapeless dust moved its hands and sinned; that what was dead, and had no shape, should take over the duties of life. And this too: that that rebel horror was tied to him closer than a wife, closer than an eye. It lay caged in his flesh, where he heard it mutter and felt it struggle to be born. At every hour of weakness, and in the trust of sleep, it won against him and pushed him out of life. The hatred of Hyde for Jekyll was of a different kind. His fear of the gallows drove him continually to commit a kind of temporary suicide, and return to his lower station of a part instead of a person. But he hated the need for it. He hated the low spirits into which Jekyll had now fallen. And he resented the dislike with which he himself was seen. Hence the ape-like tricks he would play on me: writing curses in my own hand on the pages of my books, burning the letters and destroying the portrait of my father. And indeed, if it had not been for his fear of death, he would long ago have ruined himself in order to bring ruin on me. But his love of life is wonderful. I go further: I, who feel sick and cold at the mere thought of him—when I recall the lowness and passion of this attachment, and when I know how he fears my power to cut him off by suicide—I find it in my heart to pity him.

CHAPTER 8. Chapter 1（8）

It is no use, and I am very short of time, to make this description longer. No one has ever suffered such pain. Let that be enough. And yet even to this pain, habit brought—no, not relief—but a certain hardness of soul, a certain acceptance of despair. And my punishment might have gone on for years, but for the last disaster which has now fallen, and which has finally cut me off from my own face and nature. My supply of the salt, which had never been replaced since the first experiment, began to run low. I sent out for a fresh supply, and mixed the drink. The boiling followed, and the first change of colour, not the second. I drank it and it had no effect. You will learn from Poole how I have had London searched; it was no use. And I am now sure that my first supply was not pure, and that it was that unknown impurity which gave the drink its power.

About a week has passed, and I am now finishing this statement under the influence of the last of the old powders. This, then, is the last time, unless there is a miracle, that Henry Jekyll can think his own thoughts or see his own face (now how sadly changed!) in the glass. Nor must I wait too long to bring my writing to an end. For if my story has until now escaped destruction, it has been by a combination of great care and great good luck. If the pains of change take me while I am writing it, Hyde will tear it in pieces. But if some time has passed after I have put it down, his wonderful selfishness and his living only in the moment will probably save it once again from his ape-like hate. And indeed the fate that is closing on us both has already changed and crushed him. Half an hour from now, when I shall again and for ever put on that hated person, I know how I shall sit shaking and weeping in my chair. Or I shall walk up and down this room (my last refuge on earth), listening with the most strained and fearful joy, and give ear to every sound of threat. Will Hyde die on the scaffold? Or will he find courage to free himself at the last moment? God knows; I do not care. This is my true hour of death, and what is to follow concerns another than myself. Here then, as I lay down the pen and go on to seal up my confession, I bring the life of that unhappy Henry Jekyll to an end.
