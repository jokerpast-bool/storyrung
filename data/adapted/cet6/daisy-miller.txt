CHAPTER 1. Part 1

At the little town of Vevey, in Switzerland, there is a very comfortable hotel. There are, in fact, many hotels, because the tourist business is the main business of the place. As many travelers will remember, the town sits on the edge of a remarkably blue lake. Every tourist should visit this lake. The shore of the lake has an unbroken line of hotels of this kind, of every type. They range from the "grand hotel" of the newest fashion, with a white front, a hundred balconies, and a dozen flags flying from its roof, to the little Swiss guesthouse of an older day. That older place has its name written in German-looking letters on a pink or yellow wall and an awkward garden house in the corner of the garden. One of the hotels at Vevey, however, is famous, even classic. It stands apart from many of its new neighbors by an air of both luxury and maturity. In this region, in the month of June, American travelers are extremely numerous. In fact, it may be said that Vevey takes on some of the qualities of an American resort town at this time. There are sights and sounds that bring to mind a vision, an echo, of Newport and Saratoga. There is a moving here and there of "stylish" young girls. There is a soft sound of cotton dresses. There is dance music in the morning hours. There is a sound of high voices at all times. You receive an impression of these things at the excellent hotel of the "Trois Couronnes." In your mind you are carried to the Ocean House or to Congress Hall. But at the "Trois Couronnes," it must be added, there are other features that are very different from these suggestions. There are neat German waiters, who look like embassy secretaries. There are Russian princesses sitting in the garden. There are little Polish boys walking about, held by the hand, with their tutors. There is a view of the sunny top of the Dent du Midi and the beautiful towers of the Castle of Chillon.

I hardly know whether it was the similarities or the differences that were uppermost in the mind of a young American. Two or three years ago, he sat in the garden of the "Trois Couronnes," looking about him rather idly at some of the beautiful things I have mentioned. It was a beautiful summer morning. In whatever way the young American looked at things, they must have seemed charming to him. He had come from Geneva the day before by the little steamer to see his aunt, who was staying at the hotel. Geneva had been his home for a long time. But his aunt had a headache. His aunt almost always had a headache. Now she was shut up in her room, smelling camphor, so he was free to wander about. He was about twenty-seven years of age. When his friends spoke of him, they usually said that he was at Geneva "studying." When his enemies spoke of him, they said--but after all, he had no enemies. He was an extremely friendly fellow, and everyone liked him. What I should say is simply that when certain persons spoke of him, they claimed that the reason he spent so much time at Geneva was that he was extremely devoted to a lady who lived there. She was a foreign lady, a person older than himself. Very few Americans--indeed, I think none--had ever seen this lady. There were some strange stories about her. But Winterbourne had an old attachment for the little city of Calvinism. He had been sent to school there as a boy, and he had afterward gone to college there. These circumstances had led to his forming a great many young friendships. He had kept many of these, and they were a source of great satisfaction to him.

After knocking at his aunt's door and learning that she was unwell, he had taken a walk about the town. Then he had come in to his breakfast. He had now finished his breakfast. But he was drinking a small cup of coffee, which had been served to him on a little table in the garden by one of the waiters who looked like an embassy secretary. At last he finished his coffee and lit a cigarette. Presently a small boy came walking along the path--a child of nine or ten. The child, who was small for his years, had an old expression on his face, a pale skin, and sharp little features. He was dressed in short trousers, with red stockings, which showed his poor little thin legs. He also wore a bright red tie. He carried in his hand a long mountain walking stick. He pushed its sharp point into everything he approached--the flowerbeds, the garden benches, the trains of the ladies' dresses. In front of Winterbourne he paused, looking at him with a pair of bright, sharp little eyes.

"Will you give me a piece of sugar?" he asked in a sharp, hard little voice--a voice young and yet, somehow, not young.

Winterbourne glanced at the small table near him, on which his coffee set rested, and saw that several pieces of sugar remained. "Yes, you may take one," he answered. "But I do not think sugar is good for little boys."

This little boy stepped forward and carefully selected three of the wanted pieces. He buried two of them in the pocket of his short trousers and put the other quickly in another place. He pushed his mountain walking stick, like a spear, into Winterbourne's bench and tried to break the piece of sugar with his teeth.

"Oh, blazes; it is har-r-d!" he exclaimed, pronouncing the word in a peculiar manner.

Winterbourne had immediately seen that he might have the honor of claiming him as a fellow countryman. "Take care you do not hurt your teeth," he said, in a fatherly way.

"I have not got any teeth to hurt. They have all come out. I have only got seven teeth. My mother counted them last night, and one came out right afterward. She said she would slap me if any more came out. I cannot help it. It is this old Europe. It is the climate that makes them come out. In America they did not come out. It is these hotels."

Winterbourne was much amused. "If you eat three pieces of sugar, your mother will certainly slap you," he said.

"She has got to give me some candy, then," replied his young conversation partner. "I cannot get any candy here--any American candy. American candy is the best candy."

"And are American little boys the best little boys?" asked Winterbourne.

"I do not know. I am an American boy," said the child.

"I see you are one of the best!" laughed Winterbourne.

"Are you an American man?" pursued this lively infant. And then, on Winterbourne's affirmative reply--"American men are the best," he declared.

His companion thanked him for the compliment. The child, who had now got astride of his mountain walking stick, stood looking about him while he attacked a second piece of sugar. Winterbourne wondered if he himself had been like this in his infancy, for he had been brought to Europe at about this age.

"Here comes my sister!" cried the child in a moment. "She is an American girl."

Winterbourne looked along the path. He saw a beautiful young lady coming toward him. “American girls are the best girls,” he said happily to his young companion.

“My sister is not the best!” the child said. “She is always shouting at me.”

“I think that is your fault, not hers,” said Winterbourne. The young lady had now come near. She wore a white cotton dress with many folds and loose edges, and knots of light-colored ribbon. She had no hat, but she held a large sun umbrella in her hand. It had a wide border of decorative stitching. She was very, very pretty. “How pretty they are!” thought Winterbourne. He sat up straighter on his seat, as if he were ready to stand.

The young lady stopped in front of his bench. It was near the low garden wall, which looked over the lake. The little boy had now turned his walking stick into a jumping pole. With its help he was jumping about in the small stones and kicking them up a good deal.

“Randolph,” said the young lady, “what ARE you doing?”

“I am going up the Alps,” replied Randolph. “This is the way!” And he gave another little jump. Small stones flew around Winterbourne’s ears.

“That is the way they come down,” said Winterbourne.

“He is an American man!” cried Randolph in his small hard voice.

The young lady paid no attention to this. She looked straight at her brother. “Well, I think you had better be quiet,” she said simply.

It seemed to Winterbourne that he had been introduced in some way. He stood up and walked slowly toward the young girl. He threw away his cigarette. “This little boy and I have met,” he said with great politeness. In Geneva, he knew very well, a young man could not speak to a young unmarried lady except in certain rare situations. But here at Vevey, what situation could be better than this? A pretty American girl came and stood in front of you in a garden. This pretty American girl, however, only glanced at him when she heard his words. Then she turned her head and looked over the low wall at the lake and the mountains on the other side. He wondered if he had gone too far. But he decided he must go farther, not step back. While he was thinking of something else to say, the young lady turned to the little boy again.

“I would like to know where you got that pole,” she said.

“I bought it,” answered Randolph.

“You do not mean to say you are going to take it to Italy?”

“Yes, I am going to take it to Italy,” the child said.

The young girl looked down at the front of her dress. She smoothed out a knot or two of ribbon. Then she looked at the view again. “Well, I think you had better leave it somewhere,” she said after a moment.

“Are you going to Italy?” Winterbourne asked in a tone of great respect.

The young lady glanced at him again. “Yes, sir,” she replied. And she said nothing more.

“Are you--a--going over the Simplon?” Winterbourne went on, a little uncomfortable.

“I do not know,” she said. “I think it is some mountain. Randolph, what mountain are we going over?”

“Going where?” the child asked.

“To Italy,” Winterbourne explained.

“I do not know,” said Randolph. “I do not want to go to Italy. I want to go to America.”

“Oh, Italy is a beautiful place!” said the young man.

“Can you get candy there?” Randolph asked loudly.

“I hope not,” said his sister. “I think you have had enough candy, and mother thinks so too.”

“I have not had any for a very long time--for a hundred weeks!” cried the boy. He was still jumping about.

The young lady looked at her dress folds and smoothed her ribbons again. Winterbourne soon risked a comment on the beauty of the view. He was becoming less uncomfortable. He had begun to see that she was not uncomfortable at all. There had been no change at all in her lovely face. She was clearly neither angry nor pleased. If she looked another way when he spoke to her, and seemed not to hear him very well, this was simply her habit, her way. Yet he talked a little more. He pointed out some interesting things in the view, which she seemed not to know at all. She slowly gave him more of her glance. Then he saw that this glance was completely direct and steady. It was not, however, what people would call a bold glance. The young girl’s eyes were unusually honest and fresh. They were very pretty eyes. Indeed, Winterbourne had not seen anything prettier for a long time than his fair countrywoman’s different features--her face, her nose, her ears, her teeth. He enjoyed female beauty very much. He liked to watch it and study it. And he made several observations about this young lady’s face. It was not at all dull, but it was not exactly full of expression. And though it was very delicate, Winterbourne silently thought--very kindly--that it lacked finish. He thought it very possible that Master Randolph’s sister was a flirt. He was sure she had a spirit of her own. But in her bright, sweet, light little face there was no mocking, no irony. Before long it became clear that she was very willing to talk. She told him that they were going to Rome for the winter--she and her mother and Randolph. She asked him if he was a “real American.” She would not have taken him for one. He seemed more like a German--she said this after a little hesitation--especially when he spoke. Winterbourne laughed. He answered that he had met Germans who spoke like Americans. But he had not, as far as he remembered, met an American who spoke like a German. Then he asked her if she would not be more comfortable sitting on the bench he had just left. She answered that she liked standing up and walking about. But she soon sat down. She told him she was from New York State--“if you know where that is.” Winterbourne learned more about her by catching her small, slippery brother and making him stand by his side for a few minutes.

“Tell me your name, my boy,” he said.

“Randolph C. Miller,” said the boy sharply. “And I will tell you her name.” He pointed his walking stick at his sister.

“You had better wait till you are asked!” said this young lady calmly.

“I would like very much to know your name,” said Winterbourne.

“Her name is Daisy Miller!” cried the child. “But that is not her real name. That is not her name on her cards.”

“It is a pity you do not have one of my cards!” said Miss Miller.

“Her real name is Annie P. Miller,” the boy went on.

“Ask him HIS name,” said his sister, pointing at Winterbourne.

But Randolph did not seem to care about this at all. He went on giving information about his own family. "My father's name is Ezra B. Miller," he announced. "My father is not in Europe. My father is in a better place than Europe."

For a moment Winterbourne thought this was how the child had been taught to say that Mr. Miller had gone to heaven. But Randolph added at once, "My father is in Schenectady. He has a big business. My father is rich, you bet!"

"Well!" Miss Miller cried out. She lowered her parasol and looked at its decorated edge. Winterbourne soon let the child go. Randolph walked away, pulling his alpenstock along the path. "He does not like Europe," said the young girl. "He wants to go back."

"To Schenectady, you mean?"

"Yes, he wants to go straight home. He has no boys here. There is one boy here, but he always walks around with a teacher. They will not let him play."

"And your brother has no teacher?" Winterbourne asked.

"Mother thought of getting him one, to travel around with us. A lady told her about a very good teacher. An American lady--perhaps you know her--Mrs. Sanders. I think she came from Boston. She told her about this teacher, and we thought of getting him to travel around with us. But Randolph said he did not want a teacher traveling around with us. He said he would not have lessons when he was in the cars. And we are in the cars about half the time. There was an English lady we met in the cars--I think her name was Miss Featherstone. Perhaps you know her. She wanted to know why I did not give Randolph lessons--give him 'instruction,' she called it. I guess he could give me more instruction than I could give him. He is very smart."

"Yes," said Winterbourne. "He seems very smart."

"Mother is going to get a teacher for him as soon as we get to Italy. Can you get good teachers in Italy?"

"Very good, I should think," said Winterbourne.

"Or else she is going to find some school. He ought to learn more. He is only nine. He is going to college." And in this way Miss Miller went on talking about her family and about other topics. She sat there with her very pretty hands folded in her lap. Her hands had very bright rings on them. Her pretty eyes rested now on Winterbourne's eyes, now wandered over the garden, the people who passed by, and the beautiful view. She talked to Winterbourne as if she had known him a long time. He found it very pleasant. It was many years since he had heard a young girl talk so much. Someone might have said that this unknown young lady, who had come and sat down beside him on a bench, talked too much. She was very quiet. She sat in a charming, calm way. But her lips and her eyes were always moving. She had a soft, thin, pleasant voice, and her tone was very friendly. She gave Winterbourne a history of her movements and plans and those of her mother and brother in Europe. She listed, in particular, the various hotels where they had stopped. "That English lady in the cars," she said--"Miss Featherstone--asked me if we did not all live in hotels in America. I told her I had never been in so many hotels in my life as since I came to Europe. I have never seen so many--it is nothing but hotels." But Miss Miller did not say this in a complaining voice. She seemed to be in the best mood with everything. She declared that the hotels were very good, once you got used to their ways, and that Europe was perfectly sweet. She was not disappointed--not a bit. Perhaps it was because she had heard so much about it before. She had very many close friends who had been there very many times. And then she had had very many dresses and things from Paris. Whenever she put on a Paris dress she felt as if she were in Europe.

"It was a kind of wishing cap," said Winterbourne.

"Yes," said Miss Miller, without examining this comparison. "It always made me wish I was here. But I did not need to do that for dresses. I am sure they send all the pretty ones to America. You see the most terrible things here. The only thing I do not like," she went on, "is the society. There is no society. Or, if there is, I do not know where it keeps itself. Do you? I suppose there is some society somewhere, but I have not seen anything of it. I am very fond of society, and I have always had a great deal of it. I do not mean only in Schenectady, but in New York. I used to go to New York every winter. In New York I had lots of society. Last winter I had seventeen dinners given me. And three of them were by gentlemen," added Daisy Miller. "I have more friends in New York than in Schenectady--more gentleman friends. And more young lady friends too," she resumed in a moment. She paused again for an instant. She was looking at Winterbourne with all her prettiness in her lively eyes and in her light, slightly monotonous smile. "I have always had," she said, "a great deal of gentlemen's society."

Poor Winterbourne was amused, confused, and clearly charmed. He had never heard a young girl speak in just this way. He had heard such things only in cases where they seemed to show a lack of good manners. And yet, should he accuse Miss Daisy Miller of bad conduct, as they said at Geneva? He felt he had lived at Geneva so long that he had lost a great deal. He had become unused to the American way of speaking. Never, since he had grown old enough to understand things, had he met a young American girl of such a strong type as this. Certainly she was very charming, but how very friendly! Was she simply a pretty girl from New York State? Were all pretty girls like that, the ones who had a good deal of company with men? Or was she also a clever, a bold, a careless young person? Winterbourne had lost his instinct in this matter, and his reason could not help him. Miss Daisy Miller looked extremely innocent. Some people had told him that, after all, American girls were very innocent. And others had told him that, after all, they were not. He was likely to think Miss Daisy Miller was a flirt--a pretty American flirt. He had never, up to now, had any contact with young ladies of this kind. He had known, here in Europe, two or three women--persons older than Miss Daisy Miller, and given, for the sake of good name, husbands--who were great flirts--dangerous, terrible women, with whom a man's contact was likely to become serious. But this young girl was not a flirt in that sense. She was very simple and inexperienced. She was only a pretty American flirt. Winterbourne was almost thankful for having found the formula that applied to Miss Daisy Miller. He leaned back in his seat. He said to himself that she had the most charming nose he had ever seen. He wondered what were the usual conditions and limits of a man's contact with a pretty American flirt. It soon became clear that he was on the way to learn.

CHAPTER 2. Part 2

"Have you been to that old castle?" asked the young girl. She pointed with her umbrella to the far-shining walls of the Chateau de Chillon.

"Yes, before, more than once," said Winterbourne. "You too, I suppose, have seen it?"

"No, we have not been there. I want very much to go there. Of course I mean to go there. I would not go away from here without having seen that old castle."

"It is a very nice trip," said Winterbourne. "And very easy to make. You can drive, you know. Or you can go by the little boat."

"You can go in the cars," said Miss Miller.

"Yes, you can go in the cars," Winterbourne agreed.

"Our courier says they take you right up to the castle," the young girl continued. "We were going last week, but my mother gave up. She suffers very much from stomach trouble. She said she could not go. Randolph would not go either. He says he does not think much of old castles. But I guess we will go this week, if we can get Randolph."

"Your brother is not interested in ancient monuments?" Winterbourne asked, smiling.

"He says he does not care much about old castles. He is only nine. He wants to stay at the hotel. Mother is afraid to leave him alone. And the courier will not stay with him. So we have not been to many places. But it will be too bad if we do not go up there." And Miss Miller pointed again at the Chateau de Chillon.

"I should think it might be arranged," said Winterbourne. "Could you not get someone to stay for the afternoon with Randolph?"

Miss Miller looked at him a moment. Then, very quietly, "I wish YOU would stay with him!" she said.

Winterbourne hesitated a moment. "I should much rather go to Chillon with you."

"With me?" asked the young girl with the same quietness.

She did not rise, turning red, as a young girl at Geneva would have done. Yet Winterbourne, aware that he had been very bold, thought it possible she was offended. "With your mother," he answered very courteously.

But it seemed that both his boldness and his respect were lost on Miss Daisy Miller. "I guess my mother will not go, after all," she said. "She does not like to ride around in the afternoon. But did you really mean what you said just now—that you would like to go up there?"

"Most truly," Winterbourne declared.

"Then we may arrange it. If mother will stay with Randolph, I guess Eugenio will."

"Eugenio?" the young man asked.

"Eugenio is our courier. He does not like to stay with Randolph. He is the most difficult man I ever saw. But he is a wonderful courier. I guess he will stay at home with Randolph if mother does. And then we can go to the castle."

Winterbourne thought for a moment as clearly as possible. "We" could only mean Miss Daisy Miller and himself. This plan seemed almost too pleasant to believe. He felt as if he ought to kiss the young lady's hand. Possibly he would have done so and quite ruined the plan. But at this moment another person appeared, probably Eugenio. A tall, handsome man, with excellent side hair, wearing a velvet morning coat and a bright watch chain, came up to Miss Miller. He looked sharply at her companion. "Oh, Eugenio!" said Miss Miller with the friendliest tone.

Eugenio had looked at Winterbourne from head to foot. He now bowed seriously to the young lady. "I have the honor to inform mademoiselle that lunch is on the table."

Miss Miller slowly rose. "See here, Eugenio!" she said. "I am going to that old castle, anyway."

"To the Chateau de Chillon, mademoiselle?" the courier asked. "Mademoiselle has made arrangements?" he added in a tone that struck Winterbourne as very rude.

Eugenio's tone apparently threw, even to Miss Miller's own understanding, a slightly mocking light on the young girl's situation. She turned to Winterbourne, turning a little red—a very little. "You will not back out?" she said.

"I shall not be happy till we go!" he protested.

"And you are staying in this hotel?" she went on. "And you are really an American?"

The courier stood looking at Winterbourne in an offensive way. The young man, at least, thought his manner of looking was an offense to Miss Miller. It suggested that she "picked up" friends. "I shall have the honor of presenting to you a person who will tell you all about me," he said, smiling and referring to his aunt.

"Oh, well, we will go some day," said Miss Miller. And she gave him a smile and turned away. She put up her umbrella and walked back to the hotel beside Eugenio. Winterbourne stood looking after her. And as she moved away, drawing her muslin frills over the gravel, he said to himself that she had the figure of a princess.

He had, however, promised to do more than proved possible, in promising to present his aunt, Mrs. Costello, to Miss Daisy Miller. As soon as the former lady had got better of her headache, he waited upon her in her apartment. And, after the proper inquiries about her health, he asked her if she had observed in the hotel an American family—a mamma, a daughter, and a little boy.

"And a courier?" said Mrs. Costello. "Oh yes, I have observed them. Seen them—heard them—and kept out of their way." Mrs. Costello was a widow with a fortune. She was a person of much distinction, who frequently hinted that, if she were not so very likely to get sick headaches, she would probably have left a deeper mark upon her time. She had a long, pale face, a high nose, and a great deal of very striking white hair, which she wore in large rolls and coils over the top of her head. She had two sons married in New York and another who was now in Europe. This young man was enjoying himself at Hamburg. And, though he was on his travels, was rarely perceived to visit any particular city at the moment selected by his mother for her own appearance there. Her nephew, who had come up to Vevey expressly to see her, was therefore more attentive than those who, as she said, were nearer to her. He had taken in at Geneva the idea that one must always be attentive to one's aunt. Mrs. Costello had not seen him for many years, and she was greatly pleased with him. She showed her approval by initiating him into many of the secrets of that social sway which, as she gave him to understand, she exerted in the American capital. She admitted that she was very exclusive. But, if he were acquainted with New York, he would see that one had to be. And her picture of the minutely hierarchical constitution of the society of that city, which she presented to him in many different lights, was, to Winterbourne's imagination, almost oppressively striking.

He immediately perceived, from her tone, that Miss Daisy Miller's place in the social scale was low. "I am afraid you do not approve of them," he said.

"They are very common," Mrs. Costello declared. "They are the sort of Americans that one does one's duty by not—not accepting."

“Ah, you do not accept them?” said the young man.

“I cannot, my dear Frederick. I would if I could, but I cannot.”

“The young girl is very pretty,” said Winterbourne in a moment.

“Of course she is pretty. But she is very common.”

“I see what you mean, of course,” said Winterbourne after another pause.

“She has that charming look that they all have,” his aunt resumed. “I cannot think where they pick it up. And she dresses in perfection--no, you do not know how well she dresses. I cannot think where they get their taste.”

“But, my dear aunt, she is not, after all, a Comanche savage.”

“She is a young lady,” said Mrs. Costello, “who has an intimacy with her mamma’s courier.”

“An intimacy with the courier?” the young man demanded.

“Oh, the mother is just as bad! They treat the courier like a familiar friend--like a gentleman. I should not wonder if he dines with them. Very likely they have never seen a man with such good manners, such fine clothes, so like a gentleman. He probably corresponds to the young lady’s idea of a count. He sits with them in the garden in the evening. I think he smokes.”

Winterbourne listened with interest to these disclosures. They helped him to make up his mind about Miss Daisy. Evidently she was rather wild. “Well,” he said, “I am not a courier, and yet she was very charming to me.”

“You had better have said at first,” said Mrs. Costello with dignity, “that you had made her acquaintance.”

“We simply met in the garden, and we talked a bit.”

“Tout bonnement! And pray what did you say?”

“I said I should take the liberty of introducing her to my admirable aunt.”

“I am much obliged to you.”

“It was to guarantee my respectability,” said Winterbourne.

“And pray who is to guarantee hers?”

“Ah, you are cruel!” said the young man. “She is a very nice young girl.”

“You do not say that as if you believed it,” Mrs. Costello observed.

“She is completely uncultivated,” Winterbourne went on. “But she is wonderfully pretty, and, in short, she is very nice. To prove that I believe it, I am going to take her to the Chateau de Chillon.”

“You two are going off there together? I should say it proved just the contrary. How long had you known her, may I ask, when this interesting project was formed? You have not been twenty-four hours in the house.”

“I have known her half an hour!” said Winterbourne, smiling.

“Dear me!” cried Mrs. Costello. “What a dreadful girl!”

Her nephew was silent for some moments. “You really think, then,” he began earnestly, and with a desire for trustworthy information--“you really think that--” But he paused again.

“Think what, sir?” said his aunt.

“That she is the sort of young lady who expects a man, sooner or later, to carry her off?”

“I have not the least idea what such young ladies expect a man to do. But I really think that you had better not meddle with little American girls that are uncultivated, as you call them. You have lived too long out of the country. You will be sure to make some great mistake. You are too innocent.”

“My dear aunt, I am not so innocent,” said Winterbourne, smiling and curling his mustache.

“You are guilty too, then!”

Winterbourne continued to curl his mustache meditatively. “You will not let the poor girl know you then?” he asked at last.

“Is it literally true that she is going to the Chateau de Chillon with you?”

“I think that she fully intends it.”

“Then, my dear Frederick,” said Mrs. Costello, “I must decline the honor of her acquaintance. I am an old woman, but I am not too old, thank Heaven, to be shocked!”

“But do they not all do these things--the young girls in America?” Winterbourne inquired.

Mrs. Costello stared a moment. “I should like to see my granddaughters do them!” she declared grimly.

This seemed to throw some light upon the matter, for Winterbourne remembered to have heard that his pretty cousins in New York were “tremendous flirts.” If, therefore, Miss Daisy Miller exceeded the liberal margin allowed to these young ladies, it was probable that anything might be expected of her. Winterbourne was impatient to see her again. And he was vexed with himself that, by instinct, he should not appreciate her justly.

Though he was impatient to see her, he hardly knew what he should say to her about his aunt’s refusal to become acquainted with her. But he discovered, promptly enough, that with Miss Daisy Miller there was no great need of walking on tiptoe. He found her that evening in the garden, wandering about in the warm starlight like an indolent sylph, and swinging to and fro the largest fan he had ever beheld. It was ten o’clock. He had dined with his aunt, had been sitting with her since dinner, and had just taken leave of her till the morrow. Miss Daisy Miller seemed very glad to see him. She declared it was the longest evening she had ever passed.

“Have you been all alone?” he asked.

“I have been walking round with mother. But mother gets tired walking round,” she answered.

“Has she gone to bed?”

“No; she does not like to go to bed,” said the young girl. “She does not sleep--not three hours. She says she does not know how she lives. She is dreadfully nervous. I guess she sleeps more than she thinks. She has gone somewhere after Randolph. She wants to try to get him to go to bed. He does not like to go to bed.”

“Let us hope she will persuade him,” observed Winterbourne.

“She will talk to him all she can. But he does not like her to talk to him,” said Miss Daisy, opening her fan. “She is going to try to get Eugenio to talk to him. But he is not afraid of Eugenio. Eugenio is a wonderful courier, but he cannot make much impression on Randolph! I do not believe he will go to bed before eleven.” It appeared that Randolph’s watch was in fact successfully made longer. Winterbourne walked about with the young girl for some time without meeting her mother. “I have been looking round for that lady you want to introduce me to,” his companion resumed. “She is your aunt.” Then Winterbourne admitted the fact and expressed some curiosity about how she had learned it. She said she had heard all about Mrs. Costello from the chambermaid. She was very quiet and very proper. She wore white puffs. She spoke to no one, and she never ate at the common table. Every two days she had a headache. “I think that is a lovely description, headache and all!” said Miss Daisy, talking along in her thin, gay voice. “I want to know her very much. I know just what YOUR aunt would be. I know I should like her. She would be very exclusive. I like a lady to be exclusive. I am dying to be exclusive myself. Well, we ARE exclusive, mother and I. We do not speak to everyone--or they do not speak to us. I suppose it is about the same thing. Anyway, I shall be very glad to know your aunt.”

Winterbourne was embarrassed. “She would be most happy,” he said. “But I am afraid those headaches will interfere.”

The young girl looked at him through the dark. “But I suppose she does not have a headache every day,” she said with sympathy.

Winterbourne was silent a moment. “She tells me she does,” he answered at last. He did not know what to say.

Miss Daisy Miller stopped and stood looking at him. Her beauty was still visible in the darkness. She was opening and closing her enormous fan. “She does not want to know me!” she said suddenly. “Why do you not say so? You do not need to be afraid. I am not afraid!” And she gave a little laugh.

Winterbourne thought there was a shake in her voice. He was touched, shocked, and made to feel shame by it. “My dear young lady,” he protested, “she knows no one. It is her bad health.”

The young girl walked on a few steps, laughing still. “You do not need to be afraid,” she repeated. “Why should she want to know me?” Then she paused again. She was close to the low wall of the garden, and in front of her was the lake under the stars. There was a vague shine upon its surface, and in the distance mountain forms were seen faintly. Daisy Miller looked out upon the mysterious view and then she gave another little laugh. “Good heavens! she IS exclusive!” she said. Winterbourne wondered whether she was seriously hurt. For a moment he almost wished that her sense of injury might be such as to make it proper for him to try to reassure and comfort her. He had a pleasant sense that she would be very open to comfort. He felt then, for the instant, quite ready to give up his aunt in talk. He was ready to admit that she was a proud, impolite woman, and to declare that they did not need to mind her. But before he had time to commit himself to this dangerous mixture of gallantry and disrespect, the young lady resumed her walk. She gave an exclamation in quite another tone. “Well, here is Mother! I guess she has not got Randolph to go to bed.” The figure of a lady appeared at a distance, very unclear in the darkness, and advancing with a slow and unsteady movement. Suddenly it seemed to pause.

“Are you sure it is your mother? Can you distinguish her in this thick dark?” Winterbourne asked.

“Well!” cried Miss Daisy Miller with a laugh. “I guess I know my own mother. And when she has got on my shawl, too! She is always wearing my things.”

The lady in question stopped advancing. She moved vaguely about the spot where she had checked her steps.

“I am afraid your mother does not see you,” said Winterbourne. “Or perhaps,” he added, thinking, with Miss Miller, the joke permissible--“perhaps she feels guilty about your shawl.”

“Oh, it is a fearful old thing!” the young girl replied calmly. “I told her she could wear it. She will not come here because she sees you.”

“Ah, then,” said Winterbourne, “I had better leave you.”

“Oh, no; come on!” urged Miss Daisy Miller.

“I am afraid your mother does not approve of my walking with you.”

Miss Miller gave him a serious glance. “It is not for me; it is for you--that is, it is for HER. Well, I do not know who it is for! But mother does not like any of my gentlemen friends. She is right down timid. She always makes a fuss if I introduce a gentleman. But I DO introduce them--almost always. If I did not introduce my gentlemen friends to Mother,” the young girl added in her little soft, flat monotone, “I should not think I was natural.”

“To introduce me,” said Winterbourne, “you must know my name.” And he proceeded to pronounce it.

“Oh, dear, I cannot say all that!” said his companion with a laugh. But by this time they had come up to Mrs. Miller. As they drew near, she walked to the low wall of the garden and leaned upon it. She looked intently at the lake and turned her back to them. “Mother!” said the young girl in a tone of decision. Upon this the elder lady turned round. “Mr. Winterbourne,” said Miss Daisy Miller, introducing the young man very frankly and prettily. “Common,” she was, as Mrs. Costello had pronounced her. Yet it was a wonder to Winterbourne that, with her commonness, she had a surprisingly delicate grace.

Her mother was a small, spare, light person, with a wandering eye, a very small nose, and a large forehead. Her forehead was decorated with a certain amount of thin, much curled hair. Like her daughter, Mrs. Miller was dressed with extreme elegance. She had enormous diamonds in her ears. So far as Winterbourne could observe, she gave him no greeting. She certainly was not looking at him. Daisy was near her, pulling her shawl straight. “What are you doing, poking round here?” this young lady inquired. But her accent was by no means as harsh as her choice of words may imply.

“I do not know,” said her mother, turning toward the lake again.

“I should not think you would want that shawl!” Daisy exclaimed.

“Well I do!” her mother answered with a little laugh.

“Did you get Randolph to go to bed?” asked the young girl.

“No; I could not make him,” said Mrs. Miller very gently. “He wants to talk to the waiter. He likes to talk to that waiter.”

“I was telling Mr. Winterbourne,” the young girl went on. And to the young man’s ear her tone might have indicated that she had been saying his name all her life.

“Oh, yes!” said Winterbourne. “I have the pleasure of knowing your son.”

Randolph’s mother was silent. She turned her attention to the lake. But at last she spoke. “Well, I do not see how he lives!”

“Anyhow, it is not so bad as it was at Dover,” said Daisy Miller.

“And what happened at Dover?” Winterbourne asked.

“He would not go to bed at all. I guess he sat up all night in the public sitting room. He was not in bed at twelve o’clock. I know that.”

“It was half-past twelve,” declared Mrs. Miller with mild emphasis.

“Does he sleep much during the day?” Winterbourne asked.

“I guess he does not sleep much,” Daisy answered.

“I wish he would!” said her mother. “It seems as if he could not.”

“I think he is really tiresome,” Daisy went on.

Then, for some moments, there was silence. “Well, Daisy Miller,” said the elder lady, after a moment, “I should not think you would want to talk against your own brother!”

“Well, he IS tiresome, Mother,” said Daisy, quite without the sharpness of a reply.

“He is only nine,” urged Mrs. Miller.

CHAPTER 3. Part 3

“Well, he would not go to that castle,” said the young girl. “I am going there with Mr. Winterbourne.”

Daisy’s mother gave no answer to this announcement. It was made very quietly. Winterbourne thought she strongly did not approve of the planned trip. But he said to himself that she was a simple person who was easy to manage. He thought a few polite words of protest would soften her displeasure. “Yes,” he began. “Your daughter has kindly given me the honor of being her guide.”

Mrs. Miller’s wandering eyes fixed themselves on Daisy with a sort of appealing look. Daisy, however, walked a few steps farther and sang softly to herself. “I suppose you will go in the cars,” said her mother.

“Yes, or in the boat,” said Winterbourne.

“Well, of course, I do not know,” Mrs. Miller answered. “I have never been to that castle.”

“It is a shame you should not go,” said Winterbourne. He was beginning to feel less worried about her opposition. And yet he was quite prepared to find that, as a matter of course, she meant to go with her daughter.

“We have been thinking about going very much,” she went on. “But it seems as if we could not. Of course Daisy—she wants to go around. But there is a lady here—I do not know her name—she says she should not think we would want to go to see castles here. She should think we would want to wait until we got to Italy. It seems as if there would be so many there,” Mrs. Miller continued with an air of growing confidence. “Of course we only want to see the main ones. We visited several in England,” she added soon after.

“Ah yes! In England there are beautiful castles,” said Winterbourne. “But Chillon here is very well worth seeing.”

“Well, if Daisy feels strong enough for it—” said Mrs. Miller. Her tone showed that she felt the size of the undertaking. “It seems as if there was nothing she would not try.”

“Oh, I think she will enjoy it!” Winterbourne declared. And he wanted more and more to be sure that he would have the privilege of a private talk with the young lady. She was still walking along in front of them, softly singing. “Are you not willing, madam,” he asked, “to undertake it yourself?”

Daisy’s mother looked at him sideways for a moment, and then walked forward in silence. Then—“I guess she had better go alone,” she said simply. Winterbourne said to himself that this was a very different type of motherhood from that of the watchful married women who placed themselves at the front of social life in the dark old city at the other end of the lake. But his thoughts were interrupted by hearing his name very clearly said by Mrs. Miller’s unprotected daughter.

“Mr. Winterbourne!” murmured Daisy.

“Mademoiselle!” said the young man.

“Do you not want to take me out in a boat?”

“Now?” he asked.

“Of course!” said Daisy.

“Well, Annie Miller!” her mother cried out.

“I beg you, madam, to let her go,” said Winterbourne with strong feeling. He had never yet enjoyed the feeling of guiding a small boat through the summer starlight with a fresh and beautiful young girl in it.

“I should not think she would want to,” said her mother. “I should think she would rather go indoors.”

“I am sure Mr. Winterbourne wants to take me,” Daisy declared. “He is so awfully devoted!”

“I will row you over to Chillon in the starlight.”

“I do not believe it!” said Daisy.

“Well!” the older lady cried out again.

“You have not spoken to me for half an hour,” her daughter went on.

“I have been having some very pleasant conversation with your mother,” said Winterbourne.

“Well, I want you to take me out in a boat!” Daisy repeated. They had all stopped, and she had turned round and was looking at Winterbourne. Her face wore a charming smile, her pretty eyes were shining, and she was swinging her great fan about. No, it is impossible to be prettier than that, thought Winterbourne.

“There are half a dozen boats tied at that landing place,” he said. He pointed to certain steps which went down from the garden to the lake. “If you will do me the honor to accept my arm, we will go and choose one of them.”

Daisy stood there smiling. She threw back her head and gave a little, light laugh. “I like a gentleman to be formal!” she declared.

“I assure you it is a formal offer.”

“I was determined to make you say something,” Daisy went on.

“You see, it is not very difficult,” said Winterbourne. “But I am afraid you are teasing me.”

“I think not, sir,” remarked Mrs. Miller very gently.

“Then do let me give you a row,” he said to the young girl.

“It is quite lovely, the way you say that!” cried Daisy.

“It will be still more lovely to do it.”

“Yes, it would be lovely!” said Daisy. But she made no movement to go with him. She only stood there laughing.

“I should think you had better find out what time it is,” her mother put in.

“It is eleven o’clock, madam,” said a voice with a foreign accent from the darkness nearby. Winterbourne turned and saw the florid person who was in attendance upon the two ladies. He had apparently just come near.

“Oh, Eugenio,” said Daisy, “I am going out in a boat!”

Eugenio bowed. “At eleven o’clock, mademoiselle?”

“I am going with Mr. Winterbourne—this very minute.”

“Do tell her she cannot,” said Mrs. Miller to the courier.

“I think you had better not go out in a boat, mademoiselle,” Eugenio declared.

Winterbourne wished to Heaven that this pretty girl were not so familiar with her courier. But he said nothing.

“I suppose you do not think it is proper!” Daisy cried out. “Eugenio does not think anything is proper.”

“I am at your service,” said Winterbourne.

“Does mademoiselle propose to go alone?” asked Eugenio of Mrs. Miller.

“Oh, no; with this gentleman!” answered Daisy’s mother.

The courier looked for a moment at Winterbourne—the latter thought he was smiling—and then, solemnly, with a bow, “As mademoiselle pleases!” he said.

“Oh, I hoped you would make a fuss!” said Daisy. “I do not care to go now.”

“I myself shall make a fuss if you do not go,” said Winterbourne.

“That is all I want—a little fuss!” And the young girl began to laugh again.

“Mr. Randolph has gone to bed!” the courier announced coldly.

“Oh, Daisy; now we can go!” said Mrs. Miller.

Daisy turned away from Winterbourne, looking at him, smiling and fanning herself. “Good night,” she said. “I hope you are disappointed, or disgusted, or something!”

He looked at her and took the hand she offered him. “I am puzzled,” he answered.

“Well, I hope it will not keep you awake!” she said very smartly. And, with the privileged Eugenio leading them, the two ladies passed toward the house.

Winterbourne stood looking after them. He was indeed puzzled. He stayed beside the lake for a quarter of an hour, turning over the mystery of the young girl’s sudden familiarities and caprices. But the only very definite conclusion he came to was that he should enjoy very much “going off” with her somewhere.

Two days later, he went off with her to the Castle of Chillon. He waited for her in the large hall of the hotel. There, the couriers, the servants, and the foreign tourists were standing about and staring. It was not the place he would have chosen, but she had chosen it. She came down the stairs quickly. She was buttoning her long gloves and holding her folded parasol against her pretty figure. She was dressed in the perfection of a simple, elegant traveling costume. Winterbourne was a man of imagination and, as our ancestors used to say, sensibility. He looked at her dress and at her small, quick, trusting steps on the great staircase. He felt as if something romantic was happening. He could have believed he was going to elope with her. He went out with her among all the idle people gathered there. They were all looking at her very hard. She had begun to chatter as soon as she joined him. Winterbourne would have preferred to take a carriage to Chillon. But she expressed a lively wish to go in the little steamer. She declared that she had a passion for steamboats. There was always such a lovely breeze on the water, and you saw so many people. The sail was not long, but Winterbourne’s companion found time to say a great many things. To the young man himself, their little excursion was so much of an escapade—an adventure—that, even allowing for her habitual sense of freedom, he expected her to see it the same way. But it must be confessed that, in this particular, he was disappointed. Daisy Miller was extremely animated, and she was in charming spirits. But she was apparently not at all excited. She was not fluttered. She avoided neither his eyes nor anyone else’s. She blushed neither when she looked at him nor when she felt that people were looking at her. People continued to look at her a great deal. Winterbourne took much satisfaction in his pretty companion’s distinguished air. He had been a little afraid that she would talk loud, laugh overmuch, and even, perhaps, want to move about the boat a good deal. But he quite forgot his fears. He sat smiling, with his eyes upon her face. Without moving from her place, she delivered a great number of original reflections. It was the most charming garrulity he had ever heard. He had assented to the idea that she was “common.” But was she so, after all? Or was he simply getting used to her commonness? Her conversation was chiefly of what metaphysicians call the objective cast. But every now and then it took a subjective turn.

“What on EARTH are you so grave about?” she suddenly demanded. She fixed her agreeable eyes upon Winterbourne’s.

“Am I grave?” he asked. “I had an idea I was grinning from ear to ear.”

“You look as if you were taking me to a funeral. If that’s a grin, your ears are very near together.”

“Should you like me to dance a hornpipe on the deck?”

“Pray do, and I’ll carry round your hat. It will pay the expenses of our journey.”

“I never was better pleased in my life,” murmured Winterbourne.

She looked at him a moment and then burst into a little laugh. “I like to make you say those things! You’re a queer mixture!”

In the castle, after they had landed, the subjective element decidedly prevailed. Daisy tripped about the vaulted chambers. She rustled her skirts in the corkscrew staircases. She flirted back with a pretty little cry and a shudder from the edge of the oubliettes. She turned a singularly well-shaped ear to everything that Winterbourne told her about the place. But he saw that she cared very little for feudal antiquities. The dusky traditions of Chillon made only a slight impression upon her. They had the good fortune to be able to walk about without other companionship than that of the custodian. Winterbourne arranged with this functionary that they should not be hurried. They should linger and pause wherever they chose. The custodian interpreted the bargain generously—Winterbourne, on his side, had been generous—and ended by leaving them quite to themselves. Miss Miller’s observations were not remarkable for logical consistency. For anything she wanted to say, she was sure to find a pretext. She found a great many pretexts in the rugged embrasures of Chillon. She used them for asking Winterbourne sudden questions about himself—his family, his previous history, his tastes, his habits, his intentions. She also used them for supplying information upon corresponding points in her own personality. Of her own tastes, habits, and intentions, Miss Miller was prepared to give the most definite, and indeed the most favorable, account.

“Well, I hope you know enough!” she said to her companion, after he had told her the history of the unhappy Bonivard. “I never saw a man that knew so much!” The history of Bonivard had evidently, as they say, gone into one ear and out of the other. But Daisy went on to say that she wished Winterbourne would travel with them and “go round” with them. They might know something, in that case. “Don’t you want to come and teach Randolph?” she asked. Winterbourne said that nothing could possibly please him so much, but that he had unfortunately other occupations. “Other occupations? I don’t believe it!” said Miss Daisy. “What do you mean? You are not in business.” The young man admitted that he was not in business. But he had engagements which, even within a day or two, would force him to go back to Geneva. “Oh, bother!” she said. “I don’t believe it!” And she began to talk about something else. But a few moments later, when he was pointing out to her the pretty design of an antique fireplace, she broke out irrelevantly, “You don’t mean to say you are going back to Geneva?”

“It is a melancholy fact that I shall have to return to Geneva tomorrow.”

“Well, Mr. Winterbourne,” said Daisy, “I think you’re horrid!”

“Oh, don’t say such dreadful things!” said Winterbourne—“just at the last!”

"The last!" cried the young girl. "I call it the first. I almost want to leave you here and go straight back to the hotel alone." For the next ten minutes she did nothing but call him awful. Poor Winterbourne was very confused. No young lady had ever been so upset by the news of his plans. After this, his companion stopped paying attention to the sights of Chillon or the beauty of the lake. She began to ask about the mysterious woman in Geneva. She seemed to think at once that he was rushing back to see her. How did Miss Daisy Miller know there was a woman in Geneva? Winterbourne said no such person existed. He could not find out how she knew. He was torn between surprise at how fast she had guessed and fun at how openly she joked. She seemed to him a strange mix of innocence and roughness. "Does she never let you stay more than three days at a time?" asked Daisy with a smile. "Doesn't she give you a holiday in summer? No one works so hard that they cannot get leave to go somewhere at this time of year. I suppose, if you stay another day, she will come after you in the boat. Do wait until Friday, and I will go down to the landing to see her arrive!" Winterbourne began to think he had been wrong to feel let down by the mood in which the young lady had started. If he had missed her personal tone, her personal tone was now showing. It sounded very clearly at last when she told him she would stop "teasing" him if he would promise her seriously to come down to Rome in the winter.

"That is not a hard promise to make," said Winterbourne. "My aunt has taken an apartment in Rome for the winter. She has already asked me to come and see her."

"I do not want you to come for your aunt," said Daisy. "I want you to come for me." And this was the only time the young man would ever hear her speak of his disliked relative. He said that, in any case, he would certainly come. After this Daisy stopped teasing. Winterbourne took a carriage, and they drove back to Vevey in the fading light. The young girl was very quiet.

In the evening Winterbourne told Mrs. Costello that he had spent the afternoon at Chillon with Miss Daisy Miller.

"The Americans--of the courier?" asked this lady.

"Ah, luckily," said Winterbourne, "the courier stayed at home."

"She went with you all alone?"

"All alone."

Mrs. Costello sniffed a little at her smelling bottle. "And that," she said, "is the young person whom you wanted me to know!"

CHAPTER 4. Part 4

Winterbourne went back to Geneva the day after his trip to Chillon. Then he went to Rome near the end of January. His aunt had been living there for several weeks. He had received two letters from her. "Those people you liked so much last summer at Vevey have appeared here, with their courier and everyone," she wrote. "They seem to have made several friends. But the courier is still the closest to them. The young lady is also very friendly with some low-quality Italians. She goes around with them in a way that causes a lot of talk. Bring me that nice novel by Cherbuliez--Paule Mere--and do not come later than the 23rd."

In the normal course of things, Winterbourne would soon have found Mrs. Miller's address at the American bank and gone to pay his respects to Miss Daisy. "After what happened at Vevey, I think I may surely visit them," he said to Mrs. Costello.

"If, after what happens--at Vevey and everywhere--you want to keep up the friendship, you are very welcome. Of course a man may know everyone. Men are welcome to that privilege!"

"Please tell me what it is that happens--here, for example?" Winterbourne asked.

"The girl goes around alone with her foreigners. As for what happens further, you must ask someone else for information. She has picked up half a dozen of the usual Roman men who chase rich women. She takes them to people's houses. When she comes to a party, she brings a gentleman with good manners and a wonderful mustache."

"And where is the mother?"

"I have no idea at all. They are very bad people."

Winterbourne thought for a moment. "They are very uneducated--very innocent only. I am sure they are not bad."

"They are hopelessly common," said Mrs. Costello. "Whether being hopelessly common is being 'bad' is a question for philosophers. They are bad enough to dislike, at any rate. And for this short life that is quite enough."

The news that Daisy Miller was surrounded by half a dozen wonderful mustaches stopped Winterbourne's urge to go straight to see her. Perhaps he had not definitely told himself that he had made a permanent impression on her heart. But he was annoyed to hear of a situation so different from an image that had recently come and gone in his own thoughts. The image was of a very pretty girl looking out of an old Roman window. She was asking herself urgently when Mr. Winterbourne would arrive. However, he decided to wait a little before reminding Miss Miller of his claims to her attention. But he went very soon to visit two or three other friends. One of these friends was an American lady who had spent several winters at Geneva. She had placed her children at school there. She was a very skilled woman, and she lived in the Via Gregoriana. Winterbourne found her in a little dark red sitting room on the third floor. The room was filled with southern sunshine. He had not been there ten minutes when the servant came in. The servant announced "Madame Mila!" This announcement was soon followed by the entrance of little Randolph Miller. He stopped in the middle of the room and stood staring at Winterbourne. A moment later his pretty sister crossed the threshold. Then, after a considerable interval, Mrs. Miller slowly came forward.

"I know you!" said Randolph.

"I am sure you know a great many things," said Winterbourne, taking him by the hand. "How is your education coming on?"

Daisy was greeting her hostess very prettily. But when she heard Winterbourne's voice she quickly turned her head. "Well, I declare!" she said.

"I told you I should come, you know," Winterbourne replied, smiling.

"Well, I did not believe it," said Miss Daisy.

"I am much obliged to you," laughed the young man.

"You might have come to see me!" said Daisy.

"I arrived only yesterday."

"I do not believe that!" the young girl declared.

Winterbourne turned with a protesting smile to her mother. But this lady avoided his glance. She sat down and fixed her eyes upon her son. "We have got a bigger place than this," said Randolph. "It is all gold on the walls."

Mrs. Miller turned uneasily in her chair. "I told you if I were to bring you, you would say something!" she murmured.

"I told YOU!" Randolph said. "I tell YOU, sir!" he added jokingly, giving Winterbourne a thump on the knee. "It IS bigger, too!"

Daisy had entered upon a lively conversation with her hostess. Winterbourne judged it proper to address a few words to her mother. "I hope you have been well since we parted at Vevey," he said.

Mrs. Miller now certainly looked at him--at his chin. "Not very well, sir," she answered.

"She has got the dyspepsia," said Randolph. "I have got it too. Father has got it. I have got it most!"

This announcement, instead of embarrassing Mrs. Miller, seemed to relieve her. "I suffer from the liver," she said. "I think it is this climate. It is less healthy than Schenectady, especially in the winter season. I do not know whether you know we live at Schenectady. I was saying to Daisy that I certainly had not found anyone like Dr. Davis. And I did not believe I should. Oh, at Schenectady he stands first. They think everything of him. He has so much to do, and yet there was nothing he would not do for me. He said he never saw anything like my dyspepsia, but he was bound to cure it. I am sure there was nothing he would not try. He was just going to try something new when we came away. Mr. Miller wanted Daisy to see Europe for herself. But I wrote to Mr. Miller that it seems as if I could not get on without Dr. Davis. At Schenectady he stands at the very top. And there is a great deal of sickness there, too. It affects my sleep."

Winterbourne had a good deal of medical gossip with Dr. Davis's patient. During this, Daisy chattered without stopping to her own companion. The young man asked Mrs. Miller how she was pleased with Rome. "Well, I must say I am disappointed," she answered. "We had heard so much about it. I suppose we had heard too much. But we could not help that. We had been led to expect something different."

"Ah, wait a little, and you will become very fond of it," said Winterbourne.

"I hate it worse and worse every day!" cried Randolph.

"You are like the infant Hannibal," said Winterbourne.

"No, I am not!" Randolph declared at a guess.

"You are not much like an infant," said his mother. "But we have seen places," she resumed, "that I should put a long way before Rome." And in reply to Winterbourne's question, "There is Zurich," she concluded. "I think Zurich is lovely. And we had not heard half so much about it."

"The best place we have seen is the City of Richmond!" said Randolph.

"He means the ship," his mother explained. "We crossed in that ship. Randolph had a good time on the City of Richmond."

“It is the best place I have seen,” the child repeated. “Only it was turned the wrong way.”

“Well, we have to turn the right way some time,” said Mrs. Miller with a little laugh. Winterbourne said he hoped her daughter at least found some pleasure in Rome. Mrs. Miller said that Daisy was quite carried away. “It is because of the people--the people are wonderful. She goes round everywhere. She has made a great number of friends. Of course she goes round more than I do. I must say they have been very friendly. They have taken her right in. And then she knows a great many gentlemen. Oh, she thinks there is nothing like Rome. Of course, it is a great deal nicer for a young lady if she knows plenty of gentlemen.”

By this time Daisy had turned her attention again to Winterbourne. “I have been telling Mrs. Walker how mean you were!” the young girl announced.

“And what is the evidence you have offered?” asked Winterbourne. He was rather annoyed at Miss Miller’s lack of appreciation. He was a friend who on his way down to Rome had stopped neither at Bologna nor at Florence, simply because of a certain tender impatience. He remembered that a bitter fellow countryman had once told him that American women--the pretty ones, and this gave a largeness to the saying--were at once the most demanding in the world and the least thankful.

“Why, you were very mean at Vevey,” said Daisy. “You would not do anything. You would not stay there when I asked you.”

“My dearest young lady,” cried Winterbourne, with feeling, “have I come all the way to Rome to meet your blame?”

“Just hear him say that!” said Daisy to her hostess. She gave a twist to a bow on this lady’s dress. “Did you ever hear anything so strange?”

“So strange, my dear?” said Mrs. Walker in the tone of a supporter of Winterbourne.

“Well, I do not know,” said Daisy, touching Mrs. Walker’s ribbons. “Mrs. Walker, I want to tell you something.”

“Mother,” said Randolph, with his rough ends to his words, “I tell you you have to go. Eugenio will raise--something!”

“I am not afraid of Eugenio,” said Daisy with a toss of her head. “Look here, Mrs. Walker,” she went on, “you know I am coming to your party.”

“I am delighted to hear it.”

“I have got a lovely dress!”

“I am very sure of that.”

“But I want to ask a favor--permission to bring a friend.”

“I shall be happy to see any of your friends,” said Mrs. Walker, turning with a smile to Mrs. Miller.

“Oh, they are not my friends,” answered Daisy’s mamma, smiling shyly in her own way. “I never spoke to them.”

“It is a close friend of mine--Mr. Giovanelli,” said Daisy without a shake in her clear little voice or a shadow on her bright little face.

Mrs. Walker was silent a moment. She gave a quick glance at Winterbourne. “I shall be glad to see Mr. Giovanelli,” she then said.

“He is an Italian,” Daisy went on with the prettiest calm. “He is a great friend of mine. He is the most handsome man in the world--except Mr. Winterbourne! He knows plenty of Italians, but he wants to know some Americans. He thinks very much of Americans. He is very clever. He is perfectly lovely!”

It was settled that this brilliant person should be brought to Mrs. Walker’s party. Then Mrs. Miller prepared to leave. “I guess we will go back to the hotel,” she said.

And then she must seem to him very pretty and interesting. I rather doubt that he dreams of marrying her. That must appear to him too impossible a piece of luck. He has nothing but his handsome face to offer. And there is a rich Mr. Miller in that mysterious land of dollars. Giovanelli knows that he has no title to offer. If he were only a count or a marchese! He must wonder at his luck, at the way they have taken him up."

"He explains it by his handsome face and thinks Miss Miller a young lady who just follows her fancies!" said Mrs. Costello.

"It is very true," Winterbourne went on, "that Daisy and her mamma have not yet risen to that stage of--what shall I call it?--of culture at which the idea of catching a count or a marchese begins. I believe that they are not able to think of that."

"Ah! but the lawyer cannot believe it," said Mrs. Costello.

Winterbourne gathered enough proof that day at St. Peter's of the attention Daisy's "secret love" caused. A dozen of the American colonists in Rome came to talk with Mrs. Costello. She sat on a little portable stool at the base of one of the great pillars. The evening service was going forward in splendid songs and organ sounds in the nearby choir. Meanwhile, between Mrs. Costello and her friends, there was a great deal said about poor little Miss Miller's going really "too far." Winterbourne was not pleased with what he heard. But when he came out upon the great steps of the church, he saw Daisy. She had come out before him. He saw her get into an open cab with her friend and roll away through the mocking streets of Rome. He could not deny to himself that she was going very far indeed. He felt very sorry for her. It was not exactly that he believed she had completely lost her head. It was because it was painful to hear so much that was pretty, and undefended, and natural put in a common place among the types of disorder. He made an attempt after this to give a hint to Mrs. Miller. One day he met a friend in the Corso, a tourist like himself. His friend had just come out of the Doria Palace, where he had been walking through the beautiful gallery. His friend talked for a moment about the superb portrait of Innocent X by Velasquez that hangs in one of the rooms of the palace. Then he said, "And in the same room, by the way, I had the pleasure of looking at a picture of a different kind--that pretty American girl you pointed out to me last week." In answer to Winterbourne's questions, his friend said that the pretty American girl--prettier than ever--was seated with a companion in the quiet corner where the great papal portrait was kept.

"Who was her companion?" asked Winterbourne.

"A little Italian with a bouquet in his buttonhole. The girl is delightfully pretty, but I thought I understood from you the other day that she was a young lady of the best society."

"So she is!" answered Winterbourne. He made sure that his friend had seen Daisy and her companion only five minutes before. Then he jumped into a cab and went to call on Mrs. Miller. She was at home. But she apologized to him for receiving him while Daisy was absent.

"She has gone out somewhere with Mr. Giovanelli," said Mrs. Miller. "She is always going around with Mr. Giovanelli."

"I have noticed that they are very close," Winterbourne observed.

"Oh, it seems as if they could not live without each other!" said Mrs. Miller. "Well, he is a real gentleman, anyhow. I keep telling Daisy she is engaged!"

"And what does Daisy say?"

"Oh, she says she is not engaged. But she might as well be!" this fair parent went on; "she goes on as if she was. But I have made Mr. Giovanelli promise to tell me, if she does not. I should want to write to Mr. Miller about it--should not you?"

Winterbourne replied that he certainly should. But Daisy’s mother seemed so strange to him. She was so unlike anything he had ever heard of in stories of parents who watch their children. He gave up all attempts to warn her as completely useless.

After this, Daisy was never at home. Winterbourne stopped meeting her at the houses of their common friends. He saw that these clever people had quite made up their minds that she was going too far. They stopped inviting her. They let it be known that they wished to show careful Europeans a great truth. That truth was that, although Miss Daisy Miller was a young American lady, her behavior was not typical. Her own people regarded it as unusual. Winterbourne wondered how she felt about all the cold shoulders turned toward her. Sometimes it made him angry to suspect that she did not feel anything at all. He said to himself that she was too light and silly, too simple and foolish, too small-town, to have thought about being pushed out, or even to have noticed it. Then at other moments he believed that inside her elegant and careless little body she carried a brave, passionate, and fully alert awareness of the impression she made. He asked himself whether Daisy’s courage came from a sense of innocence, or from her being, at heart, a young person of the wild kind. It must be admitted that holding on to a belief in Daisy’s “innocence” came to seem to Winterbourne more and more a matter of fine, invented good manners. As I have already had reason to tell, he was angry to find himself reduced to arguing about this young lady. He was upset at his lack of a sure, natural sense of how far her odd ways were general and national, and how far they were personal. From either view of them, he had somehow missed her, and now it was too late. She was “carried away” by Mr. Giovanelli.

A few days after his short talk with her mother, he met her in that beautiful, flower-filled, ruined place known as the Palace of the Caesars. The early Roman spring had filled the air with flowers and sweet smells. The rough surface of the Palatine was covered with soft green growth. Daisy was walking along the top of one of those great mounds of ruin. These mounds were held up by marble covered in moss and paved with old stone writings. It seemed to him that Rome had never been so lovely as just then. He stood looking off at the charming harmony of line and color that circles the city from far away. He breathed in the soft, wet smells. He felt the newness of the year and the great age of the place both proving themselves in a mysterious mix. It seemed to him also that Daisy had never looked so pretty. But this had been his thought whenever he met her. Giovanelli was at her side. Giovanelli, too, wore a look of even unusual brightness.

“Well,” said Daisy, “I should think you would be lonely!”

“Lonely?” asked Winterbourne.

“You are always going round by yourself. Can’t you get anyone to walk with you?”

“I am not so lucky,” said Winterbourne, “as your companion.”

Giovanelli, from the first, had treated Winterbourne with great good manners. He listened with a respectful air to his remarks. He laughed carefully at his jokes. He seemed ready to show that he believed Winterbourne was a superior young man. He carried himself in no way like a jealous rival. He clearly had a great deal of skill with people. He had no objection to your expecting a little modesty from him. It even seemed to Winterbourne at times that Giovanelli would feel some mental relief in being able to have a private understanding with him. He would say to him, as an intelligent man, that, believe me, HE knew how extraordinary this young lady was. And he did not fool himself with false hopes—or at least not TOO false—of marriage and money. On this occasion he walked away from his companion to pick a small branch of almond flowers. He carefully arranged it in his buttonhole.

“I know why you say that,” said Daisy, watching Giovanelli. “Because you think I go round too much with HIM.” And she nodded at her attendant.

“Every one thinks so—if you care to know,” said Winterbourne.

“Of course I care to know!” Daisy exclaimed seriously. “But I don’t believe it. They are only pretending to be shocked. They don’t really care at all what I do. Besides, I don’t go round so much.”

“I think you will find they do care. They will show it in an unpleasant way.”

Daisy looked at him a moment. “How unpleasant?”

“Haven’t you noticed anything?” Winterbourne asked.

“I have noticed you. But I noticed you were as stiff as an umbrella the first time I saw you.”

“You will find I am not so stiff as several others,” said Winterbourne, smiling.

“How shall I find it?”

“By going to see the others.”

“What will they do to me?”

“They will give you the cold shoulder. Do you know what that means?”

Daisy was looking at him closely. She began to turn red. “Do you mean as Mrs. Walker did the other night?”

“Exactly!” said Winterbourne.

She looked away at Giovanelli, who was decorating himself with his almond flowers. Then looking back at Winterbourne, “I shouldn’t think you would let people be so unkind!” she said.

“How can I help it?” he asked.

“I should think you would say something.”

“I do say something;” and he paused a moment. “I say that your mother tells me that she believes you are engaged.”

“Well, she does,” said Daisy very simply.

Winterbourne began to laugh. “And does Randolph believe it?” he asked.

“I guess Randolph doesn’t believe anything,” said Daisy. Randolph’s doubt made Winterbourne laugh even more. He noticed that Giovanelli was coming back to them. Daisy noticed it too, and spoke again to her countryman. “Since you have mentioned it,” she said, “I AM engaged.” * * * Winterbourne looked at her. He had stopped laughing. “You don’t believe it!” she added.

He was silent a moment. Then, “Yes, I believe it,” he said.

“Oh, no, you don’t!” she answered. “Well, then—I am not!”

The young girl and her guide were on their way to the gate of the park. Winterbourne had only just arrived, so he soon said goodbye to them. A week later he went to eat dinner at a beautiful house on the Caelian Hill. When he arrived, he sent away his hired car. The evening was lovely. He promised himself the pleasure of walking home under the Arch of Constantine and past the dimly lit monuments of the Forum. There was a shrinking moon in the sky. Her light was not bright, but a thin cloud seemed to spread and even it out. When he returned from the house (it was eleven), Winterbourne came near the dark circle of the Colosseum. It came to his mind, as a lover of beautiful views, that the inside, in the pale moon light, would be well worth a look. He turned aside and walked to one of the empty arches. Near it, as he saw, an open car—one of the little Roman cabs—was parked. Then he passed in, among the deep shadows of the great structure, and came out upon the clear and silent arena. The place had never seemed more impressive to him. One half of the huge circle was in deep shade. The other half was sleeping in the bright dusk. As he stood there he began to say softly Byron’s famous lines from “Manfred.” But before he finished the quote, he remembered that poets recommend night thoughts in the Colosseum, but doctors advise against them. The historic air was there, certainly. But the historic air, scientifically considered, was no better than a bad mist. Winterbourne walked to the middle of the arena to take a more general look. He meant to leave quickly after that. The great cross in the center was covered with shadow. Only as he came near it did he make it out clearly. Then he saw that two persons were standing on the low steps which formed its base. One of them was a woman, seated. Her companion was standing in front of her.

Soon the sound of the woman’s voice came to him clearly in the warm night air. “Well, he looks at us as one of the old lions or tigers may have looked at the Christian martyrs!” These were the words he heard, in the familiar accent of Miss Daisy Miller.

“Let us hope he is not very hungry,” answered the clever Giovanelli. “He will have to take me first. You will serve for dessert!”

Winterbourne stopped, with a kind of horror, and, it must be added, with a kind of relief. It was as if a sudden light had been flashed upon the unclear meaning of Daisy’s behavior. The riddle had become easy to read. She was a young lady whom a gentleman need no longer take pains to respect. He stood there, looking at her—looking at her companion and not thinking that though he saw them dimly, he himself must have been more brightly visible. He felt angry with himself that he had worried so much about the right way of thinking about Miss Daisy Miller. Then, as he was going to step forward again, he checked himself. He did not stop from fear that he was doing her injustice, but from a sense of the danger of appearing improperly excited by this sudden change from careful criticism. He turned away toward the entrance of the place. But as he did so, he heard Daisy speak again.

“Why, it was Mr. Winterbourne! He saw me, and he cuts me!”

What a clever little bad girl she was, and how smartly she played at hurt innocence! But he would not cut her. Winterbourne came forward again and went toward the great cross. Daisy had got up. Giovanelli lifted his hat. Winterbourne had now begun to think simply of the craziness, from a health point of view, of a delicate young girl spending the evening in this nest of malaria. What if she WERE a clever little bad girl? That was no reason for her dying of the perniciosa. “How long have you been here?” he asked almost brutally.

Daisy, lovely in the flattering moon light, looked at him a moment. Then—“All the evening,” she answered, gently. * * * “I never saw anything so pretty.”

“I am afraid,” said Winterbourne, “that you will not think Roman fever very pretty. This is the way people catch it. I wonder,” he added, turning to Giovanelli, “that you, a native Roman, should support such a terrible lack of care.”

“Ah,” said the handsome native, “for myself I am not afraid.”

“Neither am I—for you! I am speaking for this young lady.”

Giovanelli lifted his well-shaped eyebrows and showed his brilliant teeth. But he took Winterbourne’s rebuke with meekness. “I told the signorina it was a grave lack of care, but when was the signorina ever prudent?”

“I never was sick, and I do not mean to be!” the signorina declared. “I do not look like much, but I’m healthy! I was bound to see the Colosseum by moon light. I should not have wanted to go home without that. And we have had the most beautiful time, haven’t we, Mr. Giovanelli? If there has been any danger, Eugenio can give me some pills. He has got some splendid pills.”

“I should advise you,” said Winterbourne, “to drive home as fast as possible and take one!”

“What you say is very wise,” Giovanelli rejoined. “I will go and make sure the car is at hand.” And he went forward rapidly.

Daisy followed with Winterbourne. He kept looking at her. She seemed not in the least embarrassed. Winterbourne said nothing. Daisy chattered about the beauty of the place. “Well, I HAVE seen the Colosseum by moon light!” she exclaimed. “That’s one good thing.” Then, noticing Winterbourne’s silence, she asked him why he did not speak. He made no answer. He only began to laugh. They passed under one of the dark arches. Giovanelli was in front with the car. Here Daisy stopped a moment, looking at the young American. “DID you believe I was engaged, the other day?” she asked.

“It does not matter what I believed the other day,” said Winterbourne, still laughing.

“Well, what do you believe now?”

“I believe that it makes very little difference whether you are engaged or not!”

He felt the young girl’s pretty eyes fixed upon him through the thick gloom of the arch. She was apparently going to answer. But Giovanelli hurried her forward. “Quick! quick!” he said. “If we get in by midnight we are quite safe.”

Daisy took her seat in the car, and the fortunate Italian placed himself beside her. “Do not forget Eugenio’s pills!” said Winterbourne as he lifted his hat.

“I do not care,” said Daisy in a little strange tone, “whether I have Roman fever or not!” Upon this the cab driver cracked his whip, and they rolled away over the uneven patches of the old pavement.

Winterbourne, to be fair to him, said nothing to anyone about meeting Miss Miller at midnight in the Colosseum with a gentleman. But two days later, every member of the small American circle knew she had been there in those circumstances. They talked about it. Winterbourne thought they had of course known it at the hotel. After Daisy returned, the servant at the door and the cab driver had exchanged remarks. But the young man also felt it no longer troubled him seriously that low-minded servants should talk about the little American woman who liked attention. A day or two later, these people had serious news to give: the little American woman who liked attention was very ill in a worrying way. When Winterbourne heard the rumor, he went to the hotel at once for more news. He found that two or three kind friends had arrived before him. Mrs. Miller was entertaining them in her sitting room, with Randolph.

"It goes around at night," said Randolph. "That is what made her sick. She is always going around at night. I should not think she would want to. It is so very dark. You cannot see anything here at night, except when there is a moon. In America there is always a moon!" Mrs. Miller was not visible. She was now, at least, giving her daughter the benefit of her company. It was clear that Daisy was very sick.

Winterbourne went often to ask for news of her. Once he saw Mrs. Miller. She was deeply worried, but, to his surprise, she was perfectly calm. She also seemed to be a most capable and sensible nurse. She talked a good deal about Dr. Davis. But Winterbourne said to himself that she was not, after all, such a very foolish woman. "Daisy spoke of you the other day," she said to him. "Half the time she does not know what she is saying. But that time I think she did. She gave me a message to tell you. She told me to tell you that she was never engaged to that handsome Italian. I am sure I am very glad. Mr. Giovanelli has not been near us since she was taken ill. I thought he was so much of a gentleman. But I do not call that very polite! A lady told me that he was afraid I was angry with him for taking Daisy around at night. Well, so I am. But I suppose he knows I am a lady. I would never lower myself to scold him. Anyway, she says she is not engaged. I do not know why she wanted you to know. But she said to me three times, 'Be sure to tell Mr. Winterbourne.' And then she told me to ask if you remembered the time you went to that castle in Switzerland. But I said I would not give any such messages as that. Only, if she is not engaged, I am sure I am glad to know it."

But, as Winterbourne had said, it mattered very little. A week after this, the poor girl died. It had been a terrible case of the fever. Daisy's grave was in the little Protestant cemetery. It lay in a corner of the wall of imperial Rome, beneath the cypress trees and the thick spring flowers. Winterbourne stood there beside it, with a number of other mourners. The number was larger than the scandal excited by the young lady's life would have led you to expect. Near him stood Giovanelli. He came nearer still before Winterbourne turned away. Giovanelli was very pale. On this occasion he had no flower in his buttonhole. He seemed to wish to say something. At last he said, "She was the most beautiful young lady I ever saw, and the most friendly." And then he added in a moment, "And she was the most innocent."

Winterbourne looked at him and soon repeated his words, "And the most innocent?"

"The most innocent!"

Winterbourne felt hurt and angry. "Why the devil," he asked, "did you take her to that fatal place?"

Mr. Giovanelli's politeness was apparently calm and steady. He looked on the ground a moment, and then he said, "For myself I had no fear. And she wanted to go."

"That was no reason!" Winterbourne declared.

The subtle Roman again dropped his eyes. "If she had lived, I should have got nothing. She would never have married me, I am sure."

"She would never have married you?"

"For a moment I hoped so. But no. I am sure."

Winterbourne listened to him. He stood staring at the fresh mound among the April daisies. When he turned away again, Mr. Giovanelli, with his light, slow step, had gone.

Winterbourne almost immediately left Rome. But the following summer he again met his aunt, Mrs. Costello, at Vevey. Mrs. Costello was fond of Vevey. In the interval Winterbourne had often thought of Daisy Miller and her puzzling manners. One day he spoke of her to his aunt. He said it was on his conscience that he had done her wrong.

"I am sure I do not know," said Mrs. Costello. "How did your wrong affect her?"

"She sent me a message before her death which I did not understand at the time. But I have understood it since. She would have valued one's respect."

"Is that a modest way," asked Mrs. Costello, "of saying that she would have returned one's affection?"

Winterbourne offered no answer to this question. But he soon said, "You were right in that remark that you made last summer. I was sure to make a mistake. I have lived too long in foreign parts."

Nevertheless, he went back to live at Geneva. From there, the most contradictory accounts of his reasons for staying continue to come. One report says that he is "studying" hard. Another suggests that he is much interested in a very clever foreign lady.
