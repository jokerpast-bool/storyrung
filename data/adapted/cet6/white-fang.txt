CHAPTER 1. THE MAKERS OF FIRE

THE MAKERS OF FIRE

The young wolf came upon it suddenly. It was his own fault. He had not been careful. He had left the cave and run down to the stream to drink. Maybe he did not notice because he was heavy with sleep. (He had been out all night on the meat-trail, and had just then awakened.) And his lack of care might have been because the trail to the pool was so familiar. He had travelled it often, and nothing had ever happened on it.

He went down past the blasted pine, crossed the open space, and walked quickly among the trees. Then, at the same moment, he saw and smelt. Before him, sitting silently on their back legs, were five live things. He had never seen anything like them before. It was his first glimpse of human beings. But when they saw him, the five men did not jump to their feet. They did not show their teeth or make angry sounds. They did not move. They just sat there, silent and threatening.

Nor did the young wolf move. Every instinct in him would have pushed him to run wildly away. But suddenly, for the first time, another opposite instinct rose in him. A great fear and wonder came over him. He was frozen in place by a huge sense of his own weakness and smallness. Here was power and control, something far beyond him.

The young wolf had never seen man, yet the instinct about man was his. In dim ways he knew that man was the animal that had fought its way to the top over the other animals of the Wild. The young wolf was now looking at man not only with his own eyes, but with the eyes of all his ancestors. Those eyes had circled in the darkness around countless winter camp-fires. They had looked from safe distances and from deep inside bushes at the strange, two-legged animal that was master over living things. The spell of the young wolf's heritage was on him. It was the fear and respect born of centuries of struggle and the collected experience of the generations. The heritage was too strong for a wolf that was only young. If he had been full-grown, he would have run away. As it was, he lowered himself down frozen with fear. He was already half offering the yielding that his kind had offered from the first time a wolf came in to sit by the fire of man and be made warm.

One of the Indians stood up and walked over to him and bent down above him. The young wolf lowered himself closer to the ground. It was the unknown, made real at last, in solid flesh and blood, bending over him and reaching down to grab him. His hair stood up on its own. His lips pulled back and his little teeth were shown. The hand, hanging above him like fate, stopped. The man spoke laughing, "_Wabam wabisca ip pit tah_." ("Look! The white teeth!")

The other Indians laughed loudly, and urged the man on to pick up the young wolf. As the hand came down closer and closer, a battle of instincts raged inside the young wolf. He felt two great pushes—to yield and to fight. The resulting action was a compromise. He did both. He yielded until the hand almost touched him. Then he fought. His teeth flashed in a snap that sank them into the hand. The next moment he received a blow on the side of the head that knocked him over on his side. Then all fight left him. His young age and the instinct to yield took charge of him. He sat up on his back legs and cried. But the man whose hand he had bitten was angry. The young wolf received a blow on the other side of his head. Then he sat up and cried louder than ever.

The four Indians laughed more loudly. Even the man who had been bitten began to laugh. They surrounded the young wolf and laughed at him, while he cried out his terror and his hurt. In the middle of it, he heard something. The Indians heard it too. But the young wolf knew what it was. With a last, long cry that had more of triumph in it than grief, he stopped his noise and waited for his mother to come. His fierce and strong mother who fought and killed all things and was never afraid. She was making angry sounds as she ran. She had heard the cry of her young one and was rushing to save him.

She jumped in among them. Her worried and fighting role as a mother made her anything but a pretty sight. But to the young wolf, the sight of her protective anger was pleasing. He let out a glad little cry and jumped to meet her, while the man-animals went back quickly several steps. The she-wolf stood over against her young one, facing the men. Her hair stood up, and an angry sound rolled deep in her throat. Her face was twisted and full of threat. Even the bridge of her nose wrinkled from tip to eyes, so huge was her angry sound.

Then a cry went up from one of the men. "Kiche!" was what he said. It was a cry of surprise. The young wolf felt his mother weakening at the sound.

"Kiche!" the man cried again, this time with force and authority.

And then the young wolf saw his mother, the she-wolf, the one without fear. She was lowering herself down until her belly touched the ground. She was making soft crying sounds, moving her tail from side to side, and making peace signs. The young wolf could not understand. He was shocked. The fear and wonder of man rushed over him again. His instinct had been true. His mother proved it. She, too, gave way to the man-animals.

The man who had spoken came over to her. He put his hand on her head, and she only lowered herself closer. She did not snap, nor threaten to snap. The other men came up, and surrounded her, and felt her, and touched her with their hands. She made no attempt to fight these actions. They were very excited, and made many noises with their mouths. These noises were not a sign of danger, the young wolf decided. He lowered himself near his mother, still standing his hair up from time to time but doing his best to yield.

"It is not strange," an Indian was saying. "Her father was a wolf. It is true, her mother was a dog. But did not my brother tie her out in the woods all of three nights in the mating season? Therefore was the father of Kiche a wolf."

"It is a year, Grey Beaver, since she ran away," spoke a second Indian.

"It is not strange, Salmon Tongue," Grey Beaver answered. "It was the time of the great hunger, and there was no meat for the dogs."

"She has lived with the wolves," said a third Indian.

"So it would seem, Three Eagles," Grey Beaver answered, laying his hand on the young wolf; "and this be the sign of it."

The young wolf made a small angry sound when the hand touched him. The hand flew back to hit him. Then the young wolf showed his teeth and lay down quietly. The hand came back and rubbed behind his ears and up and down his back.

“This is the sign of it,” Grey Beaver went on. “It is clear that his mother is Kiche. But his father was a wolf. That is why he has a little dog and much wolf in him. His teeth are white, and White Fang shall be his name. I have spoken. He is my dog. Was not Kiche my brother’s dog? And is not my brother dead?”

The young wolf, who had now received a name in the world, lay and watched. For a time the man-animals kept making their mouth-noises. Then Grey Beaver took a knife from a cover that hung around his neck. He went into the bushes and cut a stick. White Fang watched him. He cut small cuts at each end of the stick and tied strings of raw-hide in the cuts. He tied one string around the throat of Kiche. Then he led her to a small pine tree and tied the other string around it.

White Fang followed and lay down beside her. Salmon Tongue’s hand reached out to him and rolled him over on his back. Kiche looked on with worry. White Fang felt fear growing in him again. He could not fully stop an angry sound, but he did not try to bite. The hand, with fingers bent and spread apart, rubbed his stomach in a fun way and rolled him from side to side. It was silly and awkward, lying there on his back with legs spread out in the air. Besides, it was a position of such complete helplessness that White Fang’s whole nature fought against it. He could do nothing to defend himself. If this man-animal meant harm, White Fang knew that he could not escape it. How could he jump away with his four legs in the air above him? Yet giving in made him control his fear, and he only made a soft angry sound. He could not stop this sound. Nor did the man-animal mind it by giving him a hit on the head. And further, such was the strangeness of it, White Fang felt a strange feeling of pleasure as the hand rubbed back and forth. When he was rolled on his side he stopped making the sound. When the fingers pressed and pushed at the base of his ears the pleasant feeling grew. And when, with a final rub and scratch, the man left him alone and went away, all fear had died out of White Fang. He was to know fear many times in his dealing with man. Yet it was a sign of the fearless friendship with man that was in the end to be his.

After a time, White Fang heard strange noises coming near. He was quick to sort them out, for he knew them at once as man-animal noises. A few minutes later the rest of the tribe came in, spread out as they were on the march. There were more men and many women and children, forty of them in all, and all heavily loaded with camp gear and supplies. Also there were many dogs. And these, except the half-grown young dogs, were also loaded with camp supplies. On their backs, in bags tied tightly around underneath, the dogs carried from twenty to thirty pounds of weight.

White Fang had never seen dogs before, but at the sight of them he felt that they were his own kind, only somehow different. But they showed little difference from the wolf when they found the young wolf and his mother. There was a rush. White Fang raised his hair and made angry sounds and bit at the face of the open-mouthed wave of dogs coming at him. He went down and under them, feeling the sharp cut of teeth in his body. He himself bit and tore at the legs and bellies above him. There was a great noise. He could hear the angry sounds of Kiche as she fought for him. And he could hear the cries of the man-animals, the sound of clubs hitting bodies, and the cries of pain from the dogs so hit.

Only a few seconds passed before he was on his feet again. He could now see the man-animals driving back the dogs with clubs and stones. They were defending him, saving him from the wild teeth of his kind that somehow was not his kind. And though there was no reason in his brain for a clear idea of so abstract a thing as justice, nevertheless, in his own way, he felt the justice of the man-animals. He knew them for what they were—makers of law and enforcers of law. Also, he valued the power with which they enforced the law. Unlike any animals he had ever met, they did not bite or scratch. They enforced their living strength with the power of dead things. Dead things did their bidding. So sticks and stones, directed by these strange creatures, jumped through the air like living things, causing bad hurts upon the dogs.

To his mind this was power unusual, power beyond imagining and beyond the natural, power that was like a god. White Fang, in the very nature of him, could never know anything about gods. At the best he could know only things that were beyond knowing. But the wonder and fear that he had of these man-animals in some ways looked like what would be the wonder and fear of man at the sight of some heavenly creature on a mountain top, throwing lightning bolts from either hand at an astonished world.

The last dog had been driven back. The noise died down. And White Fang licked his hurts and thought about this, his first taste of pack-cruelty and his introduction to the pack. He had never dreamed that his own kind was more than One Eye, his mother, and himself. They had made a kind apart. And here, suddenly, he had found many more creatures apparently of his own kind. And there was a hidden anger that these, his kind, at first sight had set upon him and tried to destroy him. In the same way he was angry that his mother was tied with a stick, even though it was done by the superior man-animals. It had the taste of the trap, of being tied. Yet of the trap and of being tied he knew nothing. Freedom to roam and run and lie down at will had been his birthright. And here it was being broken in upon. His mother’s movements were limited to the length of a stick, and by the length of that same stick was he limited, for he had not yet got beyond the need of his mother’s side.

He did not like it. He also did not like it when the man-animals got up and went on with their march. For a tiny man-animal took the other end of the stick and led Kiche behind him as a captive. And behind Kiche followed White Fang. He was greatly troubled and worried by this new adventure he had started.

They went down the valley of the stream, far beyond the farthest place White Fang had ever gone. At last they came to the end of the valley, where the stream ran into the Mackenzie River. Here, canoes were stored on poles high in the air. Fish-racks stood there for drying fish. They made camp here, and White Fang looked on with wondering eyes. The power of these man-animals grew with every moment. They had control over all these sharp-toothed dogs. It spoke of power. But greater than that, to the wolf cub, was their control over things not alive. They could give movement to things that did not move. They could change the very face of the world.

It was this last thing that especially affected him. The height of frames of poles caught his eye. Yet this in itself was not so remarkable. It was done by the same creatures that threw sticks and stones to great distances. But then the frames of poles were covered with cloth and skins and made into tepees. White Fang was amazed. It was the huge size of them that impressed him. They rose around him, on every side, like some strange quick-growing form of life. They filled nearly the whole circle of his field of vision. He was afraid of them. They stood above him in a threatening way. When the breeze stirred them into huge movements, he crouched down in fear. He kept his eyes carefully upon them. He was ready to jump away if they tried to throw themselves upon him.

But in a short while his fear of the tepees passed away. He saw the women and children passing in and out of them without harm. He saw the dogs often trying to get into them. They were driven away with sharp words and flying stones. After a time, he left Kiche’s side. He crawled carefully toward the wall of the nearest tepee. It was the curiosity of growth that urged him on. It was the need to learn and live and do that brings experience. The last few inches to the wall of the tepee were crawled with painful slowness and care. The day’s events had prepared him for the unknown to show itself in the most amazing and unthinkable ways. At last his nose touched the canvas. He waited. Nothing happened. Then he smelled the strange fabric, full of the man-smell. He closed on the canvas with his teeth and gave a gentle pull. Nothing happened, though the nearby parts of the tepee moved. He pulled harder. There was a greater movement. It was delightful. He pulled still harder, and again and again, until the whole tepee was in motion. Then the sharp cry of a woman inside sent him running back to Kiche. But after that he was no longer afraid of the looming bulk of the tepees.

A moment later he was wandering away again from his mother. Her stick was tied to a peg in the ground and she could not follow him. A part-grown puppy, somewhat larger and older than he, came toward him slowly. He came with a showy and aggressive importance. The puppy’s name, as White Fang was afterward to hear him called, was Lip-lip. He had had experience in puppy fights and was already something of a bully.

Lip-lip was White Fang’s own kind. Being only a puppy, he did not seem dangerous. So White Fang prepared to meet him in a friendly spirit. But the stranger’s walk became stiff-legged and his lips lifted clear of his teeth. White Fang stiffened too, and answered with lifted lips. They half circled about each other, testing, snarling and bristling. This lasted several minutes. White Fang was beginning to enjoy it, as a sort of game. But suddenly, with remarkable swiftness, Lip-lip leaped in. He delivered a slashing snap, and leaped away again. The snap had taken effect on the shoulder that had been hurt by the lynx. It was still sore deep down near the bone. The surprise and hurt of it brought a yelp out of White Fang. But the next moment, in a rush of anger, he was upon Lip-lip and snapping viciously.

But Lip-lip had lived his life in camp and had fought many puppy fights. Three times, four times, and half a dozen times, his sharp little teeth scored on the newcomer. At last White Fang, yelping shamelessly, fled to the protection of his mother. It was the first of the many fights he was to have with Lip-lip. They were enemies from the start, born so, with natures destined always to clash.

Kiche licked White Fang soothingly with her tongue. She tried to persuade him to remain with her. But his curiosity was rampant. Several minutes later he was venturing forth on a new quest. He came upon one of the man-animals, Grey Beaver. Grey Beaver was squatting on his heels. He was doing something with sticks and dry moss spread before him on the ground. White Fang came near to him and watched. Grey Beaver made mouth-noises which White Fang interpreted as not hostile. So he came still nearer.

Women and children were carrying more sticks and branches to Grey Beaver. It was evidently an important affair. White Fang came in until he touched Grey Beaver’s knee. He was so curious, and already forgetful that this was a terrible man-animal. Suddenly he saw a strange thing like mist beginning to arise from the sticks and moss beneath Grey Beaver’s hands. Then, amongst the sticks themselves, appeared a live thing, twisting and turning. It had a colour like the colour of the sun in the sky. White Fang knew nothing about fire. It drew him as the light in the mouth of the cave had drawn him in his early puppyhood. He crawled the several steps toward the flame. He heard Grey Beaver chuckle above him, and he knew the sound was not hostile. Then his nose touched the flame, and at the same instant his little tongue went out to it.

For a moment he was paralysed. The unknown, hiding in the midst of the sticks and moss, was savagely holding him by the nose. He scrambled backward, bursting out in an astonished explosion of ki-yi’s. At the sound, Kiche leaped snarling to the end of her stick. There she raged terribly because she could not come to his aid. But Grey Beaver laughed loudly, and slapped his thighs. He told the happening to all the rest of the camp, till everybody was laughing loudly. But White Fang sat on his haunches and ki-yi’d and ki-yi’d. He was a lonely and pitiable little figure in the midst of the man-animals.

It was the worst hurt he had ever known. Both his nose and his tongue had been burned by the live thing, the sun-colored thing that had grown up under Grey Beaver's hands. He cried and cried without end. Every new cry was met with bursts of laughter from the man-animals. He tried to comfort his nose with his tongue, but the tongue was burned too. The two hurts coming together made a greater hurt. So he cried more without hope and without help than ever.

And then shame came to him. He knew laughter and what it meant. We do not know how some animals understand laughter, or know when they are being laughed at. But White Fang knew it in this same way. He felt shame that the man-animals were laughing at him. He turned and ran away. He did not run from the hurt of the fire. He ran from the laughter. It went deeper and hurt his spirit. And he ran to Kiche. She was raging at the end of her stick like a crazy animal. He ran to Kiche, the one creature in the world who was not laughing at him.

Twilight came down and night came on. White Fang lay by his mother's side. His nose and tongue still hurt. But a bigger trouble confused him. He was homesick. He felt an empty place in him. He needed the quiet of the stream and the cave in the cliff. Life had become too crowded. There were so many man-animals: men, women, and children, all making noises and bothering him. And there were the dogs. They were always fighting and arguing. They burst into loud noises and made confusion. The peaceful aloneness of the only life he had known was gone. Here even the air was alive and beating. It made a steady sound without stopping. It kept changing its strength and suddenly changed its pitch. It pushed on his nerves and senses. It made him nervous and unable to rest. It worried him with a constant feeling that something was about to happen.

He watched the man-animals coming and going and moving around the camp. White Fang looked at the man-animals before him in a way that was a little like how men look at the gods they create. They were greater creatures, truly gods. To his unclear understanding, they were as much wonder-workers as gods are to men. They were creatures of power. They had all kinds of unknown and impossible powers. They were masters of the living and the not living. They made moving things obey. They gave movement to things that did not move. They made life grow out of dead moss and wood, life bright like the sun and sharp. They were fire-makers! They were gods.

CHAPTER 2. THE SHE-WOLF

THE SHE-WOLF

Breakfast was eaten and the small camp equipment was tied to the sled. The men turned their backs on the bright fire and went out into the darkness. Right away, sad and fierce cries began to rise. The cries called through the darkness and cold to one another and answered back. Conversation stopped. Daylight came at nine. At noon the sky to the south warmed to a rose color. It marked where the round earth came between the noon sun and the northern world. But the rose color faded quickly. The gray light of day that remained lasted until three. Then it faded too, and the dark cover of the Arctic night fell upon the lonely and silent land.

As darkness came on, the hunting cries to the right, left and rear drew closer. They came so close that more than once they sent waves of fear through the working dogs. This threw the dogs into short panics.

At the end of one such panic, when he and Henry had got the dogs back in the traces, Bill said:

"I wish they would strike game somewhere, and go away and leave us alone."

"They do get on the nerves horrible," Henry said in sympathy.

They spoke no more until camp was made.

Henry was bending over and adding ice to the bubbling pot of beans. Then he was startled by the sound of a blow, a cry from Bill, and a sharp angry cry of pain from among the dogs. He stood up in time to see a dim form disappearing across the snow into the shelter of the dark. Then he saw Bill, standing among the dogs, half victorious, half let down. In one hand Bill held a thick club. In the other he held the tail and part of the body of a sun-dried salmon.

"It got half of it," he announced. "But I got a hit at it just the same. Did you hear it cry out?"

"What did it look like?" Henry asked.

"Couldn't see. But it had four legs and a mouth and hair and looked like any dog."

"Must be a gentle wolf, I think."

"It's damned gentle, whatever it is, coming in here at feeding time and getting its share of fish."

That night, when supper was finished, they sat on the long box and pulled at their pipes. The circle of shining eyes drew in even closer than before.

"I wish they would jump up a bunch of moose or something, and go away and leave us alone," Bill said.

Henry made a low sound that was not all sympathy. For a quarter of an hour they sat on in silence. Henry stared at the fire. Bill stared at the circle of eyes that burned in the darkness just beyond the firelight.

"I wish we were pulling into McGurry right now," he began again.

"Shut up your wishing and your complaining," Henry burst out in anger. "Your stomach is sour. That is what is wrong with you. Swallow a spoonful of soda, and you will feel wonderful and be more pleasant company."

In the morning Henry was woken by hot curses coming from the mouth of Bill. Henry propped himself up on an elbow and looked. His friend stood among the dogs beside the refilled fire. His arms were raised in anger, and his face was twisted with passion.

"Hello!" Henry called. "What is up now?"

"Frog is gone," came the answer.

"No."

"I tell you yes."

Henry jumped out of the blankets and went to the dogs. He counted them with care. Then he joined his partner in cursing the power of the Wild that had robbed them of another dog.

"Frog was the strongest dog of the bunch," Bill said finally.

"And he was no fool dog either," Henry added.

And so was recorded the second epitaph in two days.

A sad breakfast was eaten, and the four remaining dogs were tied to the sled. The day was a repeat of the days that had gone before. The men worked without speech across the face of the frozen world. The silence was complete except by the cries of their trackers. The trackers were not seen and hung upon their rear. Night came in the middle of the afternoon. The cries sounded closer as the trackers drew in as they always did. The dogs grew excited and frightened. They were guilty of panics that mixed up the traces and further made the two men feel low.

"There, that will fix you fool animals," Bill said with satisfaction that night. He stood straight up at the completion of his task.

Henry left the cooking to come and see. His partner had not only tied the dogs up. He had tied them, in the Indian way, with sticks. About the neck of each dog he had tied a leather strip. To this, and so close to the neck that the dog could not get his teeth to it, he had tied a thick stick four or five feet in length. The other end of the stick was then tied to a stake in the ground with a leather strip. The dog was unable to bite through the leather at his own end of the stick. The stick prevented him from getting at the leather that tied the other end.

Henry nodded his head in approval.

"It is the only device that will ever hold One Ear," he said. "He can bite through leather as clean as a knife and just about half as quick. They all will be here in the morning, safe and sound."

"You just bet they will," Bill affirmed. "If one of them turns up missing, I will go without my coffee."

"They just know we are not loaded to kill," Henry remarked at bedtime. He pointed at the shining circle that closed them in. "If we could put a couple of shots into them, they would be more respectful. They come closer every night. Get the firelight out of your eyes and look hard—there! Did you see that one?"

For some time the two men enjoyed themselves by watching the movement of vague forms on the edge of the firelight. They looked closely and steadily at where a pair of eyes burned in the darkness. Then the form of the animal would slowly take shape. They could even see these forms move at times.

A sound among the dogs caught the men's attention. One Ear was making quick, eager whines. He rushed at the full length of his stick toward the darkness. Now and again he stopped in order to make wild attacks on the stick with his teeth.

"Look at that, Bill," Henry whispered.

A doglike animal slid full into the firelight with a quiet, sideways movement. It moved with mixed mistrust and daring. It carefully watched the men, and its attention was fixed on the dogs. One Ear strained the full length of the stick toward the intruder and whined with eagerness.

"That fool One Ear does not seem scared much," Bill said in a low tone.

"It is a female wolf," Henry whispered back. "And that explains Fatty and Frog. She brings the dog out for the pack. She draws out the dog, and then all the rest jump in and eat him up."

The fire made small cracking sounds. A log fell apart with a loud noise. At the sound, the strange animal jumped back into the darkness.

"Henry, I'm thinking," Bill said.

"Thinking what?"

"I'm thinking that was the one I hit with the club."

"There is no doubt in the world," Henry answered.

"And right here I want to say," Bill went on, "that this animal is too familiar with campfires. It is suspicious and wrong."

"It knows more than a proud wolf should know," Henry agreed. "A wolf that knows enough to come in with the dogs at feeding time has had experiences."

"Old Villan had a dog once that ran away with the wolves," Bill said aloud. "I should know. I shot it out of the pack in a moose field over on Little Stick. And Old Villan cried like a baby. He had not seen it for three years, he said. It had been with the wolves all that time."

"I think you have called it right, Bill. That wolf is a dog, and it has eaten fish many times from the hand of man."

"And if I get a chance at it, that wolf that is a dog will just be meat," Bill declared. "We cannot afford to lose any more animals."

"But you have only got three cartridges," Henry objected.

"I will wait for a dead sure shot," was the reply.

In the morning Henry lit the fire again and cooked breakfast while his partner snored.

"You were sleeping just too comfortable for anything," Henry told him, as he woke him for breakfast. "I did not have the heart to wake you."

Bill began to eat sleepily. He noticed that his cup was empty and started to reach for the pot. But the pot was beyond arm's length and beside Henry.

"Say, Henry," he said gently, "have you forgotten something?"

Henry looked about with great care and shook his head. Bill held up the empty cup.

"You do not get any coffee," Henry announced.

"Have we run out?" Bill asked anxiously.

"No."

"Do you think it will hurt my digestion?"

"No."

A flush of angry blood spread over Bill's face.

"Then I am warm and anxious to hear you explain yourself," he said.

"Spanker is gone," Henry answered.

Without haste, with the air of one who accepts bad luck, Bill turned his head. From where he sat, he counted the dogs.

"How did it happen?" he asked without feeling.

Henry shrugged his shoulders. "I do not know. Unless One Ear gnawed him loose. He could not have done it himself, that is sure."

"The darned curse." Bill spoke gravely and slowly, with no hint of the anger that was raging within. "Just because he could not chew himself loose, he chews Spanker loose."

"Well, Spanker's troubles are over anyway. I guess he is digested by this time and running over the land in the bellies of twenty different wolves," was Henry's epitaph on this, the latest lost dog. "Have some coffee, Bill."

But Bill shook his head.

"Go on," Henry pleaded, lifting the pot.

Bill pushed his cup aside. "I will be ding-dong-danged if I do. I said I would not if any dog turned up missing, and I will not."

"It is darn good coffee," Henry said enticingly.

But Bill was stubborn. He ate a dry breakfast and washed it down with mumbled curses at One Ear for the trick he had played.

"I will tie them up out of reach of each other tonight," Bill said, as they took the trail.

They had travelled little more than a hundred yards when Henry, who was in front, bent down and picked up something his snowshoe had hit. It was dark, and he could not see it, but he recognised it by the touch. He threw it back, so that it struck the sled and bounced along until it stopped on Bill's snowshoes.

"Maybe you will need that in your business," Henry said.

Bill uttered an exclamation. It was all that was left of Spanker—the stick with which he had been tied.

"They ate his hide and all," Bill announced. "The stick is as clean as a whistle. They have eaten the leather off both ends. They are damn hungry, Henry, and they will have you and me guessing before this trip is over."

Henry laughed defiantly. "I have not been trailed this way by wolves before, but I have gone through a whole lot worse and kept my health. It takes more than a handful of these pesky creatures to do for yours truly, Bill, my son."

"I do not know, I do not know," Bill muttered ominously.

"Well, you will know all right when we pull into McGurry."

"I am not feeling especially enthusiastic," Bill persisted.

"You are off colour, that is what is the matter with you," Henry said with certainty. "What you need is quinine, and I am going to dose you up stiff as soon as we make McGurry."

Bill grunted his disagreement with the diagnosis, and fell into silence. The day was like all the days. Light came at nine o'clock. At twelve o'clock the southern horizon was warmed by the unseen sun. Then began the cold grey of afternoon that would merge, three hours later, into night.

It was just after the sun's useless effort to appear that Bill slipped the rifle from under the sled ropes and said:

"You keep right on, Henry, I am going to see what I can see."

"You had better stick by the sled," his partner protested. "You have only got three cartridges, and there is no telling what might happen."

"Who is croaking now?" Bill demanded triumphantly.

Henry made no reply, and plodded on alone, though often he cast anxious glances back into the grey solitude where his partner had disappeared. An hour later, taking advantage of the short cuts around which the sled had to go, Bill arrived.

"They are scattered and ranging along wide," he said. "They are keeping up with us and looking for game at the same time. You see, they are sure of us, only they know they have to wait to get us. In the meantime they are willing to pick up anything eatable that comes handy."

"You mean they think they are sure of us," Henry objected pointedly.

But Bill ignored him. "I saw some of them. They are pretty thin. They have not had a bite in weeks I think, outside of Fatty and Frog and Spanker. And there are so many of them that that did not go far. They are remarkably thin. Their ribs are like wash-boards, and their stomachs are right up against their backbones. They are pretty desperate, I can tell you. They will be going mad yet, and then watch out."

A few minutes later, Henry was now travelling behind the sled. He let out a low sound to warn Bill. Bill turned and looked, then quietly stopped the dogs. Behind them, from around the last bend and clearly in view, on the very trail they had just covered, a furry, moving shape came running slowly. Its nose was to the trail. It ran with a weird, sliding, easy way of walking. When they stopped, it stopped. It threw up its head and looked at them steadily. Its nose moved as it caught and studied their smell.

“It is the she-wolf,” Bill answered.

The dogs had lain down in the snow. He walked past them to join his partner at the sled. Together they watched the strange animal. It had followed them for days. It had already killed half their dog-team.

After a careful look, the animal ran forward a few steps. It did this several times, until it was only a short hundred yards away. It stopped with its head up, near a group of spruce trees. It used its eyes and nose to study the men and their sled. It looked at them in a strangely sad way, like a dog. But its sadness had none of a dog's love. It was a sadness born of hunger. It was as cruel as its own teeth. It was as pitiless as the frost itself.

It was large for a wolf. Its thin body showed it was one of the largest of its kind.

"It stands close to two and a half feet at the shoulders," Henry said. "And I bet it is not far from five feet long."

"Strange color for a wolf," Bill said. "I never saw a red wolf before. It looks almost cinnamon to me."

The animal was certainly not cinnamon-colored. Its coat was the true wolf-coat. The main color was gray, yet it had a faint reddish tint. The tint was puzzling. It appeared and disappeared. It was more like something in the eye. Now it was gray, clearly gray. Then it gave hints and flashes of a vague red color. That red could not be sorted into any normal kind of color.

"It looks just like a big husky sled-dog," Bill said. "I would not be surprised to see it wag its tail."

"Hello, you husky!" he called. "Come here, you whatever-your-name-is."

"It is not a bit scared of you," Henry laughed.

Bill waved his hand at it in a threatening way and shouted loudly. But the animal showed no fear. The only change they could see was that it became more alert. It still watched them with the pitiless sadness of hunger. They were meat, and it was hungry. It would like to go in and eat them if it dared.

"Look here, Henry," Bill said. He lowered his voice to a whisper without thinking, because of what he was copying. "We have three bullets. But it is a dead shot. I could not miss it. It has gotten away with three of our dogs, and we ought to stop it. What do you say?"

Henry nodded his consent. Bill carefully slipped the gun from under the sled rope. The gun was on its way to his shoulder, but it never got there. For in that instant the female wolf jumped sideways from the trail into the group of spruce trees and disappeared.

The two men looked at each other. Henry whistled long and with understanding.

"I might have known it," Bill scolded himself aloud as he put the gun back. "Of course a wolf that knows enough to come in with the dogs at feeding time would know all about guns. I tell you right now, Henry, that creature is the cause of all our trouble. We would have six dogs now, instead of three, if it were not for her. And I tell you right now, Henry, I am going to get her. She is too smart to be shot in the open. But I am going to wait for her. I will ambush her as sure as my name is Bill."

"You need not stray off too far in doing it," his partner warned. "If that pack ever starts to jump you, those three bullets would be worth no more than three whoops in hell. Those animals are damn hungry, and once they start in, they will sure get you, Bill."

They camped early that night. Three dogs could not drag the sled so fast or for so long hours as six could. And the dogs were showing clear signs of being worn out. The men went to bed early. Bill first made sure the dogs were tied out of biting reach of one another.

But the wolves were growing bolder. The men were woken more than once from their sleep. The wolves came so near that the dogs became wild with terror. It was necessary to add wood to the fire from time to time to keep the bold raiders at a safer distance.

"I have heard sailors talk of sharks following a ship," Bill said. He crawled back into the blankets after one such adding of wood to the fire. "Well, those wolves are land sharks. They know their business better than we do. And they are not holding our trail this way for their health. They are going to get us. They are sure going to get us, Henry."

"They have half got you already, talking like that," Henry answered sharply. "A man is half beaten when he says he is. And you are half eaten from the way you are going on about it."

"They have gotten away with better men than you and me," Bill answered.

"Oh, shut up your croaking. You make me all-fired tired."

Henry rolled over angrily on his side. But he was surprised that Bill made no similar show of temper. This was not Bill's way, for he was easily angered by sharp words. Henry thought long over it before he went to sleep. As his eyelids fluttered down and he dozed off, the thought in his mind was: "There is no mistaking it, Bill is almighty blue. I will have to cheer him up tomorrow."

CHAPTER 3. THE HUNGER CRY

THE HUNGER CRY

The day began well. They had lost no dogs during the night. They moved out onto the trail and into the silence, the darkness, and the cold with fairly light hearts. Bill seemed to have forgotten his bad feelings of the night before. He even became funny with the dogs when, at noon, they turned the sled over on a bad piece of trail.

It was an awkward mess. The sled was upside down and stuck between a tree-trunk and a huge rock. They had to take the dogs out of their harnesses to straighten out the tangle. The two men were bent over the sled and trying to turn it right, when Henry saw One Ear move away.

“Here, you, One Ear!” he cried. He straightened up and turned around toward the dog.

But One Ear began to run across the snow, his ropes trailing behind him. And there, out in the snow of their back track, was the she-wolf waiting for him. As he came near her, he became suddenly careful. He slowed down to a watchful, careful walk and then stopped. He looked at her carefully and with doubt, yet with desire. She seemed to smile at him, showing her teeth in a friendly way rather than a threatening way. She moved toward him a few steps, in a playful way, and then stopped. One Ear came near to her, still watchful and careful, his tail and ears up, his head held high.

He tried to touch noses with her, but she moved back in a playful, shy way. Every step he took forward was met by a matching step back from her. Step by step she was leading him away from the safety of his human companions. Once, as though a warning had passed in a vague way through his mind, he turned his head and looked back at the overturned sled, at his team-mates, and at the two men who were calling to him.

But whatever idea was forming in his mind was driven away by the she-wolf. She came toward him, touched noses with him for a fleeting moment, and then began her shy retreat again before his new advances.

In the meantime, Bill had thought of the rifle. But it was stuck beneath the overturned sled. By the time Henry had helped him to turn the load right, One Ear and the she-wolf were too close together and the distance was too great to risk a shot.

Too late One Ear learned his mistake. Before they saw the cause, the two men saw him turn and start to run back toward them. Then they saw a dozen wolves, lean and grey, running across the snow at right angles to the trail and cutting off his retreat. On the instant, the she-wolf’s shyness and playfulness disappeared. With a snarl she sprang upon One Ear. He pushed her off with his shoulder. His retreat was cut off, and he still wanted to get back to the sled, so he changed his course and tried to circle around to it. More wolves were appearing every moment and joining in the chase. The she-wolf was one leap behind One Ear and holding her own.

“Where are you going?” Henry suddenly demanded, laying his hand on his partner’s arm.

Bill shook it off. “I won’t stand it,” he said. “They are not going to get any more of our dogs if I can help it.”

Gun in hand, he plunged into the low bushes that lined the side of the trail. His intention was clear enough. Taking the sled as the center of the circle that One Ear was making, Bill planned to reach that circle at a point ahead of the chase. With his rifle, in the broad daylight, it might be possible for him to frighten the wolves and save the dog.

“Say, Bill!” Henry called after him. “Be careful! Don’t take any chances!”

Henry sat down on the sled and watched. There was nothing else for him to do. Bill had already gone from sight. But now and again, appearing and disappearing among the low bushes and the scattered groups of spruce trees, One Ear could be seen. Henry judged his case to be hopeless. The dog was fully aware of its danger, but it was running on the outer circle while the wolf-pack was running on the inner and shorter circle. It was useless to think that One Ear could get so far ahead of his pursuers that he could cut across their circle in front of them and get back to the sled.

The different lines were quickly approaching a point. Somewhere out there in the snow, hidden from his sight by trees and thickets, Henry knew that the wolf-pack, One Ear, and Bill were coming together. All too quickly, far more quickly than he had expected, it happened. He heard a shot, then two shots, in rapid succession, and he knew that Bill’s ammunition was gone. Then he heard a great outcry of snarls and yelps. He recognized One Ear’s yell of pain and terror, and he heard a wolf-cry that showed a stricken animal. And that was all. The snarls stopped. The yelping died away. Silence settled down again over the lonely land.

He sat for a long while upon the sled. There was no need for him to go and see what had happened. He knew it as though it had taken place before his eyes. Once, he roused with a start and hastily got the axe out from underneath the lashings. But for some time longer he sat and brooded, the two remaining dogs crouching and trembling at his feet.

At last he arose in a weary manner, as though all the strength had gone out of his body, and began to fasten the dogs to the sled. He passed a rope over his shoulder, a man-trace, and pulled with the dogs. He did not go far. At the first hint of darkness he hurried to make a camp, and he made sure that he had a generous supply of firewood. He fed the dogs, cooked and ate his supper, and made his bed close to the fire.

But he was not meant to enjoy that bed. Before his eyes closed the wolves had drawn too near for safety. It no longer required an effort of the eyes to see them. They were all about him and the fire, in a narrow circle. He could see them plainly in the firelight lying down, sitting up, crawling forward on their bellies, or moving back and forth. They even slept. Here and there he could see one curled up in the snow like a dog, taking the sleep that was now denied to himself.

He kept the fire burning bright. He knew the fire alone stood between his body and their hungry teeth. His two dogs stayed close to him, one on each side. They leaned against him for protection. They cried and made soft sounds of fear. At times they showed their teeth and made low, angry sounds when a wolf came a little closer than usual. At such moments, when his dogs made those sounds, the whole circle became unable to stay still. The wolves stood up and moved slowly forward. A chorus of low sounds and eager short cries rose around him. Then the circle would lie down again. Here and there a wolf would go back to its broken sleep.

But this circle kept trying to close in on him. Bit by bit, a little at a time, with one wolf crawling forward here and another wolf crawling forward there, the circle would narrow. Soon the animals were almost close enough to jump. Then he would grab burning sticks from the fire and throw them into the pack. A quick move back always followed. Angry short cries and frightened low sounds came when a well-aimed stick hit and burned a too bold animal.

Morning found the man tired and worn, with wide eyes from lack of sleep. He cooked breakfast in the darkness. At nine, when the daylight came and the wolf-pack moved back, he began the task he had planned through the long hours of the night. He cut down young trees. He tied them high up to the trunks of standing trees to make cross-bars of a platform. Using the sled ropes as a pulling rope, and with the help of the dogs, he lifted the coffin to the top of the platform.

"They got Bill, and they may get me, but they will sure never get you, young man," he said, speaking to the dead body in its tree grave.

Then he took the trail. The lighter sled jumped along behind the willing dogs. They too knew that safety lay in reaching Fort McGurry. The wolves were now more open in their pursuit. They ran without fear behind and spread out along either side. Their red tongues hung out. Their lean sides showed their moving ribs with every movement. They were very lean, just skin bags stretched over thin frames, with strings for muscles. They were so lean that Henry found himself wondering that they still kept their feet and did not fall down at once in the snow.

He did not dare travel until dark. At noon, the sun not only warmed the southern horizon. It even pushed its upper rim, pale and golden, above the sky-line. He took it as a sign. The days were growing longer. The sun was returning. But hardly had the cheer of its light gone than he went into camp. There were still several hours of gray daylight and dark twilight. He used them to cut an enormous supply of fire-wood.

With night came horror. Not only were the starving wolves growing bolder, but lack of sleep was telling on Henry. He slept despite himself, bending low by the fire, the blankets about his shoulders, the axe between his knees, and on each side a dog pressing close against him. He woke once and saw in front of him, not twelve feet away, a big gray wolf, one of the largest of the pack. And even as he looked, the animal deliberately stretched itself like a tired dog. It yawned full in his face and looked at him with a wanting eye. It looked as if he were merely a delayed meal that was soon to be eaten.

This certainty was shown by the whole pack. He could count fully a score. They stared with hunger at him or slept without fear in the snow. They reminded him of children gathered about a spread table and waiting for permission to begin to eat. And he was the food they were to eat! He wondered how and when the meal would begin.

As he piled wood on the fire he discovered an appreciation of his own body which he had never felt before. He watched his moving muscles. He was interested in the clever working of his fingers. By the light of the fire he bent his fingers slowly and repeatedly, now one at a time, now all together. He spread them wide or made quick gripping movements. He studied the nail-formation. He pushed the finger-tips, now sharply, and again softly, measuring the while the nerve-feelings produced. It fascinated him. He grew suddenly fond of this subtle flesh of his that worked so beautifully and smoothly and delicately. Then he would cast a glance of fear at the wolf-circle drawn with expectation about him. Like a blow the understanding would strike him that this wonderful body of his, this living flesh, was no more than so much meat. It was a search of very hungry animals, to be torn and cut by their hungry teeth. It was to be food to them as the moose and the rabbit had often been food to him.

He came out of a sleep that was half nightmare. He saw the red-colored she-wolf before him. She was not more than twelve feet away, sitting in the snow and looking at him with longing. The two dogs were crying and growling at his feet, but she took no notice of them. She was looking at the man, and for some time he returned her look. There was nothing threatening about her. She looked at him merely with a great longing, but he knew it to be the longing of an equally great hunger. He was the food, and the sight of him excited in her the feelings of taste. Her mouth opened, the saliva drooled forth, and she licked her lips with the pleasure of anticipation.

A spasm of fear went through him. He reached quickly for a stick to throw at her. But even as he reached, and before his fingers had closed on the object, she sprang back into safety. He knew that she was used to having things thrown at her. She had growled as she sprang away, showing her white teeth to their roots. All her longing vanished. It was replaced by a meat-eating hate that made him shudder. He glanced at the hand that held the stick. He noticed the clever delicacy of the fingers that gripped it. He saw how they adjusted themselves to all the uneven parts of the surface, curling over and under and about the rough wood. One little finger, too close to the burning portion of the stick, moved back by itself from the hurtful heat to a cooler gripping-place. In the same instant he seemed to see a vision of those same sensitive and delicate fingers being crushed and torn by the white teeth of the she-wolf. Never had he been so fond of this body of his as now when his hold on it was so uncertain.

All night, he fought off the hungry pack with burning sticks. When he slept in spite of himself, the crying and angry sounds of the dogs woke him. Morning came, but for the first time, the light of day did not make the wolves go away. The man waited for them to leave, but they did not. They stayed in a circle around him and his fire. They showed a proud sense of ownership that shook the courage the morning light had given him.

He made one desperate attempt to get out onto the trail. But the moment he left the protection of the fire, the boldest wolf jumped at him, but jumped short. He saved himself by jumping back. The jaws snapped together only six inches from his thigh. The rest of the pack was now up and rushing at him. He had to throw burning sticks right and left to drive them back to a safe distance.

Even in the daylight he did not dare leave the fire to cut fresh wood. Twenty feet away stood a huge dead spruce tree. He spent half the day moving his campfire closer to the tree. At any moment he kept half a dozen burning sticks ready in his hand to throw at his enemies. Once at the tree, he studied the forest around it so he could cut the tree down in the direction of the most firewood.

The night was the same as the night before, except that the need for sleep was becoming too strong to fight. The angry sounds of his dogs were losing their effect. Besides, they were making angry sounds all the time, and his numb and sleepy senses no longer noticed changes in pitch and strength. He woke with a start. The she-wolf was less than a yard from him. Without thinking, at short range, without letting go of it, he pushed a burning stick full into her open, snarling mouth. She jumped away, crying with pain. He took delight in the smell of burning flesh and hair as he watched her shake her head and growl angrily twenty feet away.

But this time, before he slept again, he tied a burning pine-knot to his right hand. His eyes were closed for only a few minutes when the burn of the flame on his flesh woke him. For several hours he followed this plan. Every time he was woken this way, he drove back the wolves with flying burning sticks, added more wood to the fire, and tied the pine-knot on his hand again. All worked well, but there came a time when he tied the pine-knot loosely. As his eyes closed it fell away from his hand.

He dreamed. It seemed to him that he was in Fort McGurry. It was warm and comfortable, and he was playing cribbage with the Factor. Also, it seemed to him that the fort was surrounded by wolves. They were howling at the very gates, and sometimes he and the Factor paused from the game to listen and laugh at the useless efforts of the wolves to get in. And then, so strange was the dream, there was a crash. The door was burst open. He could see the wolves pouring into the big living-room of the fort. They were jumping straight for him and the Factor. With the bursting open of the door, the noise of their howling had increased greatly. This howling now bothered him. His dream was merging into something else—he knew not what; but through it all, following him, persisted the howling.

And then he woke to find the howling was real. There was a great snarling and yelping. The wolves were rushing at him. They were all around him and on him. The teeth of one had closed on his arm. Without thinking he jumped into the fire, and as he jumped, he felt the sharp cut of teeth that tore through the flesh of his leg. Then began a fire fight. His thick mittens temporarily protected his hands, and he threw live coals into the air in all directions, until the campfire looked like a volcano.

But it could not last long. His face was blistering in the heat, his eyebrows and lashes were singed off, and the heat was becoming unbearable to his feet. With a flaming stick in each hand, he jumped to the edge of the fire. The wolves had been driven back. On every side, wherever the live coals had fallen, the snow was sizzling. Every little while a retreating wolf, with a wild jump and snort and snarl, announced that one such live coal had been stepped on.

Throwing his burning sticks at the nearest of his enemies, the man pushed his smouldering mittens into the snow and stamped about to cool his feet. His two dogs were missing, and he well knew that they had served as a course in the long meal which had begun days before with Fatty. The last course of that meal would likely be himself in the days to follow.

“You ain’t got me yet!” he cried, angrily shaking his fist at the hungry beasts. At the sound of his voice the whole circle was agitated. There was a general snarl, and the she-wolf slid up close to him across the snow and watched him with hungry longing.

He set to work to carry out a new idea that had come to him. He extended the fire into a large circle. Inside this circle he crouched, with his sleeping outfit under him as a protection against the melting snow. When he had thus disappeared within his shelter of flame, the whole pack came curiously to the rim of the fire to see what had become of him. Until now they had been denied access to the fire, and they now settled down in a close circle, like so many dogs, blinking and yawning and stretching their lean bodies in the unaccustomed warmth. Then the she-wolf sat down, pointed her nose at a star, and began to howl. One by one the wolves joined her, until the whole pack, on their haunches, with noses pointed skyward, was howling its hunger cry.

Dawn came, and daylight. The fire was burning low. The fuel had run out, and there was need to get more. The man attempted to step out of his circle of flame, but the wolves rushed to meet him. Burning sticks made them jump aside, but they no longer jumped back. He tried in vain to drive them back. As he gave up and stumbled inside his circle, a wolf jumped for him, missed, and landed with all four feet in the coals. It cried out with terror, at the same time snarling, and scrambled back to cool its paws in the snow.

The man sat down on his blankets. He pulled his knees up close to his body. His body leaned forward from the hips. His shoulders were relaxed and hanging down. His head rested on his knees. This showed that he had given up the struggle. Now and then he raised his head to check the dying fire. The circle of flame and coals was breaking into pieces. There were gaps between the pieces. These gaps grew bigger. The pieces grew smaller.

“I guess you can come and get me any time,” he said in a low voice. “Anyway, I’m going to sleep.”

Once he woke up. In a gap in the circle, right in front of him, he saw the she-wolf looking at him.

Again he woke up, a little later. It seemed like hours to him. A strange change had taken place. It was so strange that it woke him up completely. Something had happened. At first he could not understand. Then he found out. The wolves were gone. Only the crushed snow was left. It showed how close they had pressed around him. Sleep was rising up and taking hold of him again. His head was sinking down upon his knees. Then he woke with a sudden jump.

There were cries of men. There was the heavy sound of sleds. There was the creaking of harnesses. There was the eager whining of pulling dogs. Four sleds came in from the river bed to the camp among the trees. Half a dozen men were around the man. He bent low in the middle of the dying fire. They were shaking him and pushing him to wake him up. He looked at them like a drunk man. He spoke in strange, sleepy words.

“Red she-wolf. . . . Came in with the dogs at feeding time. . . . First she ate the dog-food. . . . Then she ate the dogs. . . . And after that she ate Bill. . . . ”

“Where’s Lord Alfred?” one of the men shouted in his ear. He shook him roughly.

He shook his head slowly. “No, she didn’t eat him. . . . He’s resting in a tree at the last camp.”

“Dead?” the man shouted.

“And in a box,” Henry answered. He pulled his shoulder away from the man’s hold. His move was sharp and annoyed. “Say, you leave me alone. . . . I’m just completely worn out. . . . Good night, everybody.”

His eyes moved and went shut. His chin fell forward on his chest. They laid him down upon the blankets. Even then, his snores were rising on the cold air.

But there was another sound. It was far and faint in the distant distance. It was the cry of the hungry wolf-pack. It had taken the trail of other meat. It had just missed the man.

CHAPTER 4. THE WALL OF THE WORLD

THE WALL OF THE WORLD

By the time his mother began leaving the cave on hunting trips, the young wolf had learned well the law that stopped him from going near the entrance. His mother had pushed this law into him many times with her nose and foot. And also, the feeling of fear was growing inside him. In his short life in the cave, he had never met anything to be afraid of. Yet fear was in him. It had come down to him from far-away ancestors through a thousand thousand lives. It was a gift he had received directly from One Eye and the she-wolf. But they, in turn, had received it from all the generations of wolves that had come before. Fear!—that gift of the Wild which no animal may escape or trade for food.

So the young wolf knew fear, though he did not know what fear was made of. Perhaps he accepted it as one of the limits of life. For he had already learned that there were such limits. He had known hunger. And when he could not stop his hunger, he had felt a limit. The hard wall of the cave, the sharp push of his mother's nose, the heavy hit of her foot, the hunger he could not stop during several periods without food—all these had made him understand that not everything in the world was freedom. He learned that life had limits and controls. These limits and controls were laws. To obey them was to escape hurt and to find happiness.

He did not think the question out in this human way. He simply put things into two groups: things that hurt and things that did not hurt. And after this grouping, he avoided the things that hurt, the limits and controls, in order to enjoy the pleasures and rewards of life.

So it was that, obeying the law his mother had set, and obeying the law of that unknown and nameless thing, fear, he stayed away from the mouth of the cave. It remained to him a white wall of light. When his mother was away, he slept most of the time. During the times he was awake, he kept very quiet. He held back the small crying sounds that rose in his throat and tried to become noise.

Once, lying awake, he heard a strange sound in the white wall. He did not know it was a wolverine, standing outside, shaking with its own boldness, and carefully smelling out what was inside the cave. The young wolf knew only that the smell was strange. It was something he could not put in a group, and so it was unknown and terrible. For the unknown was one of the main parts that made up fear.

The hair rose on the young wolf's back, but it rose silently. How could he know that this thing that smelled was a thing to raise his hair at? It was not born of any knowledge of his. Yet it was the visible sign of the fear that was in him, and for which, in his own life, there was no reason. But fear was joined by another instinct—that of hiding. The young wolf was in a storm of terror. Yet he lay without movement or sound, frozen, turned to stone, to all appearances dead. His mother, coming home, made a low sound as she smelled the wolverine's track. She jumped into the cave and licked him and pushed him with her nose with too much force of love. And the young wolf felt that somehow he had escaped a great hurt.

But there were other forces at work in the young wolf, the greatest of which was growth. Instinct and law demanded that he obey. But growth demanded that he disobey. His mother and fear pushed him to keep away from the white wall. Growth is life, and life is forever meant to move toward light. So there was no stopping the tide of life that was rising within him—rising with every bite of meat he swallowed, with every breath he drew. In the end, one day, fear and obedience were swept away by the rush of life, and the young wolf walked with wide steps toward the entrance.

Unlike any other wall he had known, this wall seemed to move back from him as he came near. No hard surface hit the soft little nose he pushed out carefully before him. The stuff of the wall seemed as open and yielding as light. And as the condition, in his eyes, had the seeming of form, so he entered into what had been wall to him and bathed in the stuff that made it.

It was confusing. He was moving through solidity. And the light grew brighter. Fear urged him to go back, but growth drove him on. Suddenly he found himself at the mouth of the cave. The wall, inside which he had thought himself, suddenly jumped back before him to a distance he could not measure. The light had become painfully bright. He was blinded by it. He was also made dizzy by this sudden and huge stretching of space. Automatically, his eyes were adjusting themselves to the brightness. They were focusing themselves to meet the increased distance of objects. At first, the wall had jumped beyond his sight. He now saw it again. But it had taken on a remarkable farness. Also, its appearance had changed. It was now a wall of many colors, made of the trees that edged the stream, the mountain across from him that rose above the trees, and the sky that rose above the mountain.

A great fear came upon him. This was more of the terrible unknown. He bent down low on the edge of the cave and looked out on the world. He was very much afraid. Because it was unknown, it was hostile to him. Therefore the hair stood up along his back. His lips pulled back weakly in an attempt at a fierce and frightening snarl. Out of his smallness and fright he challenged and threatened the whole wide world.

Nothing happened. He continued to look, and in his interest he forgot to snarl. Also, he forgot to be afraid. For the time, fear had been beaten by growth, while growth had taken the form of curiosity. He began to notice near objects—an open part of the stream that flashed in the sun, the dead pine-tree that stood at the base of the slope, and the slope itself, that ran right up to him and stopped two feet beneath the edge of the cave on which he bent low.

Now the grey cub had lived all his days on a level floor. He had never felt the hurt of a fall. He did not know what a fall was. So he stepped boldly out upon the air. His back legs still rested on the cave-lip, so he fell forward head downward. The earth struck him a harsh blow on the nose that made him yelp. Then he began rolling down the slope, over and over. He was in a panic of terror. The unknown had caught him at last. It had gripped him hard and was about to do him some terrible hurt. Growth was now routed by fear, and he cried out like any frightened puppy.

The unknown carried him on, he knew not to what terrible hurt. He yelped and cried out without stopping. This was a different thing from crouching in frozen fear while the unknown waited just alongside. Now the unknown had caught tight hold of him. Silence would do no good. Besides, it was not fear, but terror, that shook his body.

But the slope grew more gentle, and its base was covered with grass. Here the cub lost speed. When at last he came to a stop, he gave one last painful yell and then a long, whimpering cry. Also, and quite as a matter of course, as though in his life he had already cleaned himself a thousand times, he began to lick away the dry clay that dirtied him.

After that he sat up and looked about him, as the first man of the earth might have done when he landed upon Mars. The cub had broken through the wall of the world. The unknown had let go its hold of him, and here he was without hurt. But the first man on Mars would have felt less strangeness than he did. Without any earlier knowledge, without any warning at all that such a thing existed, he found himself an explorer in a totally new world.

Now that the terrible unknown had let go of him, he forgot that the unknown had any terrors. He was aware only of curiosity in all the things about him. He inspected the grass beneath him, the moss-berry plant just beyond, and the dead trunk of the blasted pine that stood on the edge of an open space among the trees. A squirrel, running around the base of the trunk, came full upon him, and gave him a great fright. He crouched down and snarled. But the squirrel was as badly scared. It ran up the tree, and from a point of safety chattered back at him fiercely.

This helped the cub’s courage. The woodpecker he next met gave him a start, but he went on his way with confidence. Such was his confidence that when a moose-bird rudely hopped up to him, he reached out at it with a playful paw. The result was a sharp peck on the end of his nose that made him crouch down and cry out. The noise he made was too much for the moose-bird, who looked for safety in flight.

But the cub was learning. His misty little mind had already made an unconscious grouping. There were live things and things not alive. Also, he must watch out for the live things. The things not alive stayed always in one place, but the live things moved about, and there was no telling what they might do. The thing to expect of them was the unexpected, and for this he must be prepared.

He travelled very awkwardly. He ran into sticks and things. A twig that he thought a long way off would the next instant hit him on the nose or scrape along his ribs. There were uneven places on the surface. Sometimes he stepped too far and hit his nose. Quite as often he stepped too short and hit his feet. Then there were the pebbles and stones that turned under him when he trod upon them. From them he came to know that the things not alive were not all as steady as his cave. He also learned that small things not alive were more likely than large things to fall down or turn over. But with every mishap he was learning. The longer he walked, the better he walked. He was adjusting himself. He was learning to judge his own muscle movements, to know his physical limits, to measure distances between objects, and between objects and himself.

His was the luck of the beginner. Born to be a hunter of meat (though he did not know it), he came upon meat just outside his own cave-door on his first trip into the world. It was by sheer chance that he found the cleverly hidden ptarmigan nest. He fell into it. He had tried to walk along the trunk of a fallen pine. The rotten bark gave way under his feet. With a despairing yelp he pitched down the rounded curve, smashed through the leaves and stalks of a small bush, and in the heart of the bush, on the ground, came to rest in the midst of seven ptarmigan chicks.

They made noises, and at first he was frightened at them. Then he saw that they were very little, and he became bolder. They moved. He placed his paw on one, and its movements became faster. This was a source of enjoyment to him. He smelled it. He picked it up in his mouth. It struggled and tickled his tongue. At the same time he became aware of a feeling of hunger. His jaws closed together. There was a crunching of fragile bones, and warm blood ran in his mouth. The taste of it was good. This was meat, the same as his mother gave him, only it was alive between his teeth and therefore better. So he ate the ptarmigan. Nor did he stop till he had eaten the whole brood. Then he licked his lips in quite the same way his mother did, and began to crawl out of the bush.

He met a whirlwind of feathers. He was confused and blinded by the rush of it and the beat of angry wings. He hid his head between his paws and yelped. The blows increased. The mother ptarmigan was in a fury. Then he became angry. He rose up, snarling, striking out with his paws. He sank his tiny teeth into one of the wings and pulled and tugged with all his strength. The ptarmigan struggled against him, raining blows upon him with her free wing. It was his first battle. He was elated. He forgot all about the unknown. He no longer was afraid of anything. He was fighting, tearing at a live thing that was striking at him. Also, this live thing was meat. The wish to kill was on him. He had just destroyed little live things. He would now destroy a big live thing. He was too busy and happy to know that he was happy. He was thrilling and rejoicing in ways new to him and greater to him than any he had known before.

He held on to the wing and made a low, angry sound between his tight teeth. The ptarmigan pulled him out of the low plants. She turned and tried to pull him back into the shelter of the plants. He pulled her away from it and on into the open. All the time she was crying out and hitting with her free wing. Feathers were flying like snow. He was very excited. All the fighting blood of his kind was up in him and rushing through him. This was living, though he did not know it. He was learning his own meaning in the world. He was doing what he was made for—killing meat and fighting to kill it. He was proving he existed, and life can do no more than that. Life reaches its highest point when it does fully what it was built to do.

After a time, the ptarmigan stopped struggling. He still held her by the wing, and they lay on the ground and looked at each other. He tried to make a low, angry sound in a threatening, fierce way. She hit his nose with her beak, which was sore from earlier fights. He winced but held on. She hit him again and again. From wincing he went to making small crying sounds. He tried to back away from her. He did not notice that by holding her he pulled her after him. A rain of beak hits fell on his hurt nose. The flood of fight went down in him. He let go of his prey, turned tail and ran across the open in shameful retreat.

He lay down to rest on the other side of the open, near the edge of the low plants. His tongue hung out, his chest heaved and panted, his nose still hurt him and made him keep making small crying sounds. But as he lay there, suddenly a feeling came to him of something terrible coming. The unknown with all its terrors rushed upon him, and he shrank back by instinct into the shelter of the low plants. As he did so, a rush of air fanned him, and a large winged body swept silently and ominously past. A hawk, driving down out of the blue, had barely missed him.

While he lay in the low plants, recovering from his fright and peering out fearfully, the mother-ptarmigan on the other side of the open space fluttered out of the ruined nest. Because of her loss she paid no attention to the winged bolt of the sky. But the cub saw, and it was a warning and a lesson to him. He saw the swift downward swoop of the hawk, the short skim of its body just above the ground, the strike of its talons in the body of the ptarmigan, the ptarmigan’s squawk of agony and fright, and the hawk’s rush upward into the blue, carrying the ptarmigan away with it.

It was a long time before the cub left its shelter. He had learned much. Live things were meat. They were good to eat. Also, live things when they were large enough could give hurt. It was better to eat small live things like ptarmigan chicks, and to leave alone large live things like ptarmigan hens. Nevertheless he felt a little prick of ambition, a secret desire to have another battle with that ptarmigan hen—only the hawk had carried her away. Maybe there were other ptarmigan hens. He would go and see.

He came down a sloping bank to the stream. He had never seen water before. The footing looked good. There were no uneven places on the surface. He stepped boldly out on it and went down, crying with fear, into the embrace of the unknown. It was cold, and he gasped, breathing quickly. The water rushed into his lungs instead of the air that had always come with his breathing. The suffocation he felt was like the pain of death. To him it meant death. He had no conscious knowledge of death, but like every animal of the Wild, he had the instinct of death. To him it stood as the greatest of hurts. It was the very essence of the unknown. It was the sum of the terrors of the unknown, the one final and unthinkable disaster that could happen to him. He knew nothing about it and feared everything about it.

He came to the surface, and the sweet air rushed into his open mouth. He did not go down again. Quite as though it had been a long-established habit of his, he struck out with all his legs and began to swim. The near bank was a yard away. But he had come up with his back to it, and the first thing his eyes rested on was the opposite bank. He immediately began to swim toward it. The stream was a small one, but in the pool it widened out to twenty feet.

Midway in the passage, the current picked up the cub and swept him downstream. He was caught in the small rapid at the bottom of the pool. Here was little chance for swimming. The quiet water had suddenly become angry. Sometimes he was under, sometimes on top. At all times he was in violent motion, now being turned over or around, and again being smashed against a rock. And with every rock he struck, he yelped. His progress was a series of yelps, from which one might have counted the number of rocks he met.

Below the rapid was a second pool, and here, caught by the eddy, he was gently carried to the bank and gently laid on a bed of gravel. He crawled frantically clear of the water and lay down. He had learned some more about the world. Water was not alive. Yet it moved. Also, it looked as solid as the earth, but was without any solidity at all. His conclusion was that things were not always what they appeared to be. The cub’s fear of the unknown was an inherited distrust, and it had now been strengthened by experience. From then on, in the nature of things, he would have a lasting distrust of appearances. He would have to learn the reality of a thing before he could put his faith into it.

One other adventure was meant for him that day. He had remembered that there was such a thing in the world as his mother. And then a feeling came to him that he wanted her more than all the rest of the things in the world. Not only was his body tired with the adventures it had gone through, but his little brain was equally tired. In all the days he had lived it had not worked so hard as on this one day. Furthermore, he was sleepy. So he started out to look for the cave and his mother, feeling at the same time an overwhelming rush of loneliness and helplessness.

He was lying stretched out between some low plants when he heard a sharp, frightening cry. There was a flash of yellow before his eyes. He saw a small wild animal jump quickly away from him. It was a small living thing, and he was not afraid. Then, before him, at his feet, he saw a very small living thing, only a few inches long. It was a young one of that animal. Like himself, it had gone out exploring when it should not have. It tried to move back away from him. He turned it over with his foot. It made a strange, harsh noise. The next moment the flash of yellow appeared again before his eyes. He heard the frightening cry again. At the same moment he received a sharp blow on the side of his neck. He felt the sharp teeth of the mother animal cut into his flesh.

While he cried out and moved back in a hurry, he saw the mother jump on her young one. She disappeared with it into the nearby dense plants. The cut of her teeth in his neck still hurt. But his feelings were hurt much more deeply. He sat down and cried softly. This mother was so small and so fierce. He did not yet know that, for size and weight, this animal was the most fierce, terrible, and awful of all the killers of the Wild. But he was going to learn part of this knowledge very soon.

He was still crying softly when the mother appeared again. She did not rush at him now that her young one was safe. She came closer more carefully. The young wolf had a full chance to look at her thin, snake-like body. He also saw her head, raised up, eager, and snake-like itself. Her sharp, threatening cry made the hair stand up along his back. He made a low warning sound at her. She came closer and closer. There was a jump, faster than his untrained sight could follow. The thin yellow body disappeared for a moment from his field of vision. The next moment she was at his throat, her teeth buried in his hair and flesh.

At first he made a low sound and tried to fight. But he was very young, and this was only his first day in the world. His low sound became a soft cry. His fight became a struggle to escape. The animal never let go. She held on, trying to press down with her teeth to the great vein where his life blood flowed. This animal was a blood drinker, and she always preferred to drink from the throat of life itself.

The young wolf would have died, and there would have been no story to write about him, if the she-wolf had not come jumping through the low plants. The animal let go of the young wolf and flashed at the she-wolf's throat. She missed, but she got a hold on the jaw instead. The she-wolf turned her head quickly like the snap of a whip. This broke the animal's hold and threw it high in the air. And, still in the air, the she-wolf's jaws closed on the thin yellow body. The animal knew death between the crushing teeth.

The young wolf felt another wave of love from his mother. Her joy at finding him seemed even greater than his joy at being found. She pushed her nose against him and touched him gently. She licked the cuts made in him by the animal's teeth. Then, together, mother and young wolf, they ate the blood drinker. After that they went back to the cave and slept.

CHAPTER 5. THE COVENANT

THE COVENANT

When December was well along, Grey Beaver went on a journey up the Mackenzie. Mit-sah and Kloo-kooch went with him. He drove one sled himself, pulled by dogs he had traded for or borrowed. Mit-sah drove a second, smaller sled, and a team of young dogs was tied to it. It was more of a toy than anything else. Yet it was Mit-sah's delight, because he felt he was beginning to do a man's work in the world. He was also learning to drive dogs and to train dogs. At the same time, the young dogs themselves were being trained to pull. The sled was also of some use, because it carried nearly two hundred pounds of supplies and food.

White Fang had seen the camp dogs working hard in the ropes, so he did not mind too much the first time the ropes were put on him. Around his neck they put a collar filled with moss. Two pulling ropes connected it to a strap that went around his chest and over his back. The long rope was tied to this, and he pulled the sled with it.

There were seven young dogs in the team. The others had been born earlier in the year and were nine and ten months old. White Fang was only eight months old. Each dog was tied to the sled by a single rope. No two ropes were the same length. The difference in length between any two ropes was at least the length of a dog's body. Every rope was brought to a ring at the front end of the sled. The sled itself had no runners. It was a sled made of tree bark, with the front end turned up to keep it from digging under the snow. This design let the weight of the sled and load spread over the largest snow surface, because the snow was powder and very soft. Following the same idea of spreading the weight as widely as possible, the dogs at the ends of their ropes spread out like a fan from the front of the sled. In this way, no dog stepped in the footprints of another.

There was also another good point in the fan shape. The ropes of different lengths stopped the dogs from attacking those that ran in front of them. For a dog to attack another, it would have to turn on one at a shorter rope. Then it would find itself face to face with the dog it attacked. It would also find itself facing the whip of the driver. But the most special good point of all was this: the dog that tried to attack one in front of him had to pull the sled faster. And the faster the sled travelled, the faster the dog attacked could run away. So the dog behind could never catch up with the one in front. The faster he ran, the faster ran the one he was after, and the faster ran all the dogs. As a result, the sled went faster. So, by a clever hidden way, man increased his control over the animals.

Mit-sah looked like his father, and he had much of the grey wisdom of his father. In the past he had seen Lip-lip attack White Fang. But at that time Lip-lip was the dog of another man, and Mit-sah had never dared do more than throw a stone at him now and then. But now Lip-lip was his dog. He went ahead to take his revenge on him by putting him at the end of the longest rope. This made Lip-lip the leader, and it looked like a great prize! But in reality it took all pride away from him. Instead of being the bully and master of the pack, he now found himself hated and attacked by the pack.

Because he ran at the end of the longest rope, the dogs always saw him running away in front of them. All they saw of him was his bushy tail and his back legs running away. This was far less fierce and frightening than his stiff neck hair and shining teeth. Also, dogs are made in their minds in such a way that the sight of him running away made them want to run after him. It gave them a feeling that he was running away from them.

The moment the sled started, the team chased Lip-lip all day long. At first he was likely to turn on his pursuers, jealous of his dignity and angry. But at such times Mit-sah would throw the stinging lash of the thirty-foot whip into his face and force him to turn and run on. Lip-lip might face the pack, but he could not face that whip. All he could do was keep his long rope tight and keep his sides ahead of the teeth of his mates.

But a still greater cleverness hid deep in the Indian mind. To make the endless chase of the leader stronger, Mit-sah gave him more favour than the other dogs. These favours stirred jealousy and hatred in them. In their presence Mit-sah would give him meat and give it to him only. This drove them mad. They would rage around just outside the reach of the whip while Lip-lip ate the meat and Mit-sah protected him. And when there was no meat to give, Mit-sah would keep the team at a distance and pretend to give meat to Lip-lip.

White Fang took to the work kindly. He had travelled a greater distance than the other dogs in giving himself to the rule of the gods. He had learned more fully that it was useless to oppose their will. Also, the attacks he had suffered from the pack had made the pack less important to him in the order of things, and man more important. He had not learned to depend on his kind for friendship. Besides, Kiche was almost forgotten. The chief way left for him to express himself was in the loyalty he gave to the gods he had accepted as masters. So he worked hard, learned discipline, and was obedient. Faithfulness and willingness marked his work. These are essential qualities of the wolf and the wild dog when they have become tame, and White Fang had these qualities in unusual measure.

There was a friendship between White Fang and the other dogs, but it was one of war and hate. He had never learned to play with them. He knew only how to fight, and fight with them he did. He returned to them a hundred times the snaps and cuts they had given him in the days when Lip-lip was leader of the pack. But Lip-lip was no longer leader—except when he ran away before his mates at the end of his rope, with the sled jumping along behind. In camp he kept close to Mit-sah or Grey Beaver or Kloo-kooch. He did not dare go away from the gods, because now the teeth of all dogs were against him. He tasted to the last drop the attacks that had been White Fang's.

After he defeated Lip-lip, White Fang could have become leader of the pack. But he was too sad and alone for that. He only beat his team-mates. Other than that, he ignored them. They moved out of his way when he came near. Even the bravest of them never dared to steal his meat. Instead, they ate their own meat quickly, because they feared he would take it from them. White Fang knew the law well: _to hurt the weak and obey the strong_. He ate his share of meat as fast as he could. And then trouble came to the dog that had not yet finished! A low growl and a flash of teeth, and that dog would cry out his anger to the cold stars while White Fang finished his portion for him.

Every little while, however, one dog or another would rise up in rebellion and be quickly put down. So White Fang was kept in training. He was jealous of the isolation he kept for himself in the middle of the pack, and he fought often to keep it. But such fights were short. He was too quick for the others. They were cut open and bleeding before they knew what had happened. They were beaten almost before they had begun to fight.

White Fang kept discipline among his fellows as firm as the sled-discipline of the gods. He never allowed them any freedom. He made them show him unending respect. They might do as they pleased among themselves. That was no concern of his. But it _was_ his concern that they leave him alone in his isolation, move out of his way when he chose to walk among them, and at all times accept his power over them. A hint of stiff legs on their part, a lifted lip or raised hair, and he would be upon them, cruel and without mercy, quickly convincing them of their mistake.

He was a huge tyrant. His power was as hard as steel. He hurt the weak with a vengeance. It was not for nothing that he had faced the pitiless struggles for life in the days of his youth, when his mother and he, alone and without help, held their own and survived in the fierce environment of the Wild. And it was not for nothing that he had learned to walk softly when greater strength went by. He hurt the weak, but he respected the strong. And during the long journey with Grey Beaver he walked very softly among the full-grown dogs in the camps of the strange man-animals they met.

The months passed by. The journey of Grey Beaver continued. White Fang’s strength grew through the long hours on the trail and the steady work at the sled. It seemed that his mental growth was almost complete. He had come to know quite well the world in which he lived. His view was dark and materialistic. The world as he saw it was a fierce and brutal world, a world without warmth, a world in which gentle touches and affection and the bright sweetnesses of the spirit did not exist.

He had no affection for Grey Beaver. True, he was a god, but a most wild god. White Fang was glad to accept his rule, but it was a rule based on greater intelligence and brute strength. There was something deep in White Fang’s nature that made this rule a thing to be desired. Otherwise he would not have come back from the Wild when he did to offer his loyalty. There were deep places in his nature that had never been reached. A kind word, a gentle touch of the hand, from Grey Beaver, might have reached these deep places. But Grey Beaver did not touch him gently, nor speak kind words. It was not his way. His rule was wild, and wildly he ruled. He gave justice with a club. He punished wrongdoing with the pain of a blow. He rewarded good behavior, not with kindness, but by holding back a blow.

So White Fang knew nothing of the heaven a man’s hand might hold for him. Besides, he did not like the hands of the man-animals. He was suspicious of them. It was true that they sometimes gave meat, but more often they gave hurt. Hands were things to keep away from. They threw stones, held sticks and clubs and whips, gave slaps and blows. And when they touched him, they were clever at hurting with a pinch and a twist and a pull. In strange villages he had met the hands of children and learned that they were cruel and hurtful. Also, he had once nearly had an eye poked out by a small child who was just learning to walk. From these experiences he became suspicious of all children. He could not bear them. When they came near with their threatening hands, he got up.

It was in a village at the Great Slave Lake that, while resenting the evil of the hands of the man-animals, he came to change the law he had learned from Grey Beaver: namely, that the crime that could not be forgiven was to bite one of the gods. In this village, following the custom of all dogs in all villages, White Fang went looking for food. A boy was cutting frozen moose-meat with an axe, and the chips were flying in the snow. White Fang, sliding by in search of meat, stopped and began to eat the chips. He saw the boy put down the axe and pick up a thick club. White Fang jumped clear, just in time to escape the falling blow. The boy chased him, and he, a stranger in the village, ran between two tents and found himself trapped against a high bank of earth.

There was no escape for White Fang. The only way out was between the two tents, and the boy guarded this. Holding his club ready to strike, he closed in on his trapped target. White Fang was furious. He faced the boy, hair raised and growling, his sense of justice outraged. He knew the law of searching for food. All the waste of meat, such as the frozen chips, belonged to the dog that found it. He had done no wrong, broken no law, yet here was this boy getting ready to beat him. White Fang hardly knew what happened. He did it in a surge of rage. And he did it so quickly that the boy did not know either. All the boy knew was that he had in some strange way been thrown into the snow, and that his club-hand had been torn wide open by White Fang’s teeth.

But White Fang knew that he had broken the law of the gods. He had sunk his teeth into the holy flesh of one of them. He could expect nothing but a most terrible punishment. He ran away to Grey Beaver. He crouched behind Grey Beaver's legs, which protected him. The bitten boy and the boy's family came and demanded revenge. But they went away without revenge. Grey Beaver defended White Fang. So did Mit-sah and Kloo-kooch. White Fang listened to the long angry argument and watched the angry hand movements. He knew that his act was right. And so he learned that there were gods and gods. There were his gods, and there were other gods, and there was a difference between them. Right or wrong, it was all the same. He must take all things from the hands of his own gods. But he did not have to accept wrong from the other gods. It was his right to show anger with his teeth. And this also was a law of the gods.

Before the day was over, White Fang was to learn more about this law. Mit-sah was alone, gathering wood in the forest. He met the boy that had been bitten. Other boys were with him. Angry words were exchanged. Then all the boys attacked Mit-sah. He was in serious trouble. Blows fell on him from all sides. White Fang watched at first. This was a matter of the gods, and not his concern. Then he understood that this was Mit-sah, one of his own special gods, who was being badly treated. White Fang did not act from reasoned thought. A wild rush of anger sent him jumping in among the fighting boys. Five minutes later the ground was covered with running boys. Many of them dropped blood on the snow, a sign that White Fang's teeth had not been still. When Mit-sah told the story in camp, Grey Beaver ordered meat to be given to White Fang. He ordered a lot of meat to be given. White Fang ate until full and felt sleepy by the fire. He knew that the law had been proven true.

Through these experiences, White Fang came to learn the law of property and the duty to defend property. From protecting his god's body to protecting his god's things was one step, and he took this step. What belonged to his god must be defended against the whole world—even to the point of biting other gods. Such an act was not only an offence against the gods, but also full of danger. The gods were all-powerful, and a dog was no match for them. Yet White Fang learned to face them, fiercely ready to fight and unafraid. Duty rose above fear, and stealing gods learned to leave Grey Beaver's property alone.

One thing White Fang quickly learned in this matter: a stealing god was usually a cowardly god and likely to run away when the alarm sounded. He also learned that only a short time passed between his alarm and Grey Beaver coming to help him. He came to know that it was not fear of him that drove the thief away, but fear of Grey Beaver. White Fang did not give the alarm by barking. He never barked. His method was to run straight at the intruder and sink his teeth in if he could. He was gloomy and alone, having nothing to do with the other dogs. Because of this, he was especially suited to guard his master's property. Grey Beaver encouraged and trained him in this. One result was to make White Fang more fierce and wild, and more alone.

The months went by, making the agreement between dog and man stronger and stronger. This was the ancient agreement that the first wolf coming in from the Wild made with man. And, like all the wolves and wild dogs after him that had done the same, White Fang worked out the agreement for himself. The terms were simple. For the possession of a living god, he traded his own freedom. Food and fire, protection and friendship, were some of the things he received from the god. In return, he guarded the god's property, defended his body, worked for him, and obeyed him.

The possession of a god means service. White Fang's service was one of duty and fear, but not of love. He did not know what love was. He had no experience of love. Kiche was a distant memory. Besides, he had left the Wild and his kind when he gave himself up to man. And the terms of the agreement were such that if he ever met Kiche again, he would not leave his god to go with her. His loyalty to man seemed somehow a law of his being, greater than the love of freedom, of kind and family.

CHAPTER 6. THE LOVE-MASTER

THE LOVE-MASTER

As White Fang watched Weedon Scott approach, he raised his hair and made a low, angry sound to show that he would not accept punishment. Twenty-four hours had passed since he had cut open the hand that was now wrapped and held up in a sling to keep the blood out of it. In the past White Fang had experienced punishments that came later. He feared that such a one was about to happen to him. How could it be otherwise? He had done what was to him a great wrong. He had sunk his teeth into the holy flesh of a god, and of a white-skinned higher god at that. In the nature of things, and of dealings with gods, something terrible waited for him.

The god sat down several feet away. White Fang could see nothing dangerous in that. When the gods gave punishment they stood on their legs. Besides, this god had no club, no whip, no gun. And furthermore, he himself was free. No chain nor stick held him. He could escape into safety while the god was getting to his feet. In the meantime he would wait and see.

The god remained quiet and made no movement. And White Fang's angry sound slowly died down to a low sound that faded in his throat and stopped. Then the god spoke. At the first sound of his voice, the hair rose on White Fang's neck and the angry sound rushed up in his throat. But the god made no hostile movement, and went on talking in a calm way. For a time White Fang made his sound together with him. A matching rhythm was set up between the sound and the voice. But the god talked on and on. He talked to White Fang as White Fang had never been talked to before. He talked softly and in a comforting way, with a gentleness that somehow, somewhere, touched White Fang. In spite of himself and all the warning signs of his instinct, White Fang began to have confidence in this god. He had a feeling of safety that was contradicted by all his experience with men.

After a long time, the god got up and went into the cabin. White Fang watched him with fear when he came out. He had neither whip nor club nor weapon. Nor was his uninjured hand behind his back hiding something. He sat down as before, in the same spot, several feet away. He held out a small piece of meat. White Fang raised his ears and examined it with doubt. He managed to look at the same time both at the meat and the god. He was alert for any open act. His body was tight and ready to jump away at the first sign of hostility.

Still the punishment was delayed. The god merely held near to his nose a piece of meat. And about the meat there seemed nothing wrong. Still White Fang doubted. The meat was offered to him with short inviting pushes of the hand, but he refused to touch it. The gods were all-wise, and there was no telling what clever treachery hid behind that apparently harmless piece of meat. In past experience, especially in dealing with squaws, meat and punishment had often been terribly connected.

In the end, the god tossed the meat on the snow at White Fang's feet. He smelled the meat carefully, but he did not look at it. While he smelled it he kept his eyes on the god. Nothing happened. He took the meat into his mouth and swallowed it. Still nothing happened. The god was actually offering him another piece of meat. Again he refused to take it from the hand, and again it was tossed to him. This was repeated a number of times. But there came a time when the god refused to toss it. He kept it in his hand and firmly offered it.

The meat was good meat, and White Fang was hungry. Bit by bit, very carefully, he approached the hand. At last the time came that he decided to eat the meat from the hand. He never took his eyes from the god. He pushed his head forward with ears pressed back. His hair rose by itself and stood up on his neck. Also a low growl sounded in his throat as a warning that he was not to be played with. He ate the meat, and nothing happened. Piece by piece, he ate all the meat, and nothing happened. Still the punishment was delayed.

He licked his mouth and waited. The god went on talking. In his voice was kindness—something of which White Fang had no experience at all. And within him it raised feelings which he had likewise never experienced before. He was aware of a certain strange satisfaction. It was as though some need were being met, as though some empty place in his being were being filled. Then again came the push of his instinct and the warning of past experience. The gods were always clever, and they had unexpected ways of reaching their goals.

Ah, he had thought so! There it came now. The god's hand, clever at hurting, pushed out at him and came down upon his head. But the god went on talking. His voice was soft and comforting. In spite of the threatening hand, the voice gave confidence. And in spite of the reassuring voice, the hand gave distrust. White Fang was torn by conflicting feelings and urges. It seemed he would fly to pieces. So terrible was the control he was using. He held together by an unusual indecision the opposing forces that struggled within him for mastery.

He compromised. He growled and raised his hair and pressed his ears flat. But he neither snapped nor jumped away. The hand came down. Nearer and nearer it came. It touched the ends of his standing hair. He shrank down under it. It followed down after him, pressing more closely against him. Shrinking, almost shaking, he still managed to hold himself together. It was a torture, this hand that touched him and violated his instinct. He could not forget in a day all the evil that had been done to him at the hands of men. But it was the will of the god, and he tried to submit.

The hand lifted and came down again in a patting, stroking movement. This continued. But every time the hand lifted, the hair lifted under it. And every time the hand came down, the ears pressed flat and a deep growl rose in his throat. White Fang growled and growled with steady warning. By this means he announced that he was prepared to fight back for any hurt he might receive. There was no telling when the god's hidden purpose might be shown. At any moment that soft, confidence-giving voice might break out in a roar of anger. That gentle and stroking hand might turn itself into a tight grip to hold him helpless and give punishment.

But the god talked on softly. His hand kept rising and falling with gentle pats. White Fang had two feelings at once. It went against his instinct. It held him back and opposed his wish to be free. And yet it did not hurt him. In fact, it was even pleasant in a physical way. The patting slowly and carefully changed to a rubbing of the ears near their bases. The physical pleasure even grew a little. Yet he kept on fearing. He stood on guard, waiting for some unknown evil. He suffered and enjoyed by turns, as one feeling or the other rose highest and moved him.

"Well, I'll be gosh-swoggled!"

So spoke Matt, coming out of the cabin. His sleeves were rolled up, and he held a pan of dirty dish-water. He stopped in the act of emptying the pan when he saw Weedon Scott patting White Fang.

At the instant his voice broke the silence, White Fang leaped back and snarled fiercely at him.

Matt looked at his employer with grieved disapproval.

"If you don't mind my saying what I feel, Mr. Scott, I'll speak freely and say you are seventeen kinds of a damn fool, and all of them different, and then some."

Weedon Scott smiled with a superior air. He got to his feet and walked over to White Fang. He talked to him in a soothing way, but not for long. Then he slowly put out his hand, rested it on White Fang's head, and went on with the patting that had been interrupted. White Fang endured it. He kept his eyes fixed with suspicion, not on the man who patted him, but on the man who stood in the doorway.

"You may be a number one, tip-top mining expert, all right all right," the dog-musher said in a wise, knowing way, "but you missed the chance of your life when you were a boy and didn't run off and join a circus."

White Fang snarled at the sound of his voice. But this time he did not leap away from under the hand that was stroking his head and the back of his neck with long, soothing strokes.

It was the beginning of the end for White Fang—the ending of the old life and the rule of hate. A new and strangely fairer life was dawning. It took much thinking and endless patience on the part of Weedon Scott to make this happen. And on the part of White Fang it took nothing less than a complete change. He had to ignore the urges and promptings of instinct and reason. He had to defy experience and deny life itself.

Life, as he had known it, had not only had no place in it for much that he now did. All its currents had run against those to which he now gave himself. In short, all things considered, he had to reach a far vaster orientation than the one he had reached when he came in on his own from the Wild and accepted Grey Beaver as his lord. At that time he was a mere puppy, soft from the making, without form, ready for the thumb of circumstance to begin its work on him. But now it was different. The thumb of circumstance had done its work only too well. By it he had been formed and hardened into the Fighting Wolf, fierce and implacable, unloving and unlovable. To make the change was like a flowing back of his whole being. And this came when the flexibility of youth was no longer his; when the fibre of him had become tough and knotty; when the warp and the woof of him had made of him an unbreakable texture, harsh and unyielding; when the face of his spirit had become iron and all his instincts and axioms had hardened into set rules, cautions, dislikes, and desires.

Yet again, in this new orientation, it was the thumb of circumstance that pressed and prodded him. It softened what had become hard and reshaped it into a fairer form. Weedon Scott was in truth this thumb. He had gone to the roots of White Fang's nature. With kindness he touched to life powers that had faded and nearly died. One such power was _love_. It took the place of _like_, which had been the highest feeling that thrilled him in his dealings with the gods.

But this love did not come in a day. It began with _like_ and out of it slowly developed. White Fang did not run away, though he was allowed to remain loose, because he liked this new god. This was certainly better than the life he had lived in the cage of Beauty Smith. And it was necessary that he should have some god. The lordship of man was a need of his nature. The seal of his dependence on man had been set upon him in that early day when he turned his back on the Wild and crawled to Grey Beaver's feet to receive the expected beating. This seal had been stamped upon him again, and ineradicably, on his second return from the Wild, when the long famine was over and there was fish once more in the village of Grey Beaver.

And so, because he needed a god and because he preferred Weedon Scott to Beauty Smith, White Fang remained. In acknowledgment of fealty, he went on to take upon himself the guardianship of his master's property. He prowled about the cabin while the sled-dogs slept. The first night-visitor to the cabin fought him off with a club until Weedon Scott came to the rescue. But White Fang soon learned to tell the difference between thieves and honest men, and to judge the true value of step and carriage. The man who travelled with loud steps, straight to the cabin door, he let alone—though he watched him vigilantly until the door opened and he received the approval of the master. But the man who went softly, by indirect ways, peering with caution, seeking after secrecy—that was the man who received no pause in judgment from White Fang, and who went away abruptly, hurriedly, and without dignity.

Weedon Scott had set himself the task of redeeming White Fang—or rather, of redeeming mankind from the wrong it had done White Fang. It was a matter of principle and conscience. He felt that the ill done White Fang was a debt incurred by man and that it must be paid. So he went out of his way to be especially kind to the Fighting Wolf. Each day he made it a point to caress and pet White Fang, and to do it at length.

At first White Fang was suspicious and not friendly. But he grew to like this petting. Yet there was one thing he never lost—his low warning sound. He would make this sound from the moment the petting began until it ended. But it was a sound with something new in it. A stranger could not hear this new thing. To such a stranger, White Fang's warning sound seemed like a show of ancient wild nature, frightening and terrible. But White Fang's throat had become rough from making fierce sounds over many years, ever since his first little angry sound in the home of his early days. He could not soften the sounds of that throat now to show the gentleness he felt. Still, Weedon Scott's ear and sympathy were sharp enough to catch the new sound almost drowned in the fierceness. It was the faintest hint of a happy low sound, and no one but he could hear it.

As the days went by, the change from _like_ into _love_ came faster. White Fang himself began to become aware of it, though in his mind he did not know what love was. It showed itself to him as an empty place in his being—a hungry, painful, longing empty place that called out to be filled. It was a pain and a restlessness. And it found relief only when the new god was near. At such times love was joy to him, a wild, sharp, thrilling satisfaction. But when he was away from his god, the pain and the restlessness returned. The empty place in him rose up and pressed against him with its emptiness, and the hunger bit and bit without stopping.

White Fang was in the process of finding himself. In spite of the maturity of his years and the wild stiffness of the shape that had formed him, his nature was going through an expansion. There was a growing within him of strange feelings and unusual impulses. His old code of conduct was changing. In the past he had liked comfort and rest from pain. He had disliked discomfort and pain, and he had adjusted his actions accordingly. But now it was different. Because of this new feeling within him, he often chose discomfort and pain for the sake of his god. So, in the early morning, instead of walking about and searching for food, or lying in a sheltered corner, he would wait for hours on the cheerless cabin steps for a sight of the god's face. At night, when the god returned home, White Fang would leave the warm sleeping-place he had dug in the snow. He did this to receive the friendly snap of fingers and the word of greeting. Meat, even meat itself, he would give up to be with his god, to receive a caress from him or to go with him down into the town.

_Like_ had been replaced by _love_. And love was the heavy weight dropped down into the deeps of him where like had never gone. And out of his deeps had come the new thing—love. That which was given to him, he returned. This was a god indeed, a love-god, a warm and shining god. In his light White Fang's nature expanded as a flower expands under the sun.

But White Fang did not show his feelings. He was too old, too firmly formed, to become skilled at expressing himself in new ways. He was too self-possessed, too strongly balanced in his own isolation. Too long had he cultivated silence, distance, and gloom. He had never barked in his life, and he could not now learn to bark a welcome when his god came near. He was never in the way, never excessive nor foolish in the expression of his love. He never ran to meet his god. He waited at a distance. But he always waited, was always there. His love was like worship, silent, unable to speak, a quiet adoration. Only by the steady look of his eyes did he express his love, and by the endless following with his eyes of his god's every movement. Also, at times, when his god looked at him and spoke to him, he showed an awkward self-consciousness. This was caused by the struggle of his love to express itself and his physical inability to express it.

He learned to adjust himself in many ways to his new mode of life. It was made clear to him that he must leave his master's dogs alone. Yet his dominant nature asserted itself, and he had first to beat them into an acknowledgment of his superiority and leadership. This accomplished, he had little trouble with them. They gave way to him when he came and went or walked among them, and when he asserted his will they obeyed.

In the same way, he came to tolerate Matt—as a possession of his master. His master rarely fed him. Matt did that, it was his business. Yet White Fang understood that it was his master's food he ate and that it was his master who thus fed him indirectly. Matt it was who tried to put him into the harness and make him pull a sled with the other dogs. But Matt failed. It was not until Weedon Scott put the harness on White Fang and worked him that he understood. He took it as his master's will that Matt should drive him and work him just as he drove and worked his master's other dogs.

Different from the Mackenzie toboggans were the Klondike sleds with runners under them. And different was the method of driving the dogs. There was no fan-shape of the team. The dogs worked in single file, one behind another, pulling on double traces. And here, in the Klondike, the leader was indeed the leader. The wisest as well as strongest dog was the leader, and the team obeyed him and feared him. That White Fang should quickly gain this post was certain. He could not be satisfied with less, as Matt learned after much inconvenience and trouble. White Fang picked out the post for himself, and Matt backed his judgment with strong language after the experiment had been tried. But, though he worked in the sled in the day, White Fang did not give up the guarding of his master's property in the night. Thus he was on duty all the time, always watchful and faithful, the most valuable of all the dogs.

"Makin' free to spit out what's in me," Matt said one day, "I beg to state that you was a wise guy all right when you paid the price you did for that dog. You clean swindled Beauty Smith on top of pushin' his face in with your fist."

A return of anger glinted in Weedon Scott's grey eyes, and he muttered fiercely, "The beast!"

In the late spring a great trouble came to White Fang. Without warning, the love-master disappeared. There had been warning, but White Fang did not know such things. He did not understand the packing of a bag. He remembered afterwards that the packing had come before the master disappeared. But at the time he suspected nothing. That night he waited for the master to return. At midnight the cold wind drove him to shelter at the back of the cabin. There he half slept, his ears listening for the first sound of the familiar step. But at two in the morning, his worry drove him out to the cold front step. There he bent low and waited.

But no master came. In the morning the door opened and Matt stepped outside. White Fang looked at him with sad eyes. There was no common speech by which he might learn what he wanted to know. The days came and went, but never the master. White Fang, who had never known illness in his life, became ill. He became very ill, so ill that Matt was finally forced to bring him inside the cabin. Also, in writing to his employer, Matt added a note about White Fang.

Weedon Scott read the letter down in Circle City. He came upon the following:

“That dam wolf will not work. Will not eat. Has got no spirit left. All the dogs is beating him. Wants to know what has become of you, and I do not know how to tell him. Mebbe he is going to die.”

It was as Matt had said. White Fang had stopped eating. He lost heart. He let every dog of the team beat him. In the cabin he lay on the floor near the stove. He had no interest in food, in Matt, or in life. Matt might talk gently to him or swear at him. It was all the same. He never did more than turn his dull eyes upon the man. Then he dropped his head back to its usual position on his front legs.

And then, one night, Matt was reading to himself with moving lips and soft sounds. He was startled by a low cry from White Fang. White Fang had got upon his feet. His ears were raised towards the door, and he was listening hard. A moment later, Matt heard a step. The door opened, and Weedon Scott stepped in. The two men shook hands. Then Scott looked around the room.

“Where’s the wolf?” he asked.

Then he found him, standing where he had been lying, near to the stove. He had not rushed forward like other dogs. He stood, watching and waiting.

“Holy smoke!” Matt said loudly. “Look at him move his tail!”

Weedon Scott walked half across the room toward him, calling him at the same time. White Fang came to him, not with a great jump, yet quickly. He was awakened from thinking of himself. But as he drew near, his eyes took on a strange expression. Something, a feeling too great for words, rose up into his eyes like a light and shone forth.

“He never looked at me that way all the time you was gone!” Matt said.

Weedon Scott did not hear. He was bent down on his heels, face to face with White Fang, and petting him. He rubbed at the roots of the ears. He made long loving strokes down the neck to the shoulders. He tapped the spine gently with the balls of his fingers. And White Fang was making a low sound in answer. The soft note of the sound was stronger than ever.

But that was not all. His joy, the great love in him, was always rising and struggling to express itself. Now it succeeded in finding a new way to express itself. He suddenly pushed his head forward and pushed his way in between the master’s arm and body. And here, held close, hidden from view all except his ears, no longer making the low sound, he continued to push and press close.

The two men looked at each other. Scott’s eyes were shining.

“Gosh!” said Matt in a voice full of wonder.

A moment later, when he had recovered himself, he said, “I always insisted that wolf was a dog. Look at him!”

With the return of the love-master, White Fang got better quickly. He spent two nights and a day in the cabin. Then he went outside. The sled-dogs had forgotten his strength. They remembered only the latest thing, which was his weakness and illness. At the sight of him as he came out of the cabin, they jumped upon him.

“Talk about your rough-houses,” Matt said quietly with joy, standing in the doorway and looking on.

“Give him hell, you wolf! Give him hell!—and then some!”

White Fang did not need the support. The return of the love-master was enough. Life was flowing through him again, wonderful and strong. He fought from pure joy. He found in it a way to express much that he felt and that otherwise had no speech. There could be only one ending. The team scattered in a defeat that brought them shame. It was not until after dark that the dogs came sneaking back, one by one. By gentleness and a lowly manner they showed their loyalty to White Fang.

Having learned to press close, White Fang did it often. It was the final word. He could not go beyond it. The one thing he had always been especially watchful and bitter about was his head. He had always hated to have it touched. It was the Wild in him, the fear of hurt and of the trap, that had given rise to the sudden fearful urges to avoid contact. It was the command of his instinct that that head must be free. And now, with the love-master, his pressing close was the chosen act of putting himself into a position of complete weakness with no way out. It was an expression of perfect confidence, of complete self-surrender. It was as though he said: “I put myself into your hands. Do your will with me.”

One night, not long after the return, Scott and Matt sat at a game of cards before going to bed. “Fifteen-two, fifteen-four and a pair makes six,” Matt was moving his marker up, when there was a loud cry and sound of angry growling outside. They looked at each other as they started to rise to their feet.

“The wolf’s nailed somebody,” Matt said.

A wild scream of fear and pain hurried them.

“Bring a light!” Scott shouted, as he sprang outside.

Matt followed with the lamp. By its light they saw a man lying on his back in the snow. His arms were folded, one above the other, across his face and throat. Thus he was trying to shield himself from White Fang’s teeth. And there was need for it. White Fang was in a rage, cruelly making his attack on the most vulnerable spot. From shoulder to wrist of the crossed arms, the coat-sleeve, blue soft shirt and under shirt were ripped in torn pieces. The arms themselves were terribly cut and streaming blood.

The two men saw all this in the first moment. In the next moment Weedon Scott had White Fang by the throat. He was pulling him away. White Fang struggled and made angry sounds. But he did not try to bite. He became quiet quickly at a sharp word from the master.

Matt helped the man to his feet. As he stood up, he lowered his crossed arms. This showed the animal-like face of Beauty Smith. The dog man let go of him very suddenly. He acted like a man who had picked up live fire. Beauty Smith blinked in the lamp light and looked around. He saw White Fang and fear rushed into his face.

At the same moment Matt noticed two objects lying in the snow. He held the lamp close to them. He pointed at them with his toe for his boss to see—a steel dog chain and a heavy club.

Weedon Scott saw and nodded. No one spoke a word. The dog man put his hand on Beauty Smith’s shoulder and turned him to the right. No word needed to be spoken. Beauty Smith started to walk.

In the meantime the love master was patting White Fang and talking to him.

“Tried to steal you, right? And you would not let him! Well, well, he made a mistake, right?”

“He must have thought he had hold of many devils,” the dog man laughed quietly.

White Fang was still excited and his hair stood up. He made low angry sounds again and again. The hair slowly lay down. The soft singing sound was far away and weak, but it grew in his throat.
