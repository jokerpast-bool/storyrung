THE BOX OF ROBBERS

No one planned to leave Martha alone that afternoon. But it happened that everyone was called away, for one reason or another. Mrs. McFarland was at the weekly card party held by the Women’s Anti-Gambling League. Sister Nell’s young man had called quite without warning to take her for a long drive. Papa was at the office, as usual. It was Mary Ann’s day out. As for Emeline, she certainly should have stayed in the house and looked after the little girl. But Emeline had a restless nature.

“Would you mind, miss, if I just crossed the alley to speak a word to Mrs. Carleton’s girl?” she asked Martha.

“’Course not,” replied the child. “You’d better lock the back door, though, and take the key, for I shall be upstairs.”

“Oh, I’ll do that, of course, miss,” said the delighted maid. She ran away to spend the afternoon with her friend. She left Martha quite alone in the big house, and locked in, too.

The little girl read a few pages in her new book. She sewed a few stitches in her embroidery and started to “play visiting” with her four favorite dolls. Then she remembered a doll’s playhouse in the attic. It had not been used for months. So she decided she would dust it and put it in order.

Filled with this idea, the girl climbed the winding stairs to the big room under the roof. It was well lighted by three dormer windows and was warm and pleasant. Around the walls were rows of boxes and trunks, piles of old carpeting, pieces of damaged furniture, bundles of discarded clothing and other odds and ends of more or less value. Every well-regulated house has an attic of this sort, so I need not describe it.

The doll’s house had been moved. But after a search Martha found it away over in a corner near the big chimney.

She drew it out and noticed a black wooden chest behind it. Uncle Walter had sent it over from Italy years and years ago—before Martha was born, in fact. Mamma had told her about it one day. There was no key to it, because Uncle Walter wished it to remain unopened until he returned home. This wandering uncle was a mighty hunter. He had gone into Africa to hunt elephants and had never been heard from afterwards.

The little girl looked at the chest curiously. It had by accident attracted her attention.

It was quite big—bigger even than mamma’s traveling trunk. It was studded all over with tarnished brassheaded nails. It was heavy, too. When Martha tried to lift one end of it she found she could not stir it a bit. But there was a place in the side of the cover for a key. She stooped to examine the lock and saw that it would take a rather big key to open it.

Then, as you may suspect, the little girl longed to open Uncle Walter’s big box and see what was in it. For we are all curious, and little girls are just as curious as the rest of us.

“I don’t b’lieve Uncle Walter’ll ever come back,” she thought. “Papa said once that some elephant must have killed him. If I only had a key—” She stopped and clapped her little hands together gayly. She remembered a big basket of keys on the shelf in the linen closet. They were of all sorts and sizes. Perhaps one of them would unlock the mysterious chest!

She flew down the stairs, found the basket and returned with it to the attic. Then she sat down before the brass-studded box and began trying one key after another in the curious old lock. Some were too large, but most were too small. One would go into the lock but would not turn. Another stuck so fast that she feared for a time that she would never get it out again. But at last, when the basket was almost empty, an oddly-shaped, ancient brass key slipped easily into the lock. With a cry of joy Martha turned the key with both hands. Then she heard a sharp “click,” and the next moment the heavy lid flew up of its own accord!

The little girl leaned over the edge of the chest an instant. The sight that met her eyes caused her to start back in amazement.

Slowly and carefully a man unpacked himself from the chest. He stepped out upon the floor, stretched his limbs and then took off his hat. He bowed politely to the astonished child.

He was tall and thin and his face seemed badly tanned or sunburnt.

Then another man emerged from the chest, yawning and rubbing his eyes like a sleepy schoolboy. He was of middle size and his skin seemed as badly tanned as that of the first.

While Martha stared open-mouthed at the remarkable sight a third man crawled from the chest. He had the same complexion as his fellows, but was short and fat.

All three were dressed in a curious manner. They wore short jackets of red velvet braided with gold, and knee breeches of sky-blue satin with silver buttons. Over their stockings were laced wide ribbons of red and yellow and blue. Their hats had broad brims with high, peaked crowns, from which fluttered yards of bright-colored ribbons.

They had big gold rings in their ears and rows of knives and pistols in their belts. Their eyes were black and glittering and they wore long, fierce mustaches, curling at the ends like a pig’s tail.

“My! but you were heavy,” exclaimed the fat one, when he had pulled down his velvet jacket and brushed the dust from his sky-blue breeches. “And you squeezed me all out of shape.”

“It was unavoidable, Luigi,” responded the thin man, lightly; “the lid of the chest pressed me down upon you. Yet I tender you my regrets.”

“As for me,” said the middle-sized man, carelessly rolling a cigarette and lighting it, “you must acknowledge I have been your nearest friend for years; so do not be disagreeable.”

“You mustn’t smoke in the attic,” said Martha, recovering herself at sight of the cigarette. “You might set the house on fire.”

The middle-sized man, who had not noticed her before, at this speech turned to the girl and bowed.

“Since a lady requests it,” said he, “I shall abandon my cigarette,” and he threw it on the floor and extinguished it with his foot.

“Who are you?” asked Martha, who until now had been too astonished to be frightened.

"Let us tell you who we are," said the thin man. He waved his hat in a smooth way. "This is Lugui," the fat man nodded. "And this is Beni," the middle-sized man bowed. "And I am Victor. We are three bandits—Italian bandits."

"Bandits!" cried Martha. She looked afraid.

"Exactly. Perhaps in all the world there are no other three bandits as terrible and fierce as us," said Victor, proudly.

"It is so," said the fat man. He nodded seriously.

"But it is bad!" exclaimed Martha.

"Yes, indeed," replied Victor. "We are extremely and very bad. Perhaps in all the world you could not find three men more bad than those who now stand before you."

"It is so," said the fat man, in agreement.

"But you should not be so bad," said the girl. "It is—it is—wrong!"

Victor cast down his eyes and his face turned red.

"Wrong!" gasped Beni. He looked horrified.

"It is a hard word," said Luigi, sadly. He buried his face in his hands.

"I little thought," said Victor in a low voice, broken by feeling, "ever to be so spoken against—and by a lady! Yet perhaps you spoke without thinking. You must consider, miss, that our badness has an excuse. For how are we to be bandits, let me ask, unless we are bad?"

Martha was puzzled and shook her head, thinking. Then she remembered something.

"You cannot remain bandits any longer," said she, "because you are now in America."

"America!" cried the three, together.

"Certainly. You are on Prairie avenue, in Chicago. Uncle Walter sent you here from Italy in this chest."

The bandits seemed greatly confused by this news. Lugui sat down on an old chair with a broken rocker. He wiped his forehead with a yellow silk cloth. Beni and Victor fell back upon the chest. They looked at her with pale faces and staring eyes.

When he had somewhat recovered himself Victor spoke.

"Your Uncle Walter has greatly wronged us," he said, in a complaining way. "He has taken us from our beloved Italy, where bandits are highly respected. He brought us to a strange country where we shall not know whom to rob or how much to ask for a ransom."

"It is so!" said the fat man. He slapped his leg sharply.

"And we had won such fine reputations in Italy!" said Beni, sadly.

"Perhaps Uncle Walter wanted to reform you," suggested Martha.

"Are there, then, no bandits in Chicago?" asked Victor.

"Well," replied the girl, her face turning red in her turn, "we do not call them bandits."

"Then what shall we do for a living?" asked Beni, without hope.

"A great deal can be done in a big American city," said the child. "My father is a lawyer" (the bandits shook with fear), "and my mother's cousin is a police inspector."

"Ah," said Victor, "that is a good employment. The police need to be inspected, especially in Italy."

"Everywhere!" added Beni.

"Then you could do other things," continued Martha, in a hopeful way. "You could be motor men on trolley cars, or clerks in a department store. Some people even become aldermen to earn a living."

The bandits shook their heads sadly.

"We are not fitted for such work," said Victor. "Our business is to rob."

Martha tried to think.

"It is rather hard to get positions in the gas office," she said, "but you might become politicians."

"No!" cried Beni, with sudden fierceness. "We must not abandon our high calling. Bandits we have always been, and bandits we must remain!"

"It is so!" agreed the fat man.

"Even in Chicago there must be people to rob," remarked Victor, with cheerfulness.

Martha was upset.

"I think they have all been robbed," she objected.

"Then we can rob the robbers, for we have experience and talent beyond the ordinary," said Beni.

"Oh, dear; oh, dear!" moaned the girl. "Why did Uncle Walter ever send you here in this chest?"

The bandits became interested.

"That is what we should like to know," declared Victor, eagerly.

"But no one will ever know, for Uncle Walter was lost while hunting elephants in Africa," she continued, with certainty.

"Then we must accept our fate and rob to the best of our ability," said Victor. "So long as we are faithful to our beloved profession we need not be ashamed."

"It is so!" cried the fat man.

"Brothers! We will begin now. Let us rob the house we are in."

"Good!" shouted the others and sprang to their feet.

Beni turned threateningly upon the child.

"Remain here!" he commanded. "If you move one step your blood will be on your own head!" Then he added, in a gentler voice: "Do not be afraid; that is the way all bandits talk to their captives. But of course we would not hurt a young lady under any circumstances."

"Of course not," said Victor.

The fat man drew a big knife from his belt and waved it about his head.

"By blood!" he said, fiercely.

"By bananas!" cried Beni, in a terrible voice.

"Confusion to our foes!" said Victor in a low, sharp voice.

And then the three bent themselves nearly double. They crept quietly down the stairway with loaded pistols in their hands and shining knives between their teeth. They left Martha shaking with fear and too horrified to even cry for help.

How long she remained alone in the attic she never knew. But finally she heard the catlike step of the returning bandits. She saw them coming up the stairs in single file.

All carried heavy loads of stolen goods in their arms. Lugui was balancing a mince pie on the top of a pile of her mother's best evening dresses. Victor came next with an armful of small ornaments, a brass candlestick holder and the parlor clock. Beni had the family Bible, the basket of silverware from the sideboard, a copper kettle and papa's fur overcoat.

"Oh, joy!" said Victor, putting down his load. "It is pleasant to rob once more."

"Oh, great joy!" said Beni. But he let the kettle drop on his toe and immediately began dancing around in pain. He said strange words in the Italian language.

"We have much wealth," continued Victor, holding the mince pie while Lugui added his stolen goods to the heap. "And all from one house! This America must be a rich place."

With a knife he then cut himself a piece of the pie and handed the rest to his comrades. Whereupon all three sat upon the floor and ate the pie while Martha looked on sadly.

"We should have a cave," remarked Beni, "for we must store our stolen goods in a safe place. Can you tell us of a secret cave?" he asked Martha.

"There is a Mammoth cave," she answered, "but it is in Kentucky. You would be obliged to ride on the cars a long time to get there."

The three bandits looked thoughtful and chewed their pie silently. But the next moment they were startled by the ringing of the electric doorbell. It was heard plainly even in the far attic.

"What is that?" demanded Victor, in a rough voice. The three scrambled to their feet with drawn knives.

Martha ran to the window. She saw it was only the postman. He had dropped a letter in the box and gone away again. But this event gave her an idea of how to get rid of her annoying robbers. So she began wringing her hands as if in great pain and cried out:

“It is the police!”

The robbers looked at one another with real fear. Lugui asked, in a shaking voice:

“Are there many of them?”

“A hundred and twelve!” cried Martha, after pretending to count them.

“Then we are lost!” said Beni. “We could never fight so many and live.”

“Are they armed?” asked Victor, who was shaking as if cold.

“Oh, yes,” she said. “They have guns and swords and pistols and axes and—and—”

“And what?” demanded Lugui.

“And cannons!”

The three bad men groaned aloud. Beni said, in a dull voice:

“I hope they will kill us quickly and not torture us. I have been told these Americans are painted Indians. They are cruel and terrible.”

“It is so!” gasped the fat man, with a shake.

Suddenly Martha turned from the window.

“You are my friends, are you not?” she asked.

“We are faithful!” answered Victor.

“We love you!” cried Beni.

“We would die for you!” added Lugui, thinking he was about to die anyway.

“Then I will save you,” said the girl.

“How?” asked the three, with one voice.

“Get back into the chest,” she said. “I will then close the lid, so they will be unable to find you.”

They looked around the room in a confused and uncertain way. But she cried out:

“You must be quick! They will soon be here to arrest you.”

Then Lugui jumped into the chest and lay flat on the bottom. Beni fell in next and packed himself against the back side. Victor followed after pausing to kiss her hand in a fine manner.

Then Martha ran up to press down the lid, but could not make it catch.

“You must squeeze down,” she said to them.

Lugui groaned.

“I am doing my best, miss,” said Victor, who was nearest the top. “But although we fitted in very well before, the chest now seems rather small for us.”

“It is so!” came the quiet voice of the fat man from the bottom.

“I know what takes up the room,” said Beni.

“What?” asked Victor, worried.

“The pie,” returned Beni.

“It is so!” came from the bottom, in weak tones.

Then Martha sat upon the lid and pressed it down with all her weight. To her great joy the lock caught. She jumped down, used all her strength and turned the key.

This story should teach us not to interfere in matters that do not concern us. For if Martha had not opened Uncle Walter’s strange chest, she would not have had to carry downstairs all the stolen goods the robbers had brought into the attic.

THE GLASS DOG

A skilled wizard once lived on the top floor of a crowded old building. He passed his time in deep study and careful thought. What he did not know about magic was hardly worth knowing. He had all the books and recipes of all the wizards who had lived before him. And he had also invented several new magic tricks himself.

This fine person would have been completely happy except for the many interruptions to his studies. People came to ask him about their troubles, which did not interest him. And there were loud knocks from the iceman, the milkman, the baker’s boy, the laundryman and the peanut woman. He never dealt with any of these people. But they knocked at his door every day to see him about this or that, or to try to sell him their goods. Just when he was most deeply interested in his books, or watching the bubbling of a pot, there would come a knock at his door. And after sending the intruder away, he always found he had lost his train of thought or ruined his mixture.

At last these interruptions made him angry. He decided he must have a dog to keep people away from his door. He did not know where to find a dog. But in the next room lived a poor glass-blower he knew slightly. So he went into the man’s apartment and asked:

“Where can I find a dog?”

“What sort of a dog?” asked the glass-blower.

“A good dog. One that will bark at people and drive them away. One that will be no trouble to keep and will not expect to be fed. One that has no fleas and is clean in his habits. One that will obey me when I speak to him. In short, a good dog,” said the wizard.

“Such a dog is hard to find,” returned the glass-blower. He was busy making a blue glass flower pot with a pink glass rosebush in it. It had green glass leaves and yellow glass roses.

The wizard watched him thoughtfully.

“Why cannot you blow me a dog out of glass?” he asked, after a moment.

“I can,” said the glass-blower. “But it would not bark at people, you know.”

“Oh, I’ll fix that easily enough,” replied the other. “If I could not make a glass dog bark, I would be a very poor wizard.”

“Very well. If you can use a glass dog, I’ll be pleased to blow one for you. Only, you must pay for my work.”

“Certainly,” agreed the wizard. “But I have none of that awful stuff you call money. You must take some of my goods in exchange.”

The glass-blower thought about the matter for a moment.

“Could you give me something to cure my rheumatism?” he asked.

“Oh, yes. Easily.”

“Then it is a deal. I’ll start the dog at once. What color of glass shall I use?”

“Pink is a pretty color,” said the wizard. “And it is unusual for a dog, is it not?”

“Very,” answered the glass-blower. “But it shall be pink.”

So the wizard went back to his studies and the glass-blower began to make the dog.

Next morning he entered the wizard’s room with the glass dog under his arm and set it carefully upon the table. It was a beautiful pink in color, with a fine coat of spun glass. About its neck was twisted a blue glass ribbon. Its eyes were bits of black glass and sparkled with intelligence, as many of the glass eyes worn by men do.

The wizard said he was pleased with the glass-blower’s skill and at once handed him a small vial.

“This will cure your rheumatism,” he said.

“But the vial is empty!” protested the glass-blower.

“Oh, no. There is one drop of liquid in it,” was the wizard’s reply.

“Will one drop cure my rheumatism?” asked the glass-blower, in wonder.

"Of course. That is a wonderful cure. The one drop inside the small bottle will cure at once any kind of sickness ever known to people. So it is especially good for joint pain. But keep it safe, because it is the only drop of its kind in the world, and I have forgotten how to make it."

“Thank you,” said the glass-blower, and went back to his room.

Then the wizard cast a magic spell. He spoke several very learned words in the wizard language over the glass dog. After that the little animal first moved its tail from side to side. Then it closed and opened its left eye in a clever way. At last it began barking in a most terrible manner. That is, when you stop to think the noise came from a pink glass dog. There is something almost surprising in the magic arts of wizards. Unless, of course, you know how to do the things yourself. Then you are not expected to be surprised at them.

The wizard was as pleased as a school teacher at the success of his spell, although he was not surprised. At once he placed the dog outside his door. There it would bark at anyone who dared knock. In that way it would not disturb the studies of its master.

The glass-blower, on returning to his room, decided not to use the one drop of wizard cure-all just then.

“My rheumatism is better to-day,” he thought. “I will be wise to save the medicine for a time when I am very ill. Then it will be of more help to me.”

So he placed the small bottle in his cupboard and went to work blowing more roses out of glass. Soon he happened to think the medicine might not keep. So he started to ask the wizard about it. But when he reached the door the glass dog barked so fiercely that he dared not knock. He returned in great haste to his own room. Indeed, the poor man was quite upset at so unfriendly a welcome from the dog he had himself so carefully and skillfully made.

The next morning, as he read his newspaper, he noticed an article. It said that the beautiful Miss Mydas, the richest young lady in town, was very ill. The doctors had given up hope of her recovery.

The glass-blower, although miserably poor, hard-working and plain of face, was a man of ideas. He suddenly remembered his precious medicine. He determined to use it to better advantage than relieving his own ills. He dressed himself in his best clothes. He brushed his hair and combed his whiskers. He washed his hands and tied his necktie. He blackened his shoes and sponged his vest. Then he put the small bottle of magic cure-all in his pocket. Next he locked his door, went downstairs and walked through the streets. He went to the grand mansion where the wealthy Miss Mydas resided.

The butler opened the door and said:

“No soap, no chromos, no vegetables, no hair oil, no books, no baking powder. My young lady is dying and we’re well supplied for the funeral.”

The glass-blower was grieved at being taken for a peddler.

“My friend,” he began, proudly. But the butler interrupted him, saying:

“No tombstones, either. There’s a family graveyard and the monument’s built.”

“The graveyard won’t be needed if you will permit me to speak,” said the glass-blower.

“No doctors, sir. They’ve given up my young lady, and she’s given up the doctors,” continued the butler, calmly.

“I’m no doctor,” returned the glass-blower.

“Nor are the others. But what is your errand?”

“I called to cure your young lady by means of a magical compound.”

“Step in, please, and take a seat in the hall. I’ll speak to the housekeeper,” said the butler, more politely.

So he spoke to the housekeeper. The housekeeper mentioned the matter to the steward. The steward consulted the chef. The chef kissed the lady’s maid and sent her to see the stranger. Thus are the very wealthy hedged around with ceremony, even when dying.

The lady’s maid heard from the glass-blower that he had a medicine which would cure her mistress. She said:

“I’m glad you came.”

“But,” said he, “if I restore your mistress to health she must marry me.”

“I’ll make inquiries and see if she’s willing,” answered the maid. She went at once to consult Miss Mydas.

The young lady did not hesitate an instant.

“I’d marry any old thing rather than die!” she cried. “Bring him here at once!”

So the glass-blower came. He poured the magic drop into a little water and gave it to the patient. The next minute Miss Mydas was as well as she had ever been in her life.

“Dear me!” she exclaimed. “I’ve an engagement at the Fritters’ reception to-night. Bring my pearl-colored silk, Marie, and I will begin my toilet at once. And don’t forget to cancel the order for the funeral flowers and your mourning gown.”

“But, Miss Mydas,” remonstrated the glass-blower, who stood by, “you promised to marry me if I cured you.”

“I know,” said the young lady. “But we must have time to make proper announcement in the society papers and have the wedding cards engraved. Call to-morrow and we’ll talk it over.”

The glass-blower had not impressed her favorably as a husband. She was glad to find an excuse for getting rid of him for a time. And she did not want to miss the Fritters’ reception.

Yet the man went home filled with joy. For he thought his plan had succeeded. He was about to marry a rich wife who would keep him in luxury forever afterward.

The first thing he did on reaching his room was to smash his glass-blowing tools and throw them out of the window.

He then sat down to figure out ways of spending his wife’s money.

The following day he called upon Miss Mydas. She was reading a novel and eating chocolate creams as happily as if she had never been ill in her life.

“Where did you get the magic compound that cured me?” she asked.

“From a learned wizard,” said he. Then he thought it would interest her. So he told how he had made the glass dog for the wizard. He told how it barked and kept everybody from bothering him.

“How delightful!” she said. “I’ve always wanted a glass dog that could bark.”

“But there is only one in the world,” he answered, “and it belongs to the wizard.”

“You must buy it for me,” said the lady.

“The wizard cares nothing for money,” replied the glass-blower.

“Then you must steal it for me,” she retorted. “I can never live happily another day unless I have a glass dog that can bark.”

The glass-blower was much distressed at this. But he said he would see what he could do. For a man should always try to please his wife. And Miss Mydas has promised to marry him within a week.

On his way home he purchased a heavy sack. When he passed the wizard’s door the pink glass dog ran out to bark at him. He threw the sack over the dog and tied the opening with a piece of twine. Then he carried him away to his own room.

The next day he sent the sack by a messenger boy to Miss Mydas, with his polite words. Later in the afternoon he visited her in person. He felt quite sure she would welcome him with thanks for stealing the dog she wanted so much.

But when he came to the door and the butler opened it, he was amazed to see the glass dog rush out and begin barking at him angrily.

"Call off your dog," he shouted, in fear.

"I can’t, sir," answered the butler. "My young lady has ordered the glass dog to bark whenever you come here. You’d better be careful, sir," he added, "for if it bites you, you may have glassophobia!"

This so frightened the poor glass-blower that he went away quickly. But he stopped at a drug store and put his last dime in the telephone box. He wanted to talk to Miss Mydas without being bitten by the dog.

"Give me Pelf 6742!" he called.

"Hello! What is it?" said a voice.

"I want to speak with Miss Mydas," said the glass-blower.

Soon a sweet voice said: "This is Miss Mydas. What is it?"

"Why have you treated me so cruelly and set the glass dog on me?" asked the poor fellow.

"Well, to tell the truth," said the lady, "I don’t like your looks. Your cheeks are pale and baggy, your hair is coarse and long, your eyes are small and red, your hands are big and rough, and you are bow-legged."

"But I can’t help my looks!" pleaded the glass-blower; "and you really promised to marry me."

"If you were better looking I’d keep my promise," she returned. "But under the circumstances you are no fit mate for me. And unless you keep away from my mansion I shall set my glass dog on you!" Then she dropped the ’phone and would have nothing more to say.

The miserable glass-blower went home with a heart bursting with disappointment. He began tying a rope to the bedpost by which to hang himself.

Some one knocked at the door, and, upon opening it, he saw the wizard.

"I’ve lost my dog," he announced.

"Have you, indeed?" replied the glass-blower tying a knot in the rope.

"Yes; some one has stolen him."

"That’s too bad," declared the glass-blower, indifferently.

"You must make me another," said the wizard.

"But I cannot; I’ve thrown away my tools."

"Then what shall I do?" asked the wizard.

"I do not know, unless you offer a reward for the dog."

"But I have no money," said the wizard.

"Offer some of your compounds, then," suggested the glass-blower, who was making a noose in the rope for his head to go through.

"The only thing I can spare," replied the wizard, thoughtfully, "is a Beauty Powder."

"What!" cried the glass-blower, throwing down the rope, "have you really such a thing?"

"Yes, indeed. Whoever takes the powder will become the most beautiful person in the world."

"If you will offer that as a reward," said the glass-blower, eagerly, "I’ll try to find the dog for you, for above everything else I long to be beautiful."

"But I warn you the beauty will only be skin deep," said the wizard.

"That’s all right," replied the happy glass-blower; "when I lose my skin I shan’t care to remain beautiful."

"Then tell me where to find my dog and you shall have the powder," promised the wizard.

So the glass-blower went out and pretended to search, and by-and-by he returned and said:

"I’ve discovered the dog. You will find him in the mansion of Miss Mydas."

The wizard went at once to see if this were true, and, sure enough, the glass dog ran out and began barking at him. Then the wizard spread out his hands and chanted a magic spell which sent the dog fast asleep, when he picked him up and carried him to his own room on the top floor of the tenement house.

Afterward he carried the Beauty Powder to the glass-blower as a reward, and the fellow immediately swallowed it and became the most beautiful man in the world.

The next time he called upon Miss Mydas there was no dog to bark at him, and when the young lady saw him she fell in love with his beauty at once.

"If only you were a count or a prince," she sighed, "I’d willingly marry you."

"But I am a prince," he answered; "the Prince of Dogblowers."

"Ah!" said she; "then if you are willing to accept an allowance of four dollars a week I’ll order the wedding cards engraved."

The man hesitated, but when he thought of the rope hanging from his bedpost he consented to the terms.

So they were married, and the bride was very jealous of her husband’s beauty and led him a dog’s life. So he managed to get into debt and made her miserable in turn.

As for the glass dog, the wizard set him barking again by means of his wizardness and put him outside his door. I suppose he is there yet, and am rather sorry, for I should like to consult the wizard about the moral to this story.

THE QUEEN OF QUOK

A king once died, as kings are apt to do, being as liable to shortness of breath as other mortals.

It was high time this king abandoned his earth life, for he had lived in a sadly extravagant manner, and his subjects could spare him without the slightest inconvenience.

His father had left him a full treasury, both money and jewels being in abundance. But the foolish king just deceased had squandered every penny in riotous living. He had then taxed his subjects until most of them became paupers, and this money vanished in more riotous living. Next he sold all the grand old furniture in the palace; all the silver and gold plate and bric-a-brac; all the rich carpets and furnishings and even his own kingly wardrobe, reserving only a soiled and moth-eaten ermine robe to fold over his threadbare raiment. And he spent the money in further riotous living.

Don’t ask me to explain what riotous living is. I only know, from hearsay, that it is an excellent way to get rid of money. And so this spendthrift king found it.

He now picked all the magnificent jewels from this kingly crown and from the round ball on the top of his scepter, and sold them and spent the money. Riotous living, of course. But at last he was at the end of his resources. He couldn’t sell the crown itself, because no one but the king had the right to wear it. Neither could he sell the royal palace, because only the king had the right to live there.

So, finally, he found himself reduced to a bare palace, containing only a big mahogany bedstead that he slept in, a small stool on which he sat to pull off his shoes and the moth-eaten ermine robe.

In this difficult situation, he had to borrow a dime now and then from his chief counselor to buy a ham sandwich. And the chief counselor did not have many dimes. A man who gave his king such foolish advice was likely to ruin his own future as well.

So the king, having nothing more to live for, died suddenly. He left a ten-year-old son to inherit the broken kingdom, the moth-eaten robe and the crown with all its jewels gone.

No one envied the child. People had hardly thought of him until he became king himself. Then he was seen as a person of some importance. The politicians and their followers, led by the chief counselor of the kingdom, held a meeting to decide what could be done for him.

These people had helped the old king live wildly while his money lasted. Now they were poor and too proud to work. So they tried to think of a plan that would bring more money into the little king's treasury. There it would be easy for them to help themselves.

After the meeting was over, the chief counselor came to the young king. The boy was playing with a top in the courtyard. The counselor said:

"Your majesty, we have thought of a way to restore your kingdom to its former power and glory."

"All right," replied his majesty, carelessly. "How will you do it?"

"By marrying you to a lady of great wealth," replied the counselor.

"Marrying me!" cried the king. "Why, I am only ten years old!"

"I know; it is to be regretted. But your majesty will grow older. And the affairs of the kingdom demand that you marry a wife."

"Can't I marry a mother, instead?" asked the poor little king. He had lost his mother when he was a baby.

"Certainly not," declared the counselor. "To marry a mother would be illegal. To marry a wife is right and proper."

"Can't you marry her yourself?" asked his majesty. He aimed his top at the chief counselor's toe and laughed to see how he jumped to escape it.

"Let me explain," said the other. "You have not a penny in the world, but you have a kingdom. There are many rich women who would be glad to give their wealth in exchange for a queen's crown—even if the king is only a child. So we have decided to advertise that the one who offers the highest price shall become the queen of Quok."

"If I must marry at all," said the king, after a moment's thought, "I prefer to marry Nyana, the armorer's daughter."

"She is too poor," replied the counselor.

"Her teeth are pearls, her eyes are amethysts, and her hair is gold," declared the little king.

"True, your majesty. But consider that your wife's wealth must be used. How would Nyana look after you have pulled her teeth of pearls, plucked out her amethyst eyes and shaved her golden head?"

The boy shuddered.

"Have your own way," he said, despairingly. "Only let the lady be as dainty as possible and a good playfellow."

"We shall do our best," returned the chief counselor. Then he went away to advertise throughout the neighboring kingdoms for a wife for the boy king of Quok.

There were so many applicants for the privilege of marrying the little king that it was decided to put him up at auction. This way the largest possible sum of money would be brought into the kingdom. So, on the day appointed, the ladies gathered at the palace from all the surrounding kingdoms—from Bilkon, Mulgravia, Junkum and even as far away as the republic of Macvelt.

The chief counselor came to the palace early in the morning. He had the king's face washed and his hair combed. Then he padded the inside of the crown with old newspapers to make it small enough to fit his majesty's head. It was a sorry looking crown, with many big and little holes in it where the jewels had once been. It had been neglected and knocked around until it was quite battered and tarnished. Yet, as the counselor said, it was the king's crown. And it was quite proper he should wear it on the solemn occasion of his auction.

Like all boys, be they kings or paupers, his majesty had torn and soiled his one suit of clothes. They were hardly presentable, and there was no money to buy new ones. Therefore the counselor wound the old ermine robe around the king and sat him upon the stool in the middle of the otherwise empty audience chamber.

And around him stood all the courtiers and politicians and followers of the kingdom. They were such people as were too proud or lazy to work for a living. There was a great number of them, you may be sure, and they made an imposing appearance.

Then the doors of the audience chamber were thrown open. The wealthy ladies who aspired to being queen of Quok came trooping in. The king looked them over with much anxiety. He decided they were each and all old enough to be his grandmother, and ugly enough to scare away the crows from the royal cornfields. After which he lost interest in them.

But the rich ladies never looked at the poor little king squatting upon his stool. They gathered at once about the chief counselor, who acted as auctioneer.

"How much am I offered for the crown of the queen of Quok?" asked the counselor, in a loud voice.

"Where is the crown?" asked a fussy old lady. She had just buried her ninth husband and was worth several millions.

"There isn't any crown at present," explained the chief counselor. "But whoever offers the highest price will have the right to wear one, and she can then buy it."

"Oh," said the fussy old lady, "I see." Then she added: "I'll bid fourteen dollars."

"Fourteen thousand dollars!" cried a sour-looking woman. She was thin and tall and had wrinkles all over her skin—"like a frosted apple," the king thought.

The bidding now became fast and furious. The poverty-stricken courtiers brightened up as the sum began to mount into the millions.

"He'll bring us a very pretty fortune, after all," whispered one to his comrade. "And then we shall have the pleasure of helping him spend it."

The king began to be anxious. All the women who looked at all kind-hearted or pleasant had stopped bidding for lack of money. The slender old dame with the wrinkles seemed determined to get the crown at any price, and with it the boy husband. This ancient creature finally became so excited that her wig got crosswise of her head and her false teeth kept slipping out. This horrified the little king greatly, but she would not give up.

At last the chief counselor ended the auction by crying out:

"Sold to Mary Ann Brodjinsky de la Porkus for three million, nine hundred thousand, six hundred and twenty-four dollars and sixteen cents!" And the sour-looking old woman paid the money in cash and on the spot, which proves this is a fairy story.

This was not very good poetry. But when the king read it slowly in the moonlight, he was filled with joy.

“There is no doubt that I am in trouble,” he said. “So I will burn it at once and see what happens.”

That mighty person arrived looking sad and unhappy. But the boy was too full of his own good fortune to notice it. He said:

The king sighed.

“I fear you must marry Mary Ann Brodjinski,” answered the chief counselor. “Unless, indeed, you order the executioner to cut her head off.”

“That would be wrong,” said the king. “The woman must not be harmed. And it is right that we return her money. I will not marry her in any case.”

The counselor went to look for Mary Ann. She heard she was not to become a queen. She would get her money back instead. She flew into a wild anger. She hit the chief counselor on the ears so hard that they hurt for nearly an hour. But she followed him into the king’s audience room. There she demanded her money in a loud voice. “Here,” answered the king. He handed the counselor the leather purse. “It is all in silver quarters. They must be taken from the purse one at a time. But there will be plenty to pay what you ask, and more.”

So the counselor sat down on the floor in one corner, because there were no chairs. He began counting out silver twenty-five-cent pieces from the purse, one by one. And the old woman sat on the floor opposite him. She took each piece of money from his hand.

It was a large sum: three million, nine hundred thousand, six hundred and twenty-four dollars and sixteen cents. And it takes four times as many twenty-five-cent pieces as dollars to make up the amount.

He interrupted him long enough to get from the purse what money he needed to rule in a proper and grand manner. This delayed the counting somewhat. THE GIRL WHO OWNED A BEAR

Mamma had gone downtown to shop. She had asked Nora to look after Jane Gladys, and Nora promised she would. But it was her afternoon for polishing the silver. So she stayed in the pantry and left Jane Gladys to amuse herself alone in the big sitting-room upstairs.

The little girl did not mind being alone. She was working on her first piece of embroidery—a sofa pillow for papa’s birthday present. So she crept into the big bay window and curled herself up on the broad sill. She bent her brown head over her work.

Soon the door opened and closed again, quietly. Jane Gladys thought it was Nora, so she did not look up until she had taken a couple more stitches on a forget-me-not. Then she raised her eyes. She was astonished to find a strange man in the middle of the room. He looked at her seriously.

He was short and fat. He seemed to be breathing heavily from his climb up the stairs. He held a silk hat in one hand. Under his other elbow was a good-sized book. He wore a black suit that looked old and rather worn. His head was bald on top.

“Excuse me,” he said, while the child looked at him in serious surprise. “Are you Jane Gladys Brown?”

“Yes, sir,” she answered.

“Very good; very good, indeed!” he said, with a strange sort of smile. “I had quite a hunt to find you, but I succeeded at last.”

“How did you get in?” asked Jane Gladys. She began to distrust her visitor.

“That is a secret,” he said, mysteriously.

This was enough to put the girl on her guard. She looked at the man and the man looked at her. Both looks were serious and somewhat worried.

“What do you want?” she asked. She straightened herself up with a grand air.

“Ah!—now we are coming to business,” said the man, quickly. “I am going to be quite frank with you. To begin with, your father has treated me badly in a most rude manner.”

Jane Gladys got off the window sill and pointed her small finger at the door.

“Leave this room at once!” she cried. Her voice trembled with anger. “My papa is the best man in the world. He never treated anybody badly!”

“Allow me to explain, please,” said the visitor. He paid no attention to her request to go away. “Your father may be very kind to you, for you are his little girl, you know. But when he is downtown in his office he is inclined to be rather severe, especially on book agents. Now, I called on him the other day and asked him to buy the ‘Complete Works of Peter Smith.’ And what do you suppose he did?”

She said nothing.

“Why,” continued the man, with growing excitement, “he ordered me from his office. He had me put out of the building by the janitor! What do you think of such treatment as that from the ‘best papa in the world,’ eh?”

“I think he was quite right,” said Jane Gladys.

“Oh, you do? Well,” said the man, “I resolved to be revenged for the insult. So, as your father is big and strong and a dangerous man, I have decided to be revenged upon his little girl.”

Jane Gladys shivered.

“What are you going to do?” she asked.

“I am going to present you with this book,” he answered. He took it from under his arm. Then he sat down on the edge of a chair. He placed his hat on the rug and drew a fountain pen from his vest pocket.

“I will write your name in it,” said he. “How do you spell Gladys?”

“G-l-a-d-y-s,” she replied.

"Thank you. Now this," he said. He stood up and handed her the book with a bow. "This is my revenge for the way your father treated me. Perhaps he will be sorry he did not buy the 'Complete Works of Peter Smith.' Good-by, my dear."

He walked to the door. He gave her another bow and left the room. Jane Gladys could see that he was laughing to himself. He seemed very pleased.

When the door had closed behind the strange little man, the child sat down in the window again. She looked at the book. It had a red and yellow cover. The word "Thingamajigs" was across the front in big letters.

"He was a funny little man," she said to herself. She thought about it for a moment.

She turned the next page. She saw a big picture of a clown. He was dressed in green and red and yellow. His face was very white, with three-cornered spots of red on each cheek and over the eyes. He stretched his arms and legs and yawned in a rather rude way. Then he gave a silly little laugh and said:

"This is better! You do not know how tight one gets, standing so long on a flat page of paper."

Perhaps you can imagine how surprised Jane Gladys was. She stared at the clown who had just jumped out of the book.

THE ENCHANTED TYPES

One time a knook became tired of his beautiful life. He wanted something new to do. The knooks have more wonderful powers than any other immortal folk—except perhaps the fairies and ryls. So one would think that a knook who could gain anything he wished by a simple wish could not be anything but happy and content. But that was not true of Popopo, the knook we are speaking of. He had lived thousands of years. He had enjoyed all the wonders he could think of. Yet life had become as tedious to him now as it might be to one who could not gratify a single wish.

Finally, by chance, Popopo thought of the earth people who dwell in cities. So he decided to visit them and see how they lived. This would surely be fine amusement. It would help pass away many wearisome hours.

So one morning, after a breakfast so dainty that you could scarcely imagine it, Popopo set out for the earth. At once he was in the middle of a big city.

His own dwelling was so quiet and peaceful that the loud noise of the town startled him. His nerves were so shocked that before he had looked around three minutes he decided to give up the adventure. He returned home at once.

This satisfied his wish to visit the earth cities for a time. But soon the monotony of his existence again made him restless. It gave him another thought. At night the people slept, and the cities would be quiet. He would visit them at night.

So at the proper time Popopo moved himself in a jiffy to a great city. He began wandering about the streets. Everyone was in bed. No wagons rattled along the pavements. No crowds of busy men shouted and called out. Even the policemen slept quietly, and there happened to be no thieves creeping about.

His nerves were calmed by the stillness, and Popopo began to enjoy himself. He entered many of the houses and examined their rooms with much curiosity. Locks and bolts made no difference to a knook. He saw as well in darkness as in daylight.

After a time he walked into the business part of the city. Stores are unknown among the immortals. They have no need of money or of trade and exchange. So Popopo was greatly interested by the new sight of so many collections of goods and merchandise.

During his wanderings he entered a millinery shop. He was surprised to see a large glass case full of women’s hats. Each hat had a stuffed bird in one position or another. Indeed, some of the most elaborate hats had two or three birds on them.

Now knooks are the special guardians of birds, and they love them dearly. To see so many of his little friends shut up in a glass case annoyed and grieved Popopo. He had no idea they had been placed on the hats on purpose by the milliner. So he slid back one of the doors of the case. He gave the little chirruping whistle of the knooks that all birds know well, and called:

“Come, friends; the door is open—fly out!”

Popopo did not know the birds were stuffed. But stuffed or not, every bird must obey a knook's whistle and a knook's call. So they left the hats, flew out of the case and began to move their wings about the room.

"Poor dears!" said the kind knook, "you want to be in the fields and forests again."

Then he opened the outer door for them and cried: "Go on! Fly away, my beauties, and be happy again."

The surprised birds at once obeyed. When they had flown away into the night air the knook closed the door and went on walking through the streets.

By dawn he saw many interesting sights. But day broke before he had finished the city, and he decided to come the next evening a few hours earlier.

As soon as it was dark the following day he came again to the city. On passing the millinery shop he noticed a light inside. He entered and found two women. One leaned her head upon the table and cried bitterly. The other tried to comfort her.

Of course Popopo was invisible to human eyes, so he stood by and listened to their talk.

"Cheer up, sister," said one. "Even though your pretty birds have all been stolen, the hats themselves remain."

"Alas!" cried the other, who was the milliner, "no one will buy my hats partly trimmed, for the fashion is to wear birds upon them. And if I cannot sell my goods I shall be completely ruined."

Then she cried again and the knook went quietly away. He felt a little ashamed. He realized that in his love for the birds he had unknowingly wronged one of the earth people and made her unhappy.

This thought brought him back to the millinery shop later in the night, when the two women had gone home. "Here are just the creatures," thought Popopo, "to place upon the woman's hats. Their fur is almost as soft as the plumage of the birds, and it strikes me the mice are remarkably pretty and graceful animals. So he used a charm that drew all the mice from the cellar and placed them upon the hats in the glass case. There they took the places the birds had left and looked very becoming—at least, in the eyes of the unworldly knook. To stop them running about and leaving the hats Popopo made them motionless. She came in the early morning, with her sister, and her face wore a sad and accepting look. After sweeping and dusting the shop and drawing the blinds she opened the glass case and took out a hat.

"What is it? Oh! what is it?"

"A mouse!" gasped the milliner, trembling with terror.

Popopo, seeing this commotion, now realized that mice are especially disagreeable to human beings. He saw that he had made a grave mistake in placing them upon the hats. So he gave a low whistle of command that was heard only by the mice.

Instantly they all jumped from the hats, dashed out the open door of the glass case and ran away to their cellar. But this action so frightened the milliner and her sister that after giving several loud screams they fell upon their backs on the floor and fainted away.

"Thank you, Popopo. Thank you for setting us free."

"Because I find the woman considers you her property, and your loss has caused her much unhappiness," answered Popopo.

"But remember how unhappy we were in her glass case," said a robin redbreast, gravely. "And as for being her property, you are a knook, and the natural guardian of all birds. So you know that Nature created us free. To be sure, wicked men shot and stuffed us, and sold us to the milliner. But the idea of our being her property is nonsense!"

Popopo was puzzled.

"If I leave you free," he said, "wicked men will shoot you again, and you will be no better off than before."

"Pooh!" exclaimed the blue jay, "we cannot be shot now, for we are stuffed. Indeed, two men fired several shots at us this morning, but the bullets only ruffled our feathers and buried themselves in our stuffing. We do not fear men now."

"Listen!" said Popopo, sternly, for he felt the birds were getting the best of the argument; "the poor milliner's business will be ruined if I do not return you to her shop. It seems you are necessary to trim the hats properly. It is the fashion for women to wear birds upon their headgear. "Fashions," said a black bird, solemnly, "are made by men. What law is there, among birds or knooks, that requires us to be the slaves of fashion?"

"What have we to do with fashions, anyway?" screamed a linnet. "If it were the fashion to wear knooks perched upon women's hats would you be contented to stay there? Answer me, Popopo!"

But Popopo was very sad. He could not do wrong to the birds by sending them back to the milliner. He also did not want the milliner to lose them. So he went home to think about what to do.

After much deep thought, he decided to ask the king of the knooks for advice. He went at once to the king and told him the whole story.

The king frowned.

“This should teach you not to interfere with earth people,” he said. “But since you caused all this trouble, it is your duty to fix it. Our birds cannot be slaves. That is certain. So you must change the fashions. Women must stop wearing birds on their hats.”

“How shall I do that?” asked Popopo.

“Easily enough. Fashions often change among earth people. They get tired quickly of any one thing. When they read in their newspapers and magazines that the style is such-and-such, they never question it. They just obey the command of fashion at once. So you must visit the newspapers and magazines and put a spell on the type.”

“Put a spell on the type!” Popopo repeated, in wonder.

“Just so. Make them read that it is no longer the fashion to wear birds on hats. That will bring relief to your poor milliner. At the same time it will set free thousands of our dear birds who have been so badly treated.”

Popopo thanked the wise king and followed his advice.

The knook visited the office of every newspaper and magazine in the city. Then he went to other cities. Soon there was not a publication in the land that did not have a “new fashion note” in its pages. Sometimes Popopo put a spell on the type, so that whoever read the print would see only what the knook wished them to see. Sometimes he visited the busy editors and confused their brains until they wrote exactly what he wanted. Mortals seldom know how greatly they are influenced by fairies, knooks and ryls. These beings often put thoughts into their heads that only the wise little immortals could have conceived.

The following morning the poor milliner looked over her newspaper. She was overjoyed to read that “no woman could now wear a bird upon her hat and be in style, for the newest fashion required only ribbons and laces.”

After this, Popopo found much enjoyment in visiting every millinery shop he could find. He gave new life to the stuffed birds that had been carelessly thrown aside as useless. And they flew to the fields and forests with songs of thanks to the good knook who had rescued them.

Sometimes a hunter fires his gun at a bird and then wonders why he did not hit it. But, having read this story, you will understand that the bird must have been a stuffed one from some millinery shop. Of course, a gun cannot kill a stuffed bird.

THE LAUGHING HIPPOPOTAMUS

On one of the upper branches of the Congo river lived an ancient and aristocratic family of hippopotamuses. They boasted a pedigree dating back beyond the days of Noah—beyond the existence of mankind—far into the dim ages when the world was new.

They had always lived upon the banks of this same river. Every curve and sweep of its waters, every pit and shallow of its bed, every rock and stump and wallow upon its bank was as familiar to them as their own mothers. And they are living there yet, I suppose.

Not long ago the queen of this tribe of hippopotamuses had a child. She named him Keo, because he was so fat and round. Still, so that you may not be misled, I will say that in the hippopotamus language “Keo,” properly translated, means “fat and lazy” instead of fat and round. However, no one called the queen’s attention to this error. Her tusks were monstrous long and sharp, and she thought Keo the sweetest baby in the world.

He was, indeed, all right for a hippopotamus. He rolled and played in the soft mud of the river bank. He waddled inland to nibble the leaves of the wild cabbage that grew there. He was happy and contented from morning till night. And he was the jolliest hippopotamus that ancient family had ever known. His little red eyes were forever twinkling with fun. He laughed his merry laugh on all occasions, whether there was anything to laugh at or not.

Therefore the black people who dwelt in that region called him “Ippi”—the jolly one. They dared not come near him, though, because of his fierce mother. His equally fierce uncles and aunts and cousins lived in a vast colony upon the river bank.

These black people lived in little villages scattered among the trees. They dared not openly attack the royal family of hippopotamuses. But they were amazingly fond of eating hippopotamus meat whenever they could get it. This was no secret to the hippopotamuses. And when the blacks managed to catch these animals alive, they had a trick of riding them through the jungles as if they were horses. This reduced the animals to a condition of slavery.

Therefore, having these things in mind, the tribe of hippopotamuses smelled the oily odor of black people. Then they would charge upon them furiously. If by chance they overtook one of the enemy, they would rip him with their sharp tusks or stamp him into the earth with their huge feet.

It was continual warfare between the hippopotamuses and the black people.

Gouie lived in one of the little villages of the blacks. He was the son of the chief’s brother and grandson of the village sorcerer. The sorcerer was an aged man known as “the boneless wonder.” He could twist himself into as many coils as a serpent. He had no bones to hinder his bending his flesh into any position. This made him walk in a wabbly fashion, but the black people had great respect for him.

Gouie’s hut was made of branches of trees stuck together with mud. His clothing was a grass mat tied around his middle. But his relationship to the chief and the sorcerer gave him a certain dignity. He was much addicted to solitary thought. Perhaps it was natural that these thoughts frequently turned upon his enemies, the hippopotamuses. And it was natural that he should consider many ways of capturing them.

Finally he completed his plans. He set about digging a great pit in the ground, midway between two sharp curves of the river. When the pit was finished, he covered it over with small branches of trees. He strewed earth upon them, smoothing the surface so artfully that no one would suspect there was a big hole underneath. Then Gouie laughed softly to himself and went home to supper.

That evening the queen said to Keo, who was growing to be a fine child for his age:

“I wish you would run across the bend and ask your Uncle Nikki to come here. I have found a strange plant, and want him to tell me if it is good to eat.”

The happy one laughed loudly as he started on his errand. He felt as important as a boy does when he is sent for the first time to the corner store to buy a small cake.

“Guk-uk-uk-uk! guk-uk-uk-uk!” was the way he laughed. If you think a hippopotamus does not laugh this way, you only have to listen to one and you will find I am right.

He crawled out of the mud where he was lying and walked away through the low plants. The last his mother heard as she lay half in and half out of the water was his musical “guk-uk-uk-uk!” dying away in the distance.

Keo was in such a happy mood that he hardly noticed where he stepped. So he was much surprised when, in the middle of a laugh, the ground gave way beneath him. He fell to the bottom of Gouie’s deep pit. He was not badly hurt, but had hit his nose hard as he went down. So he stopped laughing and began to think how he should get out again. Then he found the walls were higher than his head, and that he was a prisoner.

So he laughed a little at his own bad luck, and the laughter calmed him to sleep. He snored all through the night until daylight came.

“Yes; I will have a piece of your leg, if I can reach it,” replied Keo; and then he laughed at his own joke: “Guk-uk-uk-uk!”

“Good-by, Gouie; in a year and a day you will see me again.”

The three hippopotamuses stopped before the cave. Their front feet were on the bank. Their bodies were in the water. Together they called out a greeting to Glinkomok. Right after that the mouth of the cave grew dark. The creature moved silently toward them.

The hippopotamuses were afraid to look at it. They bowed their heads between their legs.

"We come, O Glinkomok, to ask for your mercy and your friendly help!" began Uncle Nep. Then he told the story of how Keo was caught. He told how Keo had promised to return to the black man.

"He must keep his promise," said the creature. Its voice sounded like a sigh.

THE MAGIC BON BONS

There lived in Boston a wise and ancient chemist by the name of Dr. Daws, who dabbled somewhat in magic. There also lived in Boston a young lady by the name of Claribel Sudds. She had much money, little wit and an intense desire to go upon the stage.

So Claribel went to Dr. Daws and said:

“I can neither sing nor dance. I cannot recite verse nor play upon the piano. I am no acrobat nor leaper nor high kicker. Yet I wish to go upon the stage. What shall I do?”

“Are you willing to pay for such accomplishments?” asked the wise chemist.

“Certainly,” answered Claribel, jingling her purse.

“Then come to me to-morrow at two o’clock,” said he.

All that night he practiced what is known as chemical sorcery. So when Claribel Sudds came next day at two o’clock he showed her a small box filled with compounds that closely resembled French bonbons.

“This is a progressive age,” said the old man. “And I flatter myself your Uncle Daws keeps right along with the procession. Now, one of your old-fashioned sorcerers would have made you some nasty, bitter pills to swallow. But I have consulted your taste and convenience. Here are some magic bonbons. If you eat this one with the lavender color you can dance thereafter as lightly and gracefully as if you had been trained a lifetime. After you consume the pink confection you will sing like a nightingale. Eating the white one will enable you to become the finest elocutionist in the land. The chocolate piece will charm you into playing the piano better than Rubenstein. And after eating the lemon-yellow bonbon you can easily kick six feet above your head.”

“How delightful!” exclaimed Claribel, who was truly enraptured. “You are certainly a most clever sorcerer as well as a considerate compounder.” And she held out her hand for the box.

“Ahem!” said the wise one. “A check, please.”

“Oh, yes. To be sure! How stupid of me to forget it,” she returned.

He considerately retained the box in his own hand while she signed a check for a large amount of money. After that he allowed her to hold the box herself.

“Are you sure you have made them strong enough?” she inquired, anxiously. “It usually takes a great deal to affect me.”

“My only fear,” replied Dr. Daws, “is that I have made them too strong. For this is the first time I have ever been called upon to prepare these wonderful confections.”

“Don’t worry,” said Claribel. “The stronger they act the better I shall act myself.”

Bessie played on. The four older people stood in a close but silent and surprised group. They listened to the music and waited for the sound of the dinner bell.

The professor was watching him, so Mr. Bostwick held the dish toward him in a kind way. The professor ate the lemon-yellow piece. The senator reached out his hand and took the light purple piece. He did not eat it, however. He happened to remember that it might ruin his dinner, so he put it in his coat pocket. Mrs. Bostwick was still listening closely to her clever daughter. Without thinking what she did, she took the remaining piece, which was the white one, and slowly ate it.

The dish was now empty. Claribel Sudds' precious sweets had passed from her possession forever!

Suddenly Mr. Bostwick, who was a big man, began to sing in a high, shaking high voice. It was not the same song Bessie was playing. The harsh sound was so shocking that the professor smiled. The senator put his hands to his ears. Mrs. Bostwick cried in a horrified voice:

"William!"

Her husband continued to sing as if trying to copy the famous Christine Nillson. He paid no attention at all to his wife or his guests.

Fortunately the dinner bell now sounded. Mrs. Bostwick pulled Bessie from the piano and led her guests into the dining room. Mr. Bostwick followed, singing "The Last Rose of Summer" as if it had been an extra song demanded by a thousand delighted listeners.

The poor woman was in despair at seeing her husband's shameful actions. She wondered what she might do to control him. The professor seemed more serious than usual. The senator's face wore an offended expression. Bessie kept moving her fingers as if she still wanted to play the piano.

Mrs. Bostwick managed to get them all seated, although her husband had broken into another song. Then the servant brought in the soup.

When she carried a plate to the professor, he cried in an excited voice:

"Hold it higher! Higher—I say!" And springing up, he gave it a sudden kick that sent it nearly to the ceiling. From there the dish descended to scatter soup over Bessie and the servant and to break in pieces upon the crown of the professor's head with no hair.

At this terrible act the senator rose from his seat with a cry of horror and glanced at his hostess.

For some time Mrs. Bostwick had been staring straight ahead with a confused expression. But now, catching the senator's eye, she bowed gracefully and began saying "The Charge of the Light Brigade" in strong tones.

The senator trembled. Such shameful wild behavior he had never seen nor heard before in a decent private family. He felt that his reputation was at stake. He was apparently the only normal person in the room, and there was no one to whom he might appeal.

The servant had run away to cry wildly in the kitchen. Mr. Bostwick was singing "O Promise Me." The professor was trying to kick the glass balls off the hanging light. Mrs. Bostwick had switched her speaking to "The Boy Stood on the Burning Deck." Bessie had stolen into the sitting room and was pounding out the opening music from the "Flying Dutchman."

The senator was not at all sure he would not go crazy himself soon. So he slipped away from the confusion. Catching up his hat and coat in the hall, he hurried from the house.

That night he sat up late writing a political speech he was to deliver the next afternoon at Faneuil hall. But his experiences at the Bostwicks' had so shaken him that he could hardly collect his thoughts. Often he would pause and shake his head with pity as he remembered the strange things he had seen in that usually proper home.

The next day he met Mr. Bostwick in the street, but passed him by with a hard, cold look of no recognition. He felt he really could not afford to know this gentleman in the future. Mr. Bostwick was naturally angry at the direct insult. Yet in his mind lingered a faint memory of some quite unusual events at his dinner party the evening before. He hardly knew whether he dared resent the senator's treatment or not.

The political meeting was the main event of the day, for the senator's speaking skill was well known in Boston. So the big hall was crowded with people. In one of the front rows sat the Bostwick family, with the learned Yale professor beside them. They all looked tired and pale, as if they had passed a rather wild evening. The senator was made so nervous by seeing them that he refused to look in their direction a second time.

While the mayor was introducing him, the great man sat moving nervously in his chair. He happened to put his thumb and finger into his coat pocket and found the light purple sweet he had placed there the evening before.

"This may clear my throat," thought the senator, and slipped the sweet into his mouth.

A few minutes afterwards he arose before the vast audience, which greeted him with loud applause.

"My friends," began the senator in a serious voice, "this is a most impressive and important occasion."

Then he paused, balanced himself upon his left foot, and kicked his right leg into the air in the way favored by ballet-dancers!

There was a hum of amazement and horror from the crowd, but the senator appeared not to notice it. He whirled around upon the tips of his toes, kicked right and left in a graceful manner, and startled a man with no hair in the front row by casting a longing glance in his direction.

Suddenly Claribel Sudds, who happened to be present, uttered a scream and sprang to her feet. Pointing an accusing finger at the dancing senator, she cried in a loud voice:

"That's the man who stole my sweets! Seize him! Arrest him! Don't let him escape!"

But the ushers rushed her out of the hall, thinking she had gone suddenly insane. The senator's friends seized him firmly and carried him out the stage entrance to the street. There they put him into an open carriage and instructed the driver to take him home.

The effect of the magic sweet was still powerful enough to control the poor senator. He stood upon the rear seat of the carriage and danced energetically all the way home. This delighted the crowd of small boys who followed the carriage. It grieved the sober-minded citizens, who shook their heads sadly and whispered that "another good man had gone wrong."

It took the senator several months to get over the shame and embarrassment of this wild adventure. Strangely enough, he never had the smallest idea what had made him act in such a strange way. Perhaps it was lucky that the last sweet had now been eaten. They might easily have caused much more trouble than they did.

Of course Claribel went again to the wise chemist and signed a check for another box of magic sweets. But she must have taken better care of these, because she is now a famous vaudeville actress.

This story should teach us the foolishness of judging others for actions we do not understand. We never know what may happen to ourselves. It may also serve as a warning to be careful about leaving packages in public places. And, by the way, to leave other people's packages completely alone.

THE CAPTURE OF FATHER TIME

Jim was the son of a cowboy, and lived on the wide plains of Arizona. His father had trained him to throw a rope around a wild horse or a young bull with perfect accuracy. If Jim had had the strength to match his skill, he would have been as good a cowboy as any in all Arizona.

When he was twelve years old he made his first visit to the east, where Uncle Charles, his father's brother, lived. Of course Jim took his rope with him, because he was proud of his skill in throwing it. He wanted to show his cousins what a cowboy could do.

At first the city boys and girls were very interested in watching Jim throw his rope around posts and fence stakes. But they soon got tired of it, and even Jim decided it was not the right kind of sport for cities.

But one day the butcher asked Jim to ride one of his horses into the country, to a field that had been rented, and Jim gladly agreed. He had been longing for a horse ride, and to make it seem like old times he took his rope with him.

He rode through the streets quietly enough. But on reaching the open country roads his spirits broke out into wild joy. He urged the butcher's horse to a full gallop and dashed away in true cowboy fashion.

Then he wanted still more freedom. He let down the bars that led into a big field and began riding over the meadow. He threw his rope at imaginary cattle, while he yelled and shouted to his heart's content.

Suddenly, on making a long throw with his rope, the loop caught on something and rested about three feet from the ground. The rope pulled tight and nearly pulled Jim from his horse.

This was unexpected. More than that, it was wonderful, because the field seemed bare of even a tree stump. Jim's eyes grew big with surprise. But he knew he had caught something when a voice cried out:

"Here, let go! Let go, I say! Can't you see what you have done?"

No, Jim could not see, and he did not intend to let go until he found out what was holding the loop of his rope. So he used an old trick his father had taught him. He put the butcher's horse to a run and began riding in a circle around the spot where his rope had caught.

As he drew nearer and nearer his target he saw the rope coil up. Yet it looked like it was coiling over nothing but air. One end of the rope was tied to a ring in the saddle. When the rope was almost wound up and the horse began to pull away and snort with fear, Jim got off. Holding the reins of the bridle in one hand, he followed the rope. A moment later he saw an old man caught fast in the coils of the rope.

His head was bald and uncovered, but long white whiskers grew down to his waist. About his body was thrown a loose robe of fine white linen. In one hand he held a great scythe, and beneath the other arm he carried an hourglass.

While Jim looked at him in wonder, this venerable old man spoke in an angry voice:

"Now, then—get that rope off as fast as you can! You have brought everything on earth to a stop by your foolishness! Well—what are you staring at? Don't you know who I am?"

"No," said Jim, stupidly.

"Well, I'm Time—Father Time! Now, hurry and set me free—if you want the world to run properly."

"How did I happen to catch you?" asked Jim, without making a move to release his captive.

"I don't know. I have never been caught before," growled Father Time. "But I suppose it was because you were foolishly throwing your rope at nothing."

"I didn't see you," said Jim.

"Of course you didn't. I'm invisible to the eyes of human beings unless they get within three feet of me. And I take care to keep more than that distance away from them. That's why I was crossing this field, where I supposed no one would be. And I should have been perfectly safe had it not been for your beastly rope. Now, then," he added, crossly, "are you going to get that rope off?"

"Why should I?" asked Jim.

"Because everything in the world stopped moving the moment you caught me. I don't suppose you want to make an end of all business and pleasure, and war and love, and misery and ambition and everything else, do you? Not a watch has ticked since you tied me up here like a mummy!"

Jim laughed. It really was funny to see the old man wound round and round with coils of rope from his knees up to his chin.

"It'll do you good to rest," said the boy. "From all I've heard you lead a rather busy life."

"Indeed I do," replied Father Time, with a sigh. "I'm due in Kamchatka this very minute. And to think one small boy is upsetting all my regular habits!"

"Too bad!" said Jim, with a grin. "But since the world has stopped anyhow, it won't matter if it takes a little longer rest. As soon as I let you go Time will fly again. Where are your wings?"

"I haven't any," answered the old man. "That is a story made up by someone who never saw me. As a matter of fact, I move rather slowly."

"I see, you take your time," remarked the boy. "What do you use that scythe for?"

"To cut down the people," said the ancient one. "Every time I swing my scythe someone dies."

"Then I ought to win a life-saving medal by keeping you tied up," said Jim. "Some folks will live this much longer."

"But they won't know it," said Father Time, with a sad smile; "so it will do them no good. You may as well untie me at once."

"No," said Jim, with a determined air. "I may never capture you again; so I'll hold you for a while and see how the world goes without you."

Then he lifted the old man, still tied up, onto the back of the horse that belonged to the man who sold meat. He got into the seat himself and started back toward town. One hand held his prisoner, and the other guided the straps.

When he reached the road, his eye fell on a strange scene. A horse and carriage stood in the middle of the road. The horse was in the act of running, with his head held high and two legs in the air, but perfectly still. In the carriage a man and a woman were seated. But if they had been turned into stone, they could not have been more still and stiff.

"There's no Time for them!" sighed the old man. "Won't you let me go now?"

"Not yet," replied the boy.

He rode on until he reached the city. There all the people stood in exactly the same positions they were in when Jim caught Father Time with his rope. Stopping in front of a big store that sold cloth and clothes, the boy tied his horse and went in. The clerks were measuring out goods and showing patterns to the rows of customers in front of them. But everyone seemed suddenly to have become a statue.

There was something very bad in this scene. A cold shake began to run up and down Jim's back, so he hurried out again.

On the edge of the sidewalk sat a poor, disabled man who lived by asking for money, holding out his hat. Beside him stood a rich-looking old man who was about to drop a small coin into the hat. Jim knew this old man to be very rich but rather ungenerous. So he dared to put his hand into the man's pocket and take out his money bag, in which was a $20 gold piece. This shining coin he put in the old man's fingers instead of the small coin. Then he put the money bag back into the rich man's pocket.

"That gift will surprise him when he comes to life," thought the boy.

He got on the horse again and rode up the street. As he passed the shop of his friend, the man who sold meat, he noticed several pieces of meat hanging outside.

"I'm afraid that meat will go bad," he remarked.

"It takes Time to make meat go bad," answered the old man.

This struck Jim as being strange, but true.

"It seems Time interferes with everything," said he.

"Yes; you have made a prisoner of the most important person in the world," moaned the old man. "And you haven't enough sense to let him go again."

Jim did not reply. Soon they came to the house of his father's brother, where he got off the horse again. The street was filled with teams and people, but all were without movement. His two little cousins were just coming out the gate on their way to school, with their books and writing boards under their arms. So Jim had to jump over the fence to avoid knocking them down.

In the front room sat his aunt, reading her Bible. She was just turning a page when Time stopped. In the dining-room was his father's brother, finishing his lunch. His mouth was open and his fork held just before it, while his eyes were fixed upon the newspaper folded beside him. Jim helped himself to his relative's pie, and while he ate it he walked out to his prisoner.

"There's one thing I don't understand," said he.

"What's that?" asked Father Time.

"Why is it that I'm able to move around while everyone else is—is—frozen?"

"That is because I'm your prisoner," answered the other. "You can do anything you wish with Time now. But unless you are careful you will do something you will be sorry for."

Jim threw the hard edge of his pie at a bird that was hanging in the air, where it had been flying when Time stopped.

"Anyway," he laughed, "I'm living longer than anyone else. No one will ever be able to catch up with me again."

"Each life has its fixed length," said the old man. "When you have lived your proper time my long cutting blade will cut you down."

"I forgot your long cutting blade," said Jim, thinking carefully.

Then a spirit of fun came into the boy's head. He happened to think that the present chance to have fun would never come again. He tied Father Time to the post where his relative tied horses, so that he might not escape. Then he crossed the road to the corner food shop.

The food shop owner had told Jim off that very morning for stepping into a basket of root vegetables by accident. So the boy went to the back end of the shop and turned on the tap of the sweet syrup barrel.

"That will make a nice mess when Time starts the syrup running all over the floor," said Jim, with a laugh.

A little further down the street was a hair-cutting shop. Sitting in the hair-cutter's chair, Jim saw the man that all the boys declared was the "meanest man in town." He certainly did not like the boys, and the boys knew it. The hair-cutter was in the act of washing this person's hair when Time was captured. Jim ran to the medicine shop and got a bottle of glue. He returned and poured it over the untidy hair of the unpopular citizen.

"That will probably surprise him when he wakes up," thought Jim.

Near by was the schoolhouse. Jim entered it and found that only a few of the pupils were gathered. But the teacher sat at his desk, stern and frowning as usual.

Taking a piece of chalk, Jim marked upon the blackboard in big letters the following words:

"Every scholar is requested to yell the minute he enters the room. He will also please throw his books at the teacher's head. Signed, Prof. Sharpe."

"That ought to raise a nice disturbance," murmured the trouble-maker, as he walked away.

On the corner stood Policeman Mulligan, talking with old Miss Scrapple, the worst talker about others in town. She always took delight in saying something unkind about her neighbors. Jim thought this chance was too good to lose. So he took off the policeman's cap and coat with brass buttons and put them on Miss Scrapple. The lady's hat with feathers and ribbons he placed playfully upon the policeman's head.

The effect was so funny that the boy laughed aloud. A good many people were standing near the corner, and Jim decided that Miss Scrapple and Officer Mulligan would cause a shock when Time started upon his travels.

Then the young cowboy remembered his prisoner. He walked back to the tying post and came within three feet of it. He saw Father Time still standing patiently within the ropes. He looked angry and annoyed, however, and growled out:

"Well, when do you intend to release me?"

"I've been thinking about that ugly long cutting blade of yours," said Jim.

"What about it?" asked Father Time.

"Perhaps if I let you go you'll swing it at me the first thing, to be revenged," replied the boy.

THE WONDERFUL PUMP

Not many years ago there lived on a stony, poor New England farm a man and his wife. They were serious, honest people. They worked hard from early morning until dark to secure a small living from their poor land.

Their house was a small, one-storied building. It stood upon the side of a steep hill, and the stones lay so thickly about it that almost nothing green could grow from the ground. At the foot of the hill, a quarter of a mile from the house by the winding path, was a small stream. The woman was forced to go there for water and to carry it up the hill to the house. This was a tiring task. With the other hard work that fell to her share, it had made her thin and bent and lean.

Yet she never complained. She quietly and faithfully performed her duties. She did the housework, carried the water and helped her husband hoe the small crop that grew upon the best part of their land.

One day she walked down the path to the stream. Her big shoes scattered the pebbles right and left. She noticed a large beetle lying upon its back. It was struggling hard with its little legs to turn over, so that its feet might again touch the ground. But this it could not do. The woman had a kind heart, so she reached down and gently turned the beetle with her finger. At once it ran from the path and she went on to the stream.

The next day, as she came for water, she was surprised to see the beetle again lying upon its back and struggling helplessly to turn. Once more the woman stopped and set him upon his feet. Then, as she stooped over the tiny creature, she heard a small voice say:

“Oh, thank you! Thank you so much for saving me!”

The woman started back, half frightened at hearing a beetle speak in her own language. She exclaimed:

“Good heavens! Surely you can’t talk like humans!” Then she recovered from her alarm and again bent over the beetle, who answered her:

“Why shouldn’t I talk, if I have anything to say?”

“Because you are a bug,” replied the woman.

“That is true. And you saved my life—saved me from my enemies, the sparrows. And this is the second time you have come to my assistance, so I owe you a debt of gratitude. Bugs value their lives as much as human beings. And I am a more important creature than you, in your ignorance, may suppose. But tell me, why do you come each day to the stream?”

“For water,” she answered, staring stupidly down at the talking beetle.

“Isn’t it hard work?” the creature inquired.

“Yes, but there is no water on the hill,” said she.

“Then dig a well and put a pump in it,” replied the beetle.

She shook her head.

“My man tried it once, but there was no water,” she said, sadly.

“Try it again,” commanded the beetle. “And in return for your kindness to me I will make this promise: if you do not get water from the well you will get that which is more precious to you. I must go now. Do not forget. Dig a well.”

And then, without pausing to say good-bye, it ran swiftly away and was lost among the stones.

The woman returned to the house much confused by what the beetle had said. When her husband came in from his work she told him the whole story.

The poor man thought deeply for a time, and then declared:

“Wife, there may be some truth in what the bug told you. There must be magic in the world if a bug can speak. And if magic is real, we may get water from the well. I bought a pump to use in the well, but the well was dry. The pump is now lying in the barn. The only cost of following the talking bug’s advice will be the work of digging the hole. I am used to work. So I will dig the well.”

The next day he started to dig. He dug so far down that he could hardly reach the top to climb out again. But he did not find a drop of water.

“Perhaps you did not dig deep enough,” his wife said when he told her about his failure.

So the next day he made a long ladder and put it into the hole. Then he dug and dug and dug until the top of the ladder barely reached the top of the hole. But still there was no water.

The next time the woman went to the small river with her bucket, she saw the bug sitting on a stone beside her path. So she stopped and said:

“My husband has dug the well. But there is no water.”

“Did he put the pump in the well?” asked the bug.

“No,” she answered.

“Then do as I told you. Put in the pump. If you do not get water, I promise you something even more valuable.”

After saying this, the bug quickly slid from the stone and disappeared. The woman went back to the house and told her husband what the bug had said.

“Well,” replied the simple man, “there can be no harm in trying.”

So he got the pump from the barn and placed it in the well. Then he took hold of the handle and began to pump, while his wife stood by to watch what would happen.

No water came. But after a few moments, a gold piece dropped from the pump’s spout. Then another, and another, until several handfuls of gold lay in a small pile on the ground.

The man stopped pumping then and ran to help his wife gather the gold pieces into her apron. But their hands shook so much with excitement and joy that they could hardly pick up the shining coins.

At last she gathered them close to her chest. Together they ran to the house. There they emptied the precious gold onto the table and counted the pieces.

All were stamped with the design of the United States mint and were worth five dollars each. Some were worn and a little discolored from use. Others looked bright and new, as if they had not been handled much. When they added up the value of the pieces, they found they were worth three hundred dollars.

Suddenly the woman spoke.

“Husband, the bug said truly when he declared we should get something more precious than water from the well. But run at once and take the handle off the pump. Someone might pass this way and discover our secret.”

So the man ran to the pump and removed the handle. He carried it to the house and hid it under the bed.

They hardly slept at all that night. They lay awake thinking of their good fortune and what they should do with their store of yellow gold. In all their former lives they had never had more than a few dollars at a time. Now the cracked teapot was nearly full of gold coins.

The next day was Sunday. They got up early and ran to see if their treasure was safe. There it lay, piled closely inside the teapot. They were so eager to look at it that it was a long time before the man could leave it to build the fire, or the woman to cook the breakfast.

While they ate their simple meal, the woman said:

“We will go to church today and give thanks for the riches that have come to us so suddenly. And I will give the pastor one of the gold pieces.”

“It is well enough to go to church,” replied her husband, “and also to give thanks. But in the night I decided how we will spend all our money. So there will be none left for the pastor.”

“We can pump more,” said the woman.

“Perhaps. And perhaps not,” he answered, carefully. “We can depend on what we have. But I cannot say whether there is more in the well.”

“Then go and find out,” she returned. “For I am eager to give something to the pastor. He is a poor man and deserves it.”

So the man got the pump handle from under the bed and went to the pump. He fitted it in place. Then he set a large wooden bucket under the spout and began to pump. To their joy, the gold pieces soon began flowing into the bucket. Seeing it was about to run over the top, the woman brought another bucket. But now the stream suddenly stopped. The man said cheerfully:

“That is enough for today, good wife! We have added greatly to our treasure, and the pastor shall have his gold piece. Indeed, I think I shall also put a coin into the contribution box.”

Then, because the teapot would hold no more gold, the farmer emptied the bucket into the wood-box. He covered the money with dried leaves and small branches, so that no one might suspect what lay underneath.

Afterward they dressed in their best clothing and started for the church. Each took a bright gold piece from the teapot as a gift to the pastor.

They walked over the hill and down into the valley beyond. They felt so happy and light-hearted that they did not mind the distance at all. At last they came to the little country church and entered just as the service began.

They were proud of their wealth and of the gifts they had brought for the pastor. They could hardly wait for the moment when the deacon passed the contribution box. But at last the time came. The farmer held his hand high over the box and dropped the gold piece so that all the congregation could see what he had given. The woman did the same. She felt important and happy to be able to give the good pastor so much.

The pastor, watching from the pulpit, saw the gold drop into the box. He could hardly believe that his eyes did not deceive him. However, when the box was laid upon his desk, there were the two gold pieces. He was so surprised that he nearly forgot his sermon.

When the people were leaving the church at the end of the service, the good man stopped the farmer and his wife and asked:

“Where did you get so much gold?”

The woman was happy to tell him how she had saved the beetle. She also told him how, in return, they had been given the wonderful pump. The pastor listened to it all seriously. When the story was finished he said:

"According to tradition, strange things happened in this world long ago. Now I find that strange things may also happen today. For by your tale you have found a beetle that can speak. It also has power to give you great wealth." Then he looked carefully at the gold pieces and continued: "Either this money is fairy gold or it is real metal, stamped at the mint of the United States government. If it is fairy gold it will disappear within 24 hours. Then it will do no one any good. If it is real money, then your beetle must have robbed someone of the gold and placed it in your well. For all money belongs to someone. If you have not earned it honestly, but have come by it in the mysterious way you mention, it was surely taken from the persons who owned it, without their consent. Where else could real money come from?"

The farmer and his wife were confused by this statement. They looked guiltily at each other, for they were honest people and wished to wrong no one.

"Then you think the beetle stole the money?" asked the woman.

"By his magic powers he probably took it from its rightful owners. Even bugs which can speak have no consciences. They cannot tell the difference between right and wrong. The beetle wanted to reward you for your kindness. So he took from its lawful possessors the money you pumped from the well."

"Perhaps it really is fairy gold," suggested the man. "If so, we must go to the town and spend the money before it disappears."

"That would be wrong," answered the pastor. "For then the merchants would have neither money nor goods. To give them fairy gold would be to rob them."

"What, then, shall we do?" asked the poor woman. She wrung her hands with grief and disappointment.

"Go home and wait until tomorrow. If the gold is then in your possession it is real money and not fairy gold. But if it is real money you must try to restore it to its rightful owners. Take, also, these pieces which you have given me, for I cannot accept gold that is not honestly come by."

Sadly the poor people returned to their home. They were greatly disturbed by what they had heard. They passed another sleepless night. On Monday morning they arose at daylight and ran to see if the gold was still visible.

"It is real money, after all!" cried the man. "For not a single piece has disappeared."

THE DUMMY THAT LIVED

In all Fairyland there is no more mischievous a person than Tanko-Mankie the Yellow Ryl. He flew through the city one afternoon. He was quite invisible to mortal eyes, but he saw everything himself. He noticed a figure of a wax lady standing behind the big plate glass window of Mr. Floman’s department store.

The wax lady was beautifully dressed. In her stiff left hand was a card with these words:

“RARE BARGIN! This Stylish Costume (Imported from Paris) Former Price, $20, REDUCED TO ONLY $19.98.”

This impressive announcement had drawn a crowd of women shoppers before the window. They stood looking at the wax lady with critical eyes.

Tanko-Mankie laughed to himself. It was the low, gurgling little laugh that always means mischief. Then he flew close to the wax figure and breathed twice upon its forehead.

From that instant the dummy began to live. But she was so dazed and astonished at the unexpected sensation that she continued to stand stupidly staring at the women outside. She still held out the placard as before.

The ryl laughed again and flew away. Anyone but Tanko-Mankie would have remained to help the wax lady out of the troubles that were sure to overtake her. But this naughty elf thought it rare fun to turn the inexperienced lady loose in a cold and heartless world and leave her to shift for herself.

Fortunately it was almost six o’clock when the dummy first realized that she was alive. Before she had collected her new thoughts and decided what to do, a man came around and drew down all the window shades. This shut off the view from the curious shoppers.

Then the clerks and cashiers and floorwalkers and cash girls went home. The store was closed for the night, although the sweepers and scrubbers remained to clean the floors for the following day.

The window inhabited by the wax lady was boxed in, like a little room. One small door was left at the side for the window-trimmer to creep in and out of. So the scrubbers never noticed that the dummy, when left to herself, dropped the placard to the floor. She sat down upon a pile of silks to wonder who she was, where she was, and how she happened to be alive.

For you must consider, dear reader, that in spite of her size and her rich costume, in spite of her pink cheeks and fluffy yellow hair, this lady was very young. In reality, she was no older than a baby born but half an hour. All she knew of the world was contained in the glimpse she had secured of the busy street facing her window. All she knew of people lay in the actions of the group of women which had stood before her on the other side of the window pane. They had criticised the fit of her dress or remarked upon its stylish appearance.

So she had little enough to think about, and her thoughts moved somewhat slowly. Yet one thing she really decided upon. That was not to remain in the window and be insolently stared at by a lot of women who were not nearly so handsome or well dressed as herself.

By the time she reached this important conclusion, it was after midnight. But dim lights were burning in the big, deserted store. So she crept through the door of her window and walked down the long aisles. She paused now and then to look with much curiosity at the wealth of finery confronting her on every side.

When she came to the glass cases filled with trimmed hats, she remembered having seen similar creations on the heads of the women in the street. So she selected one that suited her fancy and placed it carefully upon her yellow locks. I won’t attempt to explain what instinct it was that made her glance into a near-by mirror to see if the hat was straight, but this she certainly did. It didn’t correspond with her dress very well, but the poor thing was too young to have much taste in matching colors.

When she reached the glove counter, she remembered that the women she had seen also wore gloves. She took a pair from the case and tried to fit them on her stiff fingers, which were covered with wax. But the gloves were too small and tore at the seams. Then she tried another pair, and several others as well. But hours passed before she finally got her hands covered with a pair of pea-green kid gloves.

Next she chose a parasol from a large and varied group at the back of the store. She had no idea what it was used for. But other ladies carried such things, so she would have one too.

When she looked at herself carefully in the mirror again, she decided her outfit was now complete. To her untrained eyes, there was no clear difference between her and the women who had stood outside the window. Then she tried to leave the store, but found every door firmly locked.

The wax lady was in no hurry. She had gained patience from her earlier existence. Just to be alive and to wear beautiful clothes was enough pleasure for her at present. So she sat down on a stool and waited quietly until daylight.

When the janitor opened the door in the morning, the wax lady swept past him and walked down the street with stiff but grand steps. The poor man was so completely exhausted at seeing the well-known wax lady leave her window and march away from the store that he fell over in a pile. He only saved himself from fainting by hitting his funny bone against the doorstep. When he recovered his senses, she had turned the corner and disappeared.

The wax lady’s young mind had reasoned that, since she had come to life, her clear duty was to mix with the world and do whatever other people did. She could not understand how different she was from people of flesh and blood. Nor did she know she was the first dummy that had ever lived, or that she owed her special experience to Tanko-Mankie’s love of mischief. So ignorance gave her a confidence in herself that she did not truly deserve.

It was still early in the day, and the few people she met were hurrying along the streets. Many of them turned into restaurants and eating houses. Following their example, the wax lady also entered one and sat on a stool before a lunch counter.

“Coffee ’n’ rolls!” said a shop girl on the next stool.

“Coffee ’n’ rolls!” repeated the dummy, and soon the waiter placed them before her. Of course she had no appetite, as her body was mostly wood and did not need food. But she watched the shop girl and saw her put the coffee to her mouth and drink it. Therefore the wax lady did the same. The next instant she was surprised to feel the hot liquid trickling out between her wooden ribs. The coffee also blistered her wax lips. The experience was so unpleasant that she stood up and left the restaurant. She paid no attention to the waiter’s demands for “20 cents, mum.” Not that she meant to defraud him, but the poor creature had no idea what he meant by “20 cents, mum.”

As she came out, she met the window trimmer at Floman’s store. The man was rather near-sighted. But seeing something familiar in the lady’s features, he politely raised his hat. The wax lady also raised her hat, thinking it the proper thing to do. The man hurried away with a horrified face.

Then a woman touched her arm and said:

“Beg pardon, ma’am; but there’s a price-mark hanging on your dress behind.”

“Yes, I know,” replied the wax lady, stiffly. “It was originally $20, but it’s been reduced to $19.98.”

The woman looked surprised at such indifference and walked on. Some carriages were standing at the edge of the sidewalk. Seeing the dummy hesitate, a driver approached her and touched his cap.

“Cab, ma’am?” he asked.

“No,” she said, misunderstanding him. “I’m wax.”

“Oh!” he exclaimed, and looked after her in wonder.

“Mine, did you say?” she asked.

“Sure! Chronicle, ’Quirer, R’public ’n’ ’Spatch! Wot’ll ye ’ave?”

“It looks all speckled and mixed up,” she said. “I’m afraid I can’t read.”

The boy gave her an angry look.

THE KING OF THE POLAR BEARS

The King of the Polar Bears lived among the icebergs in the far north country. He was old and huge. He was wise and friendly to all who knew him. His body was covered with long, white hair that shone like silver under the rays of the midnight sun. His claws were strong and sharp, that he might walk safely over the smooth ice or grasp and tear the fishes and seals upon which he fed.

The seals were afraid when he drew near, and tried to avoid him. But the gulls, both white and gray, loved him because he left the remains of his feasts for them to eat.

Often his subjects, the polar bears, came to him for advice when ill or in trouble. But they wisely kept away from his hunting grounds, in case they might interfere with his sport and make him angry.

The wolves, who sometimes came as far north as the icebergs, whispered among themselves that the King of the Polar Bears was either a magician or under the protection of a powerful fairy. For no earthly thing seemed able to harm him. He never failed to secure plenty of food, and he grew bigger and stronger day by day and year by year.

Yet the time came when this monarch of the north met man, and his wisdom failed him.

He came out of his cave among the icebergs one day and saw a boat moving through the strip of water which had been uncovered by the shifting of the summer ice. In the boat were men.

The great bear had never seen such creatures before. Therefore he advanced toward the boat, sniffing the strange scent with aroused curiosity. He wondered whether he might take them for friends or foes, food or carrion.

When the king came near the water's edge a man stood up in the boat and with a strange instrument made a loud "bang!" The polar bear felt a shock. His brain became numb. His thoughts deserted him. His great limbs shook and gave way beneath him and his body fell heavily upon the hard ice.

That was all he remembered for a time.

When he awoke he was smarting with pain on every inch of his huge bulk. For the men had cut away his hide with its glorious white hair and carried it with them to a distant ship.

Above him circled thousands of his friends the gulls. They wondered if their benefactor were really dead and it was proper to eat him. But when they saw him raise his head and groan and tremble they knew he still lived. One of them said to his comrades:

"The wolves were right. The king is a great magician, for even men cannot kill him. But he suffers for lack of covering. Let us repay his kindness to us by each giving him as many feathers as we can spare."

This idea pleased the gulls. One by one they pulled with their beaks the softest feathers from under their wings. Then they flew down and dropped them gently upon the body of the King of the Polar Bears.

Then they called to him together:

“Be brave, friend! Our feathers are as soft and beautiful as your own rough hair. They will keep you from the cold winds and warm you while you sleep. Be brave, then, and live!”

And the King of the Polar Bears was brave enough to bear his pain. He lived and was strong again.

The feathers grew as they had grown upon the bodies of the birds. They covered him as his own hair had done. Mostly they were pure white in color. But some from the gray gulls gave him a slightly spotted look.

For the rest of that summer and all through the six months of night, the king left his ice cave only to fish or catch seals for food. He felt no shame at his covering of feathers. But it was still strange to him, and he avoided meeting any of his brother bears.

During this time alone he thought much of the men who had harmed him. He remembered the way they had made the great “bang!” And he decided it was best to keep away from such fierce creatures. So he added to his wisdom.

When the moon fell away from the sky and the sun came to make the icebergs shine with the beautiful colors of the rainbow, two of the polar bears arrived at the king’s cave. They came to ask his advice about the hunting season. But when they saw his great body covered with feathers instead of hair, they began to laugh. One said:

“Our mighty king has become a bird! Who ever before heard of a polar bear with feathers?”

Then the king became angry. He moved toward them with deep growls and a proud step. With one blow of his huge paw he stretched the laughing bear dead at his feet.

The other ran away to his fellows and carried the news of the king’s strange appearance. The result was a meeting of all the polar bears upon a broad field of ice. There they talked seriously of the remarkable change that had come upon their king.

“He is, in reality, no longer a bear,” said one. “Nor can he justly be called a bird. But he is half bird and half bear, and so unfit to remain our king.”

“Then who shall take his place?” asked another.

“He who can fight the bird-bear and overcome him,” answered an aged member of the group. “Only the strongest is fit to rule our race.”

There was silence for a time. At length a great bear moved to the front and said:

“I will fight him; I—Woof—the strongest of our race! And I will be King of the Polar Bears.”

The others nodded in agreement. They sent a messenger to the king to say he must fight the great Woof and master him or give up his rule.

“For a bear with feathers,” added the messenger, “is no bear at all. The king we obey must look like the rest of us.”

“I wear feathers because it pleases me,” growled the king. “Am I not a great magician? But I will fight, nevertheless. And if Woof masters me he shall be king in my place.”

Then he visited his friends, the gulls. They were even then eating the dead bear, and he told them of the coming battle.

“I shall conquer,” he said, proudly. “Yet my people are in the right. Only a hairy one like themselves can hope to command their obedience.”

The queen gull said:

“I met an eagle yesterday. It had escaped from a big city of men. And the eagle told me he had seen a huge polar bear skin thrown over the back of a carriage that rolled along the street. That skin must have been yours, oh king. If you wish, I will send a hundred of my gulls to the city to bring it back to you.”

“Let them go!” said the king, gruffly. And the hundred gulls were soon flying rapidly southward.

For three days they flew straight as an arrow, until they came to scattered houses, to villages, and to cities. Then their search began.

The gulls were brave, and clever, and wise. Upon the fourth day they reached the great city. They hovered over the streets until a carriage rolled along with a great white bear robe thrown over the back seat. Then the birds swooped down—the whole hundred of them—and seized the skin in their beaks. They flew quickly away.

They were late. The king’s great battle was upon the seventh day, and they must fly swiftly to reach the Polar regions by that time.

Meanwhile the bird-bear was preparing for his fight. He sharpened his claws in the small cracks of the ice. He caught a seal and tested his big yellow teeth by crushing its bones between them. And the queen gull set her band to smoothing the king bear’s feathers until they lay smoothly upon his body.

But every day they cast anxious looks into the southern sky, watching for the hundred gulls to bring back the king’s own skin.

The seventh day came, and all the Polar bears in that region gathered around the king’s cave. Among them was Woof, strong and sure of his success.

“The bird-bear’s feathers will fly fast enough when I get my claws upon him!” he boasted. And the others laughed and encouraged him.

The king was disappointed at not having recovered his skin. But he resolved to fight bravely without it. He came out from the opening of his cave with a proud and kingly bearing. When he faced his enemy he gave so terrible a growl that Woof’s heart stopped beating for a moment. He began to realize that a fight with the wise and mighty king of his race was no laughing matter.

After exchanging one or two heavy blows with his foe, Woof’s courage returned. He determined to dishearten his adversary by bluster.

“Come nearer, bird-bear!” he cried. “Come nearer, that I may pull your plumage!”

The defiance filled the king with rage. He ruffled his feathers as a bird does, till he appeared to be twice his actual size. Then he strode forward and struck Woof so powerful a blow that his skull cracked like an egg-shell and he fell flat upon the ground.

While the assembled bears stood looking with fear and wonder at their fallen champion, the sky became darkened.

A hundred gulls flew down from above and dropped upon the king’s body a skin covered with pure white hair that shone in the sun like silver.

And behold! the bears saw before them the well-known form of their wise and respected master. With one accord they bowed their rough heads in respect to the mighty King of the Polar Bears.

This story teaches us that true dignity and courage do not depend on how we look on the outside. They come from within. It also teaches us that bragging and loud talk are poor weapons to carry into battle.

THE MANDARIN AND THE BUTTERFLY

A mandarin once lived in Kiang-ho. He was so very cross and unpleasant that everyone hated him. He spoke angrily at every person he met. No one ever knew him to laugh or be happy in any situation. He especially hated boys and girls. The boys laughed at him, and this made him angry. The girls made fun of him, and this hurt his pride.

He became so unpopular that no one would speak to him. The emperor heard about this and ordered him to move to America. This suited the mandarin very well. But before he left China, he stole the Great Book of Magic that belonged to the wise magician Haot-sai. Then he gathered his small amount of money and took a ship to America.

He settled in a city in the middle west. Of course he started a laundry, since that seems to be the natural job of every Chinaman, whether he is a worker or a mandarin.

He made no friends with the other Chinamen in the town. When they met him and saw the red button on his hat, they knew he was a real mandarin and bowed low before him. He put up a red and white sign. People brought their laundry to him and got paper checks with Chinese characters on them in exchange. This was the only kind of character the mandarin had left.

One day the ugly one was ironing in his shop in the basement of 263 1/2 Main street. He looked up and saw a crowd of young faces pressed against the window. Most Chinamen make friends with children. This one hated them and tried to drive them away. But as soon as he returned to his work, they were back at the window again, smiling at him in a playful, bad way.

The bad mandarin said terrible words in the Manchu language and made fierce movements with his hands. But this did no good at all. The children stayed as long as they pleased. They came again the very next day as soon as school was over. They came the next day too, and the next. For they saw that their presence at the window bothered the Chinaman, and they were delighted by this.

The following day was Sunday, so the children did not appear. But the mandarin, being a heathen, worked in his little shop. A big butterfly flew in at the open door and moved its wings about the room.

The mandarin closed the door and chased the butterfly until he caught it. He pinned it against the wall by sticking two pins through its beautiful wings. This did not hurt the butterfly, because there is no feeling in its wings. But it made him a safe prisoner.

This butterfly was large. Its wings were beautifully marked with gorgeous colors laid out in regular designs, like the colored glass windows of a cathedral.

The mandarin now opened his wooden chest and took out the Great Book of Magic he had stolen from Haot-sai. He turned the pages slowly and came to a passage describing "How to understand the language of butterflies." He read this carefully. Then he mixed a magic formula in a tin cup and drank it down with an unhappy face. Right after that he spoke to the butterfly in its own language, saying:

"Why did you enter this room?"

"I smelled bees-wax," answered the butterfly. "So I thought I might find honey here."

"But you are my prisoner," said the mandarin. "If I please, I can kill you. Or I can leave you on the wall to die of hunger."

"I expect that," replied the butterfly, with a sigh. "But my race does not live long anyway. It does not matter whether death comes sooner or later."

"Yet you like to live, do you not?" asked the mandarin.

"Yes. Life is pleasant and the world is beautiful. I do not seek death."

"Then," said the mandarin, "I will give you life—a long and pleasant life—if you will promise to obey me for a time and carry out my instructions."

"How can a butterfly serve a man?" asked the creature, in surprise.

"Usually they cannot," was the reply. "But I have a book of magic which teaches me strange things. Do you promise?"

"Oh, yes. I promise," answered the butterfly. "For even as your slave I will get some enjoyment out of life. But if you kill me, that is the end of everything!"

"Truly," said the mandarin, "butterflies have no souls. So they cannot live again."

"But I have enjoyed three lives already," returned the butterfly, with some pride. "I was a caterpillar and a chrysalis before I became a butterfly. You were never anything but a Chinaman, although I admit your life is longer than mine."

"I will extend your life for many days, if you will obey me," declared the Chinaman. "I can easily do so by means of my magic."

"Of course I will obey you," said the butterfly, carelessly.

"Then listen! You know children, do you not? Boys and girls?"

"Yes, I know them. They chase me and try to catch me, as you have done," replied the butterfly.

"And they make fun of me and laugh at me through the window," continued the mandarin, bitterly. "So they are your enemies and mine! But with your aid and the help of the magic book, we shall have a fine revenge for their insults."

"I do not care much for revenge," said the butterfly. "They are only children. It is natural that they should wish to catch such a beautiful creature as I am."

"Nevertheless, I care! And you must obey me," retorted the mandarin, harshly. "I, at least, will have my revenge."

Then he stuck a drop of molasses on the wall beside the butterfly's head and said:

"Eat that, while I read my book and prepare my magic formula."

So the butterfly ate the molasses, and the mandarin studied his book. After that he began to mix a magic compound in the tin cup.

When the mixture was ready, he released the butterfly from the wall and said to it:

"I command you to dip your two front feet into this magic compound. Then fly away until you meet a child. Fly close, whether it is a boy or a girl, and touch the child on its forehead with your feet. The book says that whoever is touched in this way will at once become a pig and will stay that way forever after. Then return to me and dip your legs again in the contents of this cup. So all my enemies, the children, will become miserable pigs, and no one will think of accusing me of sorcery."

“Very well. Since that is your command, I obey,” said the butterfly. Then it put its front legs, which were the shortest of the six, into the liquid in the tin cup. It flew out of the door and away over the houses to the edge of the town. There it landed in a flower garden. Soon it forgot all about its task to turn children into pigs.

Going from flower to flower, it soon rubbed the magic liquid off its legs. So when the sun began to set and the butterfly finally remembered its master, the mandarin, it could not have hurt a child even if it had tried.

But it did not plan to try.

“That awful old Chinaman,” it thought, “hates children and wants to destroy them. But I quite like children myself and will not harm them. Of course I must return to my master, because he is a magician. He would find me and kill me. But I can easily trick him about this matter.”

When the butterfly flew in at the door of the mandarin’s laundry, he asked with great interest:

“Well, did you meet a child?”

“I did,” replied the butterfly in a calm voice. “It was a pretty girl with golden hair—but now it is a grunting pig!”

“Good! Good! Good!” cried the mandarin, dancing with joy around the room. “You shall have molasses for your supper. Tomorrow you must change two children into pigs.”

The butterfly did not reply. It ate the molasses in silence. It had no soul, so it had no conscience. And having no conscience, it was able to lie to the mandarin very easily and with some pleasure.

Next morning, by the mandarin’s command, the butterfly put its legs in the mixture and flew away to look for children.

When it came to the edge of the town, it noticed a pig in a sty. It landed on the rail of the sty and looked down at the creature and thought.

“If I could change a child into a pig by touching it with the magic liquid, what could I change a pig into, I wonder?”

The butterfly was curious to settle this fine point in magic. It flew down and touched its front feet to the pig’s nose. At once the animal disappeared. In its place was a boy with messy hair and a dirty look. He jumped out of the sty and ran down the road, shouting loudly.

“That’s funny,” said the butterfly to itself. “The mandarin would be very angry with me if he knew about this. I have set free one more of the creatures that bother him.”

It flew along after the boy, who had stopped to throw stones at a cat. But the cat escaped by running up a tree, where thick branches protected her from the stones. Then the boy found a newly planted garden. He stepped all over the beds until the seeds were scattered far and wide, and the garden was ruined. Next he picked up a thin stick and hit a young calf that stood quietly eating grass in a field. The poor creature ran away with sad cries. The boy laughed and followed after it, hitting the frightened animal again and again.

“Really,” thought the butterfly, “I do not wonder the mandarin hates children, if they are all as cruel and bad as this one.”

The calf having escaped him, the boy came back to the road. There he met two little girls on their way to school. One of them had a red apple in her hand. The boy grabbed it away and began eating it. The little girl began to cry, but her companion, braver and stronger, cried out:

“You ought to be ashamed of yourself, you nasty boy!”

At this the boy reached out and slapped her pretty face. Then she also began to cry.

Although it had neither soul nor conscience, the butterfly had a very kind heart. It now decided it could not bear this boy any longer.

“If I let him live,” it thought, “I should never forgive myself. The monster would do nothing but evil from morning to night.”

So it flew straight into his face and touched his forehead with its sticky front feet.

The next moment the boy had disappeared. But a grunting pig ran quickly up the road toward its sty.

The butterfly gave a sigh of relief.

“This time I have really used the mandarin’s magic on a child,” it whispered, as it floated slowly on the light breeze. “But since the child was first a pig, I do not think I have any reason to blame myself. The little girls were sweet and gentle, and I would not hurt them to save my life. But if all boys were like this transformed pig, I should not wait to carry out the mandarin’s orders.”

Then it flew into a rose bush, where it stayed comfortably until evening. At sundown it returned to its master.

“Have you changed two of them into pigs?” he asked at once.

“I have,” replied the butterfly. “One was a pretty baby with black eyes. The other was a new boy with freckles, red hair, and no shoes.”

“Good! Good! Good!” screamed the mandarin, wild with delight. “Those are the ones who torment me the most! Change every new boy you meet into a pig!”

“Very well,” answered the butterfly quietly, and ate its supper of molasses.

Several days passed in the same way. The butterfly flew without purpose about the flower gardens while the sun shone. At night it returned to the mandarin with false stories of turning children into pigs. Sometimes it was one child who was transformed, sometimes two, and occasionally three. But the mandarin always greeted the butterfly’s report with great delight and gave him molasses for supper.

One evening, however, the butterfly thought it might be good to change the report, so that the mandarin would not become suspicious. When its master asked what child had been changed into a pig that day, the lying creature answered:

“It was a Chinese boy, and when I touched him he became a black pig.”

This angered the mandarin, who was in an especially bad mood. He angrily hit the butterfly with his finger and nearly broke its beautiful wing. He forgot that Chinese boys had once laughed at him. He only remembered his hatred for American boys.

The butterfly became very angry at this abuse from the mandarin. It refused to eat its molasses and was silent and angry all the evening. It had grown to hate the mandarin almost as much as the mandarin hated children.

When morning came it was still shaking with anger. But the mandarin cried out:

“Hurry, miserable slave! Today you must change four children into pigs, to make up for yesterday.”

The butterfly did not reply. His little black eyes were shining with anger. As soon as he had put his feet into the magic liquid, he flew straight into the mandarin’s face and touched him on his ugly, flat forehead.

Soon after, a gentleman came into the room for his laundry. The mandarin was not there. But running around the place was a very ugly, thin pig. It made loud, sad cries.

The butterfly flew away to a small stream. It washed from its feet all signs of the magic liquid. When night came, it slept in a rose bush.
